When a UK company faces liquidation, its directors navigate a complex landscape of legal obligations and potential personal liabilities. Missteps can lead to severe consequences, including personal financial ruin, disqualification from acting as a director, and even criminal charges. Understanding the main risks and pitfalls is crucial for directors in this challenging situation.

Here are the key risks, pitfalls, and common mistakes UK company directors often make or regret when dealing with liquidation:

### 1. Wrongful Trading and Personal Liability

**What it is:** Wrongful trading occurs when a company continues to trade and incur debts at a time when its directors knew, or ought to have known, that there was no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvent liquidation.

**The Pitfall:** Directors often hold onto the hope of a turnaround for too long, continuing to trade and accrue further debt even when the company is clearly insolvent. This can exacerbate losses for creditors.

**Consequences:** Under Section 214 of the Insolvency Act 1986, the court can declare that a director is personally liable to contribute to the company's assets to the extent of the loss caused to creditors by the wrongful trading. This liability can extend to shadow directors and de facto directors, not just formally appointed ones. A finding of wrongful trading can also lead to director disqualification.

**Rule/Source:** Section 214, Insolvency Act 1986.

### 2. Preferring Some Creditors Over Others (Preferences)

**What it is:** A preference payment occurs when an insolvent company pays one or more creditors, or a guarantor for its debts, in a way that puts them in a better position than they would have been in the event of the company going into insolvent liquidation.

**The Pitfall:** Directors, often out of loyalty, personal connection (e.g., family or friends), or to reduce personal guarantees, may prioritise paying certain creditors (e.g., a bank where they have a personal guarantee, or a key supplier) over others when the company is in financial distress.

**Consequences:** If a company gives a preference at a "relevant time" before liquidation, the liquidator can apply to the court to have the transaction set aside, and the funds recovered for the benefit of all creditors. For unconnected creditors, the "relevant time" is usually six months before liquidation, but for "connected persons" (like directors or their relatives), it extends to two years. If a director is found to have a "desire to prefer" a specific creditor, they could face personal liability.

**Rule/Source:** Section 239, Insolvency Act 1986.

### 3. Overdrawn Director's Loan Account Being Called In

**What it is:** A director's loan account (DLA) records money taken from or paid into the company by a director, outside of salary, dividends, or reimbursed expenses. If a director has taken more out than they have put in, the DLA is overdrawn, meaning the director owes money to the company.

**The Pitfall:** Many directors, particularly in small businesses, treat the company's funds as their own, making informal withdrawals without formalising them as salary or dividends. They often don't realise the personal liability this creates until the company faces insolvency.

**Consequences:** In liquidation, an overdrawn DLA is considered a company asset. The liquidator has a statutory duty to pursue the repayment of this debt from the director for the benefit of creditors. If the director cannot repay, it can lead to personal insolvency (e.g., IVA or bankruptcy). Attempts to retrospectively reclassify drawings as salary or dividends after insolvency are usually scrutinised and often rejected by courts.

**Rule/Source:** General company law principles regarding company assets and director duties; liquidators' duties under the Insolvency Act 1986.

### 4. Personal Guarantees Crystallising

**What it is:** A personal guarantee (PG) is a legally binding commitment by a company director to repay a company's debt if the company itself cannot. These are commonly requested by lenders, landlords, and suppliers, especially for smaller or newer businesses.

**The Pitfall:** Directors often provide personal guarantees to secure finance, sometimes underestimating the personal risk involved, believing the company will always be able to repay. They may fail to understand that PGs bypass the protection of limited liability.

**Consequences:** When a company enters liquidation, any outstanding personal guarantees "crystallise", meaning the responsibility for repayment transfers directly to the individual who signed the guarantee. The creditor can then pursue the guarantor personally for the outstanding balance, including interest and fees. This can lead to legal action, charging orders over property (including the family home), and even personal bankruptcy if the director cannot repay.

**Rule/Source:** Contract law (the terms of the personal guarantee agreement itself).

### 5. Misuse of Dissolution / Strike-Off to Avoid Creditors

**What it is:** Dissolution or strike-off is a process to remove a company from the Companies House register. It's intended for genuinely dormant companies with no liabilities.

**The Pitfall:** Directors may attempt to use voluntary strike-off as a quick and cheap alternative to formal liquidation, hoping to avoid dealing with outstanding debts and creditors.

**Consequences:** Attempting to strike off a company with outstanding debts is an improper use of the process. Directors have a legal responsibility to notify all creditors of a strike-off application. Creditors can object to the strike-off, which will halt the process. If a company is improperly struck off, creditors can apply to restore it to the register, even up to 20 years later. Upon restoration, the directors may face investigation by Companies House or the Insolvency Service for their conduct, potentially leading to director disqualification, fines, or personal liability for company debts. The Rating (Coronavirus) and Directors Disqualification (Dissolved Companies) Act 2021 specifically extended the Insolvency Service's powers to investigate directors of dissolved companies, even retrospectively.

**Rule/Source:** Sections 1003-1011 and 1032(6) of the Companies Act 2006; Rating (Coronavirus) and Directors Disqualification (Dissolved Companies) Act 2021.

### 6. Phoenix Company Restrictions on Reusing a Company Name (s.216 Insolvency Act 1986)

**What it is:** A "phoenix company" describes a situation where the business of an insolvent company is transferred to a new company, often with the same directors, that rises "from the ashes" of the old one. While legitimate if done correctly, strict rules apply to prevent abuse.

**The Pitfall:** Directors may attempt to set up a new company with the same or a very similar name to the liquidated company, without following the specific legal procedures. They might mistakenly believe they can simply continue trading under a new entity without addressing the debts of the old one.

**Consequences:** Section 216 of the Insolvency Act 1986 prohibits a director (or shadow director) of a company that has gone into insolvent liquidation from being involved in another company trading under the same or a similar name for five years. Breaching this restriction is a criminal offence, punishable by imprisonment and/or a fine. Furthermore, under Section 217 of the Insolvency Act 1986, directors who breach Section 216 can be made personally liable for all debts of the new company incurred while using the prohibited name. There are limited statutory exceptions, which typically involve giving notice to creditors or court approval.

**Rule/Source:** Sections 216 and 217, Insolvency Act 1986.

### 7. Director Disqualification

**What it is:** Directors can be disqualified (banned) from acting as a company director for a period if found to have engaged in unfit conduct.

**The Pitfall:** Directors may not realise the seriousness of their duties, particularly when a company is in financial distress. Actions such as wrongful trading, preferential payments, failing to keep proper records, or misusing company funds can all lead to disqualification.

**Consequences:** Under the Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986, a court can make a disqualification order for a period ranging from 2 to 15 years. This prevents an individual from being a director of a company, acting as a receiver, or being involved in the promotion, formation, or management of a company. The Insolvency Service will investigate directors' conduct, especially in insolvent liquidations, and can bring proceedings if unfit conduct is identified. A disqualification order can severely impact a director's reputation and future business career.

**Rule/Source:** Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986.

### 8. HMRC's Preferential Status and Personal Liability Notices

**HMRC's Preferential Status:**

**What it is:** Since 1 December 2020, HMRC regained its status as a secondary preferential creditor for certain taxes in UK insolvency proceedings.

**The Pitfall:** Directors may not be aware of this change, potentially making decisions about creditor payments based on outdated information. They might assume all unsecured creditors are treated equally.

**Consequences:** HMRC now ranks ahead of floating charge holders and unsecured creditors for taxes collected by the company on behalf of others, such as PAYE income tax, employee National Insurance contributions, VAT, student loan repayments, and Construction Industry Scheme (CIS) deductions. This means less money is available for other creditors, including those with floating charges (like banks) where directors might have given personal guarantees, increasing the likelihood of those guarantees being called upon.

**Rule/Source:** Finance Act 2020 (amending the Insolvency Act 1986).

**Personal Liability Notices (PLNs):**

**What it is:** HMRC can issue a Personal Liability Notice (PLN) to make a director personally liable for certain unpaid company tax debts if the failure to pay was due to the director's fraud or neglect.

**The Pitfall:** Directors may believe that limited liability always protects them from company debts, or they may ignore HMRC correspondence and warnings while the company is struggling.

**Consequences:** PLNs can be issued for unpaid PAYE and National Insurance contributions, VAT penalties, and other deliberate inaccuracies or failures to notify HMRC. If a PLN is issued, it transfers the company's tax liability directly to the director, bypassing the corporate veil. This is a serious demand for payment and can lead to court proceedings and personal bankruptcy if unpaid. HMRC increasingly uses PLNs as an enforcement mechanism.

**Rule/Source:** Social Security Administration Act 1992 (e.g., section 121C for NICs), and other tax legislation.

In conclusion, UK company directors facing liquidation must be acutely aware of their enhanced responsibilities and potential personal liabilities. Seeking professional advice from a licensed insolvency practitioner at the earliest signs of financial difficulty is paramount to navigating these complex rules and minimising adverse consequences.
