As a UK company director facing financial difficulties, understanding your options is crucial. A Company Voluntary Arrangement (CVA) is a formal insolvency procedure designed to help a viable but struggling company restructure its debts and continue trading under the existing management. It is a legally binding agreement between your company and its unsecured creditors, overseen by a licensed insolvency practitioner (IP).

### What is a Company Voluntary Arrangement (CVA) and What Distinguishes It?

A CVA is a flexible debt restructuring tool under Part 1 of the Insolvency Act 1986. Unlike liquidation, which leads to the dissolution of the company, a CVA aims for business rescue and allows the company to continue operating. A key distinguishing feature is that directors retain control of the company, with the IP acting as a supervisor, rather than taking over management as in administration.

A CVA allows your company to agree a composition or arrangement with its creditors to satisfy some, or all, of its debts over a fixed period. It can provide a "breathing space" from creditor enforcement action once approved. While there's no statutory requirement for the company to be insolvent to propose a CVA, it's typically used when there's at least a risk of insolvency.

### The CVA Process and Its Order

The CVA process typically follows these steps:
1.  **Initial Assessment and Appointment of Nominee:** You, as director(s), must seek advice from a licensed insolvency practitioner to determine if a CVA is appropriate. The board must support the decision to proceed. The IP will act as a 'nominee' to assess the company's financial position and the viability of continued operations.
2.  **Drafting the Proposal and Statement of Affairs:** The directors, in conjunction with the nominee, prepare a formal proposal outlining how debts will be restructured and repaid, including a payment schedule. A statement of affairs detailing assets, liabilities, and creditors is also prepared. The proposal must demonstrate that the CVA offers a better return for creditors than liquidation.
3.  **Nominee's Report to Court:** The nominee must provide a statement to the court confirming that, in their opinion, the CVA proposal has a "reasonable prospect of being approved and implemented successfully".
4.  **Circulation to Creditors and Shareholders:** The proposal is sent to all known creditors and shareholders, who are given time to review and raise queries. Creditors are typically given at least 14 days' notice (HMRC often prefers 3 weeks) before the decision date.
5.  **Creditor and Shareholder Voting:** A decision procedure (which can be a physical meeting, by correspondence, electronic voting, or a virtual meeting) is held where creditors vote on the proposal. For the CVA to be approved, at least 75% (by value) of creditors who vote must agree to the proposal. Crucially, no more than 50% (by value) of *unconnected* creditors may vote against it. Shareholders also vote (by simple majority), and if their decision differs from the creditors', the creditors' decision prevails, subject to any court order.
6.  **Implementation and Supervision:** If approved, the CVA becomes legally binding on all unsecured creditors (even those who voted against it or didn't vote). The nominee then becomes the 'supervisor', monitoring the company's compliance with the CVA terms and distributing payments to creditors. The CVA is registered at Companies House.

### Who Can Start It?

A CVA is most commonly proposed by the company's directors. It can also be proposed by an administrator (if the company is in administration) or, more rarely, by a liquidator (if the company is in liquidation).

### Eligibility/Criteria

To be eligible for a CVA, a company must be insolvent or likely to become insolvent. The business needs to be fundamentally sound with potential for future profitability and have a reliable income stream to fund the repayments. Directors must be committed to the rescue and prepared for necessary changes.

Small companies may be able to obtain an optional moratorium period, providing immediate legal protection from creditor action while the CVA proposal is being prepared and considered. Eligibility for a moratorium depends on certain criteria, such as having no more than 50 employees, balance sheet assets no greater than £5.1 million, and turnover no greater than £10.2 million.

### Typical Cost and Who Pays

The costs associated with a CVA are primarily the fees of the insolvency practitioner (IP), who acts as both nominee and supervisor. These fees are usually paid from the company's cash flow as part of the arrangement, meaning directors generally do not need to fund them personally.

*   **Nominee's Fee:** This covers the preparation of the proposal, creditor consultation, and initial setup. It typically ranges from £2,500 to £8,000.
*   **Supervisor's Fee:** This is an ongoing fee for monitoring the CVA over its duration, usually charged as a percentage (10-15%) of the funds distributed to creditors or a fixed annual charge.

Overall, the total cost of a CVA can vary significantly based on the size and complexity of the business, typically ranging from £20,000 to £50,000. It is generally less expensive than administration.

### Realistic Timelines

The preparation and distribution of the CVA proposal can take several weeks, often around four weeks, depending on the complexity of the business. Creditors are then given a minimum of 14 days (with HMRC often preferring three weeks) to consider the proposal before voting. The entire process, from initial assessment to approval, usually takes between six to eight weeks.

Once approved, the CVA typically runs for a period of three to five years, during which the company makes the agreed repayments.

### Director's Personal Exposure

A significant advantage of a CVA is that directors generally retain control of the company and there is no automatic investigation into director conduct, unlike in a Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation (CVL). However, directors still have statutory duties under the Companies Act 2006 and the Insolvency Act 1986.

*   **Personal Guarantees:** Any personal guarantees you have provided for company debts will generally *survive* the CVA and you could still be held personally liable for these. The CVA itself does not automatically release you from personal guarantees unless the creditors explicitly agree to this within the CVA terms, which is rare.
*   **Overdrawn Director's Loan Account (DLA):** If your director's loan account is overdrawn, HMRC and other creditors will expect this to be repaid into the CVA 'pot' over the arrangement's term. Repaying these funds demonstrates commitment and fairness to creditors.
*   **Wrongful or Fraudulent Trading:** While a CVA is a rescue mechanism, if directors continue to trade knowing the company has no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvency (wrongful trading), or if they engage in fraudulent trading, they can face personal liability and disqualification. Seeking advice promptly can mitigate these risks.
*   **Misfeasance and Disqualification:** The IP acting as supervisor has a duty to monitor the company's adherence to the CVA. If the CVA fails and the company subsequently enters liquidation, a full investigation into director conduct will typically occur, which could lead to claims of misfeasance or director disqualification for up to 15 years if misconduct is found.

### How Creditors and HMRC are Treated

*   **Unsecured Creditors:** Once approved, a CVA is legally binding on all unsecured creditors who were entitled to vote, even if they voted against the proposal or did not receive notice (provided they were entitled to be notified). This means they cannot take further enforcement action for included debts while the CVA is being adhered to.
*   **Secured Creditors:** A CVA cannot bind secured creditors (e.g., banks with charges over assets) without their express consent. They generally retain their rights over any assets used as collateral.
*   **Preferential Creditors (including HMRC):** Since December 2020, HMRC regained its status as a secondary preferential creditor for certain tax debts, specifically VAT and PAYE. This means HMRC ranks above floating charge holders and unsecured creditors for these specific debts. HMRC is generally willing to consider and accept a CVA proposal, provided it is reasonable, offers a better outcome than liquidation, demonstrates a clear plan for future tax compliance, and treats all creditors within the same class equally. HMRC has a dedicated Voluntary Arrangement Service (VAS) that scrutinises proposals to ensure they are "fit, fair, and a better alternative to liquidation". Non-payment of new PAYE and VAT liabilities during the drafting period can lead to HMRC rejecting the proposal.

### Consequences Afterwards

*   **Successful Completion:** If the CVA is successfully completed, the company will have repaid its unsecured debts according to the agreed terms. Any remaining unsecured debt included in the arrangement is written off, and the company receives a completion certificate from the supervisor. The company then emerges financially and operationally stronger.
*   **Failure of CVA:** If the company fails to meet the agreed payment schedule or other terms of the CVA, the supervisor can terminate the arrangement. This typically leads to other formal insolvency procedures, most commonly liquidation or administration. If the CVA fails and leads to liquidation, a director conduct investigation will be triggered.

It is vital to engage with a licensed insolvency practitioner early to explore whether a CVA is the most appropriate solution for your company's specific circumstances. They can guide you through the complexities of the Insolvency Act 1986 and the Insolvency (England and Wales) Rules 2016, and help you navigate the process with official bodies like The Insolvency Service and Companies House.
