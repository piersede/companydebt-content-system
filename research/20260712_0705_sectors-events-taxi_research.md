### Early Warning Signs of Insolvency

### Insolvency and Rescue Options (and Sector Complexities)
When an events company becomes insolvent (unable to pay debts as they fall due, or liabilities exceed assets), directors have a statutory duty to act in the best interests of their creditors to avoid wrongful trading and personal liability [cite: 2, 19]. Available procedures include Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation (CVL), Administration, and Company Voluntary Arrangements (CVA). 

Sector-specific complexities include:
*   **Treatment of Customer Deposits and Ticket Sales:** In a CVL or Administration, customers who have paid deposits or purchased tickets in advance are legally classed as "unsecured creditors" [cite: 8, 21]. Unsecured creditors are placed near the bottom of the statutory payment hierarchy. Due to the lack of tangible assets in many event agencies, these consumers rarely receive full repayment from asset sales [cite: 2].
*   **Consumer Prepayment Protection:** Unless payments were made via credit card (offering Section 75 protection) or held in an independent escrow account, consumer prepayments are entirely at risk [cite: 2, 21].
*   **Contracts with Venues and Suppliers:** Liquidation usually triggers immediate termination clauses in venue and supplier contracts. Preserving these relationships requires a highly orchestrated Administration (often a pre-pack sale), where a viable part of the business or the brand itself is sold to a new entity [cite: 18].
*   **Restructuring via CVA:** A CVA allows a company to agree to a legally binding repayment plan with creditors over 3 to 5 years, pausing litigation [cite: 20]. However, it requires approval from 75% of creditors (by value). This is only realistic if the events agency can prove long-term viability and reliable future cash flows [cite: 20].

### Structural and Financial Drivers of Insolvency (2023-2026)
The UK taxi and private hire vehicle (PHV) sector is undergoing a period of severe financial contraction and structural upheaval. Insolvencies in the transport sector remain high, driven by an accumulation of legislative, taxation, and macroeconomic pressures [cite: 22, 23]. 

The primary drivers of distress between 2023 and 2026 include:
*   **The Closure of the TOMS VAT Loophole:** Following years of litigation (including the *Uber* and *Bolt* cases), the UK Government fundamentally altered the tax landscape for the sector in the Autumn Budget 2024/2025. Effective 2 January 2026, an amendment to section 53 of the Value Added Tax Act 1994 explicitly excluded taxi and PHV operators from utilizing the Tour Operators Margin Scheme (TOMS) [cite: 24, 25, 26, 27]. Previously, operators acting as "principals" attempted to use TOMS to pay VAT only on their profit margin (an effective rate of around 4%). The legislative change forces these operators to account for VAT at the standard rate of 20% on the full passenger fare, drastically compressing margins and altering cash flow dynamics [cite: 26, 27].
*   **Driver Shortages and Acquisition Costs:** The sector is experiencing a stark decline in the supply of drivers. For context, in London, only 104 new taxi licences were issued in 2024, compared to 1,010 in 2016 [cite: 28]. App-based platforms (like Uber and Bolt) have intensified competition, forcing traditional fleets to spend heavily on driver retention and acquisition.
*   **Capital Costs of the Green Transition:** The shift toward electric and ULEZ-compliant vehicles has placed a massive capital burden on fleet operators. Government support has simultaneously evaporated; the TfL scrappage scheme ended in 2022, and the Plug-in Taxi Grant (which offered up to £7,500) was steadily reduced and entirely scrapped by 2026 [cite: 28].
*   **Operational Overheads:** Operators cite intense cash flow pressure stemming from rising fuel costs, soaring fleet insurance premiums, and local-authority licensing costs [cite: 22, 23].

### Early Warning Signs of Insolvency
Directors of taxi and PHV firms should be highly vigilant of the following early warning indicators that suggest a trajectory toward insolvency:

*   **HMRC Arrears (VAT and PAYE):** The inability to meet statutory tax obligations is a critical red flag. With the 2026 TOMS legislative changes resolving previous VAT treatment uncertainty (forcing standard 20% VAT), operators can no longer use legal ambiguity to delay assessments, meaning enforcement via winding-up petitions (as seen with Carriage Company (Oxon) Limited) is an imminent threat [cite: 22, 26, 27].
*   **Missed Vehicle Finance or Lease Payments:** Cash flow strains often manifest in defaulted payments to third-party vehicle finance providers.
*   **Falling Driver Numbers & Rising Empty-Vehicle Costs:** A shrinking pool of active drivers leads directly to vehicles sitting idle in depots (empty-vehicle costs), generating no revenue while fixed finance, insurance, and depreciation costs remain constant [cite: 28].
*   **Cash Flow Strain from Insurance Renewals:** An inability to fund block fleet insurance policies without relying on high-interest short-term credit.

### Insolvency and Rescue Options (and Sector Complexities)
Should a taxi or PHV operator become insolvent, standard corporate rescue tools apply (CVL, Administration, CVA). However, the transport sector presents unique legal and operational complexities:

*   **Treatment of Vehicle Finance/HP/Lease Agreements:** In an administration or liquidation, the fleet itself is rarely an asset of the company. Vehicles under hire purchase (HP) or lease agreements are legally owned by the third-party finance provider until the final payment is cleared [cite: 35, 36]. Therefore, these assets cannot be sold by a liquidator to pay unsecured creditors. If the operator defaults, the finance company is entitled to immediate asset repossession [cite: 35, 36].
*   **Driver Employment Status:** The status of drivers (whether independent contractors, "workers," or employees) remains a complex legal issue following the 2021 Supreme Court ruling against Uber [cite: 37]. In an insolvency scenario, misclassified drivers could mount employment claims against the insolvent estate for holiday pay or minimum wage arrears, heavily diluting returns to other creditors.
*   **Operator Licence Continuity:** Local authority operator licences are strictly regulated and generally cannot be seamlessly transferred to a new entity without rigorous vetting. This makes "pre-pack" administration sales highly complex, as the purchasing company must have its own valid operating licence ready to ensure continuous trading. 
*   **Restructuring vs. Closure:** A Company Voluntary Arrangement (CVA) may be viable if the core business is profitable but suffering from historical debt (e.g., legacy HMRC arrears). However, if the business model is structurally unviable due to the new 20% VAT burden and high EV finance costs, a structured closure via CVL is the most legally compliant route for directors [cite: 19].
