For a UK company director, understanding the costs and responsibilities associated with compulsory liquidation is crucial. Compulsory liquidation, also known as winding-up by the court, is a formal insolvency process primarily governed by the **Insolvency Act 1986**.

### How much does compulsory liquidation cost?

The costs associated with compulsory liquidation can be significant and vary depending on the complexity of the case and the assets involved. These costs typically include:

1.  **Creditor's Petition Costs:** The initial costs are borne by the creditor who petitions the court for the winding-up order. These typically include:
    *   A court fee of **£343**.
    *   An Insolvency Service deposit of **£2,600**.
    *   Their own legal costs, which can range from **£1,500 to £4,000**.
    *   The total initial outlay for the petitioning creditor can therefore be in the range of **£3,500 to £4,000**.

2.  **Official Receiver (OR) and Liquidator Fees:**
    *   Initially, the **Official Receiver (OR)**, an impartial civil servant, is appointed by the court as the interim receiver and manager. The OR charges statutory fees, including an administration fee of **£5,000** and a general fee of **£6,000** for winding-up by the court.
    *   If significant assets exist, a licensed **Insolvency Practitioner (IP)** may be appointed as liquidator in place of the OR. The IP's fees for compulsory liquidation typically range from **£5,000 to £10,000**, or around **£3,000 to £5,000+** once wider costs are factored in. These fees cover professional services, legal documentation, statutory advertising, asset valuation, and administrative tasks.
    *   Overall, the process can cost between **£1,000 and £7,500**, depending on the company's size and complexity.

3.  **Other Expenses:** Additional expenses include costs for asset valuation and auctions (if company property is sold), statutory advertising (e.g., in the London Gazette), and general administrative expenses related to notifying creditors and managing correspondence.

### Who pays for it?

The general principle is that the costs of compulsory liquidation are paid from the **company's assets**.

*   **Company Assets:** The liquidator's fees and the expenses of the winding-up are paid by the company itself from the proceeds of its asset sales, after any secured creditors have been satisfied.
*   **Petitioning Creditor:** The creditor who initiates the compulsory liquidation process must pay the initial court fees and deposit. They present the winding-up petition with the expectation of recovering these costs, along with the outstanding debt, from the company's assets once a winding-up order is made.
*   **Directors' Personal Liability:** Directors are generally protected from personal liability for company debts under the principle of limited liability. However, there are specific circumstances where a director could become personally liable for costs or debts during or after compulsory liquidation:
    *   **Personal Guarantees:** If a director has provided personal guarantees for company loans, leases, or other debts, these remain enforceable even after the company is liquidated.
    *   **Wrongful Trading:** If a director continued to trade and incur debts when they knew, or ought to have known, that the company was insolvent and had no reasonable prospect of avoiding liquidation, they could be held personally liable for the losses incurred by creditors during that period.
    *   **Overdrawn Director's Loan Account:** Any money owed to the company by a director via an overdrawn director's loan account will be considered an asset of the company, and the director will be required to repay it.
    *   **Preference Payments or Unlawful Dividends:** If a director made payments to certain creditors (e.g., themselves, friends, or family) in preference to others, or took excessive dividends when the company was insolvent, the liquidator may seek to recover these funds.
    *   **Personal Liability Notices (PLNs) from HMRC:** HMRC can issue PLNs to directors if they are deemed to have intentionally or recklessly failed to ensure the company paid PAYE, National Insurance, or Construction Industry Scheme deductions.

### Relevant Insolvency Route(s) and Governing Rules

The primary insolvency route in question is **Compulsory Liquidation (Winding-up by the Court)**. This is a court-ordered process, typically initiated by a creditor.

The governing legislation for compulsory liquidation in the UK is the **Insolvency Act 1986**.

Other relevant insolvency routes for insolvent companies include:
*   **Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation (CVL):** This is a director-initiated process where directors voluntarily appoint a licensed IP to wind up an insolvent company. It generally offers more control and can be quicker than compulsory liquidation.
*   **Administration:** A process aimed at rescuing a company as a going concern or achieving a better outcome for creditors than liquidation.

For solvent companies looking to close, a **Members' Voluntary Liquidation (MVL)** is the appropriate route.

### Figures, Thresholds, and Timelines

*   **Minimum Debt Threshold for Petition:** A creditor can generally present a winding-up petition if the company owes an undisputed debt of at least **£750**. (Note: Temporary measures during the COVID-19 pandemic had increased this to £10,000, but these have since expired).
*   **Timeline:** The compulsory liquidation process can be lengthy:
    *   The period from the initial winding-up petition to a winding-up order can take a number of weeks.
    *   The entire compulsory liquidation process typically takes between **12 to 24 months or longer** for complex cases.
    *   Directors lose control of the company once the winding-up order is made.
    *   The company is typically dissolved three months after the liquidator submits the final account to the court and Companies House.

Company directors should be aware that while the company's assets are primarily used to cover liquidation costs, personal guarantees and findings of misconduct can lead to significant personal financial exposure. Seeking early professional advice from a licensed insolvency practitioner is recommended to understand all options and mitigate risks.
