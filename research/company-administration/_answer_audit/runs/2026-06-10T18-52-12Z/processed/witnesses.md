In the UK, company administration is a formal insolvency procedure governed primarily by the Insolvency Act 1986. Its objective is to rescue the company as a going concern, achieve a better result for creditors than in a winding-up, or realise property to make a distribution to secured or preferential creditors.

**Cost of Company Administration**

The cost of company administration is highly variable, depending on the complexity, size, and nature of the company, as well as the assets and liabilities involved and the duration of the administration.

*   **Initial Costs:** Initial administration costs typically range from £10,000 to £15,000.
*   **Pre-administration Fees:** For smaller administrations, pre-administration fees can be between £5,000 and £10,000, which may be higher in pre-pack administrations due to increased preparatory work.
*   **Total Fees:** For complex cases involving business sales, extensive asset realisations, or lengthy administration periods, total fees can easily exceed £20,000.
*   **Fee Basis:** Insolvency Practitioners (IPs) typically charge in one of three ways:
    *   **Time-cost basis:** This is common in more complex cases. Hourly rates for IPs can range from £100 to £300, while staff charge-out rates vary from £115 to £525 per hour, depending on experience and qualifications. Time is usually charged in 6-minute units.
    *   **Fixed fee arrangements:** These may be used for simpler aspects of the administration.
    *   **Percentage of realisations:** In some cases, fees may be calculated as a percentage of funds recovered from asset sales, typically ranging from 3% to 15% of assets realised, though higher percentages may apply for specialist or challenging recoveries.
*   **Other Costs:** Beyond the Administrator's fees, other professional fees and disbursements include agents' valuation fees and legal fees. Legal fees might increase in contested administrations or if a live business is sold, requiring a sale and purchase agreement.

**Governing Rules and Transparency:**
The fee structures for insolvency practitioners must comply with the **Statement of Insolvency Practice 9 (SIP 9)**, which governs the remuneration of insolvency practitioners to ensure transparency and fairness in fee arrangements. SIP 9 mandates that all payments from an estate should be fair, reasonable, and proportionate to the insolvency appointment. Insolvency practitioners are required to provide detailed fee estimates and obtain appropriate approvals before commencing work. Creditors have a right to request further information about fees and expenses and can challenge them if they believe they are excessive.

**Who Pays for Company Administration**

The administrator's fees and other costs of the administration are primarily paid out of the company's assets. These costs are paid before any distributions are made to creditors, including floating charge holders and unsecured creditors. This is why creditors have a direct interest in the level of these costs.

In circumstances where the company lacks sufficient assets to cover the insolvency costs, directors may, in some cases, choose to personally fund basic insolvency procedures. This is done to ensure proper company closure and to avoid potential issues such as director disqualification or personal liability under the Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986. Additionally, one or more creditors might provide funding to the administrator to facilitate the appointment, particularly before assets are realised. Any pre-administration costs, if not fixed with the directors beforehand, may also require approval by creditors.

For a UK company director, company administration, while aiming to rescue the business or achieve a better outcome for creditors than immediate liquidation, carries significant consequences, particularly regarding personal liability and future directorships.

The primary governing rule for company administration and directors' duties during insolvency is the **Insolvency Act 1986 (IA 1986)**, alongside the **Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986 (CDDA 1986)** and the **Companies Act 2006**.

Here are the key consequences and potential personal liabilities:

### 1. Loss of Management Control and Shift in Duties

Upon a company entering administration, directors immediately lose their power to make business decisions and control the company's affairs, assets, and strategic direction. An appointed administrator takes over these responsibilities. While directors may remain on the board, their role shifts to supporting the administrator, and they cannot enter into new contracts or make payments on the company's behalf.

Crucially, once a company becomes insolvent or faces the realistic prospect of insolvency, directors' duties shift from promoting the success of the company for its shareholders to prioritising the interests of the company's creditors.

### 2. Investigation into Conduct

The administrator (or a liquidator, if the company later moves into liquidation) is legally obliged to investigate the conduct of all directors who were in office during the three years leading up to the insolvency. They must submit a confidential report on this conduct to the Insolvency Service within six months of their appointment. This investigation assesses whether directors fulfilled their legal obligations and acted "unfitly" in the lead-up to insolvency.

### 3. Risk of Personal Liability

Despite the principle of limited liability, directors can face personal financial liability in several circumstances:

*   **Personal Guarantees:** Directors are personally liable for any company loans, leases, or supplier accounts for which they have provided a personal guarantee. The administrator or lender will pursue these guarantees.
*   **Overdrawn Director's Loan Account:** If a director owes money to the company through an overdrawn director's loan account, the administrator will require repayment, as this money is an asset of the company for the benefit of creditors.
*   **Wrongful Trading:**
    *   **Governing Rule:** Section 214 of the Insolvency Act 1986 (for liquidation) or Section 246ZB of the Insolvency Act 1986 (for administration).
    *   **Definition:** Wrongful trading occurs if a director continued to trade when they knew, or ought to have known, that there was no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvent liquidation or administration, and they failed to take every step to minimise potential losses to creditors. This does not require intent to defraud.
    *   **Consequence:** A court can order directors to contribute personally to the company's assets for the losses incurred from the point wrongful trading commenced. Directors are jointly and severally liable.
    *   **Timeline:** The court determines the date from which directors realised, or should have realised, that insolvency was unavoidable.
    *   **Qualifier:** Temporary suspension of personal liability for wrongful trading, introduced by the Corporate Insolvency and Governance Act 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic (1 March 2020 to 30 September 2020, and briefly in November 2020), has expired. Directors are now fully exposed to wrongful trading liability outside these periods.
*   **Fraudulent Trading:**
    *   **Governing Rule:** Section 213 of the Insolvency Act 1986 (for liquidation) or Section 246ZA of the Insolvency Act 1986 (for administration), and Section 993 of the Companies Act 2006.
    *   **Definition:** This is a more serious allegation involving carrying on the company's business with the intent to defraud creditors or for any other fraudulent purpose, requiring proof of dishonesty and actual intent.
    *   **Consequence:** Directors can be ordered to contribute personally to the company's debts. It is also a criminal offence, which can lead to imprisonment for up to 10 years, fines, or both.
*   **Misfeasance or Breach of Fiduciary Duty:**
    *   **Governing Rule:** Section 212 of the Insolvency Act 1986 and general directors' duties under the Companies Act 2006.
    *   **Definition:** This includes misapplying company money, acting outside the scope of their powers, failing to promote the company's success (now, creditors' interests), or making transactions at an undervalue or giving preferences to certain creditors.
    *   **Consequence:** Directors can be ordered to restore property or contribute to the company's assets to compensate for losses.
*   **HMRC Personal Liability Notices (PLNs) and Joint and Several Liability Notices (JLNs):**
    *   **Governing Rule:** Section 121C of the Social Security Administration Act 1992 (PLNs) and Finance Act 2020 (JLNs).
    *   **Definition:** HMRC can issue PLNs if a director intentionally or recklessly failed to ensure the company paid its PAYE, National Insurance, or Construction Industry Scheme deductions. JLNs target directors involved in patterns of repeated insolvencies ('phoenixism').
    *   **Consequence:** These notices make the director personally liable for the outstanding tax debts.
*   **Other Statutory Breaches:** Failure to comply with other statutory obligations, such as timely filing of accounts or confirmation statements with Companies House, can also result in personal fines or criminal prosecution.

### 4. Director Disqualification

*   **Governing Rule:** Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986 (CDDA 1986).
*   **Process:** If the Insolvency Service concludes a director's conduct makes them "unfit" to be involved in company management, they may initiate disqualification proceedings. Proceedings generally must be issued within three years of the company entering administration or liquidation.
*   **Period:** Disqualification periods range from **2 to 15 years**, depending on the severity of the misconduct:
    *   **2 to 5 years:** For reckless or negligent conduct.
    *   **6 to 10 years:** For serious misconduct, detrimental to public interest.
    *   **11 to 15 years:** For the most severe breaches, including fraudulent or criminal behaviour.
*   **Consequence of Disqualification:** A disqualified person is prohibited from acting as a director (whether formally appointed, a de facto director, or a shadow director), or being involved in the formation, promotion, or management of any UK-registered company. Breaching a disqualification order is a criminal offence and can lead to imprisonment and/or a fine.

### 5. Reputational Damage

Being associated with a company in administration, particularly if personal liability or disqualification proceedings arise, can significantly damage a director's professional reputation, making it challenging to secure future directorships or other business roles.

For a UK company director, proactive engagement with an insolvency practitioner at the earliest signs of financial difficulty is crucial to understand these risks and minimise potential personal liability.

Company administration is a formal insolvency procedure in the UK designed to rescue financially distressed companies or achieve a better outcome for creditors than liquidation. It is primarily governed by the **Insolvency Act 1986 (Schedule B1)**, with detailed procedures outlined in the **Insolvency Rules 1986**.

### When Company Administration is the Right Option

Company administration is typically the right option for UK company directors in the following scenarios:

*   **Fundamentally Viable Business with Cash-Flow Issues:** When the company is experiencing severe cash-flow pressures and is technically insolvent (unable to pay debts as they fall due or liabilities exceed assets), but the underlying business is still viable and has intrinsic value.
*   **Seeking Rescue and Restructuring:** The primary objective of administration is to rescue the company as a going concern through financial and operational restructuring.
*   **Protecting from Creditor Action:** It provides a statutory "breathing space" or moratorium, freeing the company from most creditor enforcement actions (such as winding-up petitions or legal proceedings) while a rescue plan is formulated. This moratorium comes into effect immediately upon filing a Notice of Intention to Appoint, offering 10 business days of protection, and then continues once the administrator is appointed.
*   **Maximising Creditor Returns:** If rescuing the company as a going concern isn't possible, administration aims to achieve a better result for the company's creditors as a whole than would likely be achieved through a direct liquidation.
*   **Realising Assets for Secured Creditors:** In some cases, if rescue isn't feasible, the objective may be to realise property to make a distribution to one or more secured or preferential creditors.
*   **Creditor Disagreement on CVA:** If creditors are unlikely to agree to a Company Voluntary Arrangement (CVA) or it's not feasible within the required timeframe.
*   **Proactive Action:** Early action when warning signs of insolvency appear (e.g., persistent cash flow problems, increasing arrears with HMRC, threats of legal action) can significantly improve the chances of saving the business.

**Key Timelines and Figures for Administration:**

*   **Moratorium:** An initial automatic moratorium lasts for 10 business days upon filing a Notice of Intention to Appoint, providing protection from most legal actions. This protection continues once the administration officially begins.
*   **Administrator's Proposals:** An appointed administrator (a licensed insolvency practitioner) has **8 weeks** to submit detailed proposals to the company's creditors.
*   **Creditors' Meeting:** An initial decision procedure to approve these proposals must be held within **10 weeks** of the company entering administration, with creditors typically given at least **14 days' notice**.
*   **Duration:** Administration is generally intended to last no longer than **12 months**. However, it can be extended for a further 12 months with the consent of creditors, or indefinitely by the Court, especially for large or complex cases.
*   **Costs:** Administration costs can be high, making it more suitable for larger companies where the potential benefits of rescue or enhanced creditor returns justify the expense.

### Alternatives to Company Administration

If administration is not deemed the most suitable path, UK company directors have several alternative insolvency and restructuring routes:

1.  **Company Voluntary Arrangement (CVA)**:
    *   **Governing Rule:** Part 1 of the Insolvency Act 1986 and Insolvency Rules 1986.
    *   **Description:** A CVA is a legally binding agreement between an insolvent company and its creditors to repay debts over an agreed period, typically **three to five years**. The company continues to trade under the control of its directors.
    *   **Justification:** Suitable for viable businesses facing temporary financial difficulties that can realistically meet reduced or deferred payments. It requires creditor approval.

2.  **Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation (CVL)**:
    *   **Governing Rule:** Part 4 of the Insolvency Act 1986 and Insolvency Rules 1986.
    *   **Description:** This is a director-initiated process to formally wind up an insolvent company. A licensed insolvency practitioner is appointed as liquidator to realise assets and distribute proceeds to creditors.
    *   **Justification:** Appropriate when there is no realistic prospect of rescuing the business, and the directors wish to close the company in an orderly manner to minimise their personal risk. It can also be an exit route from administration if rescue fails.
    *   **Timeline:** Liquidation costs tend to be lower than administration.

3.  **Compulsory Liquidation (Winding-Up by the Court)**:
    *   **Governing Rule:** Part 4 of the Insolvency Act 1986 and Insolvency Rules 1986.
    *   **Description:** This occurs when a creditor (or sometimes directors) applies to the court for a winding-up order, leading to the appointment of an Official Receiver and then potentially a liquidator to cease operations and distribute assets.
    *   **Justification:** Often a last resort, particularly for creditors who have exhausted other recovery options. It is generally a slower process.

4.  **Corporate Insolvency and Governance Act 2020 (CIGA) Moratorium (Standalone Moratorium)**:
    *   **Governing Rule:** Part A1 of the Insolvency Act 1986, introduced by CIGA 2020.
    *   **Description:** This is a standalone "breathing space" procedure for viable companies in financial distress, allowing them to pursue a rescue or restructuring plan without formal insolvency proceedings. A monitor (licensed insolvency practitioner) oversees the company.
    *   **Justification:** Provides an initial **20 business days** of protection from creditor action, extendable with consent, allowing directors to retain control of the business while developing a rescue plan. The company must continue to pay debts falling due during the moratorium, but not those due before.

5.  **Informal Arrangements / Debt Restructuring**:
    *   **Description:** Direct negotiations with individual creditors or groups of creditors to agree on revised payment plans, debt write-offs, or other modifications to existing debt obligations.
    *   **Justification:** A flexible and lower-cost option for companies that might not be able to afford formal insolvency proceedings or where the financial difficulties are less severe. However, these arrangements are not legally binding on all creditors unless formally documented and approved by all parties.

6.  **Members' Voluntary Liquidation (MVL)**:
    *   **Governing Rule:** Insolvency Act 1986 and Companies Act 2006.
    *   **Description:** This is a formal process for closing a *solvent* company (one that can pay all its debts, usually within 12 months). Assets are realised, debts paid, and remaining surplus distributed to shareholders.
    *   **Justification:** Not an alternative to administration for an insolvent company, but relevant for directors looking to close a debt-free or solvent company efficiently, for example, upon retirement or completion of its purpose.

Directors must prioritise creditors' interests once a company is considered insolvent. Seeking early professional advice from a licensed insolvency practitioner is crucial to determine the most appropriate route and avoid potential wrongful trading liability.
