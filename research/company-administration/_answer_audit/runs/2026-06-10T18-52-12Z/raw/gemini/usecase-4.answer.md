Company administration is a formal insolvency procedure in the UK designed to rescue financially distressed companies or achieve a better outcome for creditors than liquidation. It is primarily governed by the **Insolvency Act 1986 (Schedule B1)**, with detailed procedures outlined in the **Insolvency Rules 1986**.

### When Company Administration is the Right Option

Company administration is typically the right option for UK company directors in the following scenarios:

*   **Fundamentally Viable Business with Cash-Flow Issues:** When the company is experiencing severe cash-flow pressures and is technically insolvent (unable to pay debts as they fall due or liabilities exceed assets), but the underlying business is still viable and has intrinsic value.
*   **Seeking Rescue and Restructuring:** The primary objective of administration is to rescue the company as a going concern through financial and operational restructuring.
*   **Protecting from Creditor Action:** It provides a statutory "breathing space" or moratorium, freeing the company from most creditor enforcement actions (such as winding-up petitions or legal proceedings) while a rescue plan is formulated. This moratorium comes into effect immediately upon filing a Notice of Intention to Appoint, offering 10 business days of protection, and then continues once the administrator is appointed.
*   **Maximising Creditor Returns:** If rescuing the company as a going concern isn't possible, administration aims to achieve a better result for the company's creditors as a whole than would likely be achieved through a direct liquidation.
*   **Realising Assets for Secured Creditors:** In some cases, if rescue isn't feasible, the objective may be to realise property to make a distribution to one or more secured or preferential creditors.
*   **Creditor Disagreement on CVA:** If creditors are unlikely to agree to a Company Voluntary Arrangement (CVA) or it's not feasible within the required timeframe.
*   **Proactive Action:** Early action when warning signs of insolvency appear (e.g., persistent cash flow problems, increasing arrears with HMRC, threats of legal action) can significantly improve the chances of saving the business.

**Key Timelines and Figures for Administration:**

*   **Moratorium:** An initial automatic moratorium lasts for 10 business days upon filing a Notice of Intention to Appoint, providing protection from most legal actions. This protection continues once the administration officially begins.
*   **Administrator's Proposals:** An appointed administrator (a licensed insolvency practitioner) has **8 weeks** to submit detailed proposals to the company's creditors.
*   **Creditors' Meeting:** An initial decision procedure to approve these proposals must be held within **10 weeks** of the company entering administration, with creditors typically given at least **14 days' notice**.
*   **Duration:** Administration is generally intended to last no longer than **12 months**. However, it can be extended for a further 12 months with the consent of creditors, or indefinitely by the Court, especially for large or complex cases.
*   **Costs:** Administration costs can be high, making it more suitable for larger companies where the potential benefits of rescue or enhanced creditor returns justify the expense.

### Alternatives to Company Administration

If administration is not deemed the most suitable path, UK company directors have several alternative insolvency and restructuring routes:

1.  **Company Voluntary Arrangement (CVA)**:
    *   **Governing Rule:** Part 1 of the Insolvency Act 1986 and Insolvency Rules 1986.
    *   **Description:** A CVA is a legally binding agreement between an insolvent company and its creditors to repay debts over an agreed period, typically **three to five years**. The company continues to trade under the control of its directors.
    *   **Justification:** Suitable for viable businesses facing temporary financial difficulties that can realistically meet reduced or deferred payments. It requires creditor approval.

2.  **Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation (CVL)**:
    *   **Governing Rule:** Part 4 of the Insolvency Act 1986 and Insolvency Rules 1986.
    *   **Description:** This is a director-initiated process to formally wind up an insolvent company. A licensed insolvency practitioner is appointed as liquidator to realise assets and distribute proceeds to creditors.
    *   **Justification:** Appropriate when there is no realistic prospect of rescuing the business, and the directors wish to close the company in an orderly manner to minimise their personal risk. It can also be an exit route from administration if rescue fails.
    *   **Timeline:** Liquidation costs tend to be lower than administration.

3.  **Compulsory Liquidation (Winding-Up by the Court)**:
    *   **Governing Rule:** Part 4 of the Insolvency Act 1986 and Insolvency Rules 1986.
    *   **Description:** This occurs when a creditor (or sometimes directors) applies to the court for a winding-up order, leading to the appointment of an Official Receiver and then potentially a liquidator to cease operations and distribute assets.
    *   **Justification:** Often a last resort, particularly for creditors who have exhausted other recovery options. It is generally a slower process.

4.  **Corporate Insolvency and Governance Act 2020 (CIGA) Moratorium (Standalone Moratorium)**:
    *   **Governing Rule:** Part A1 of the Insolvency Act 1986, introduced by CIGA 2020.
    *   **Description:** This is a standalone "breathing space" procedure for viable companies in financial distress, allowing them to pursue a rescue or restructuring plan without formal insolvency proceedings. A monitor (licensed insolvency practitioner) oversees the company.
    *   **Justification:** Provides an initial **20 business days** of protection from creditor action, extendable with consent, allowing directors to retain control of the business while developing a rescue plan. The company must continue to pay debts falling due during the moratorium, but not those due before.

5.  **Informal Arrangements / Debt Restructuring**:
    *   **Description:** Direct negotiations with individual creditors or groups of creditors to agree on revised payment plans, debt write-offs, or other modifications to existing debt obligations.
    *   **Justification:** A flexible and lower-cost option for companies that might not be able to afford formal insolvency proceedings or where the financial difficulties are less severe. However, these arrangements are not legally binding on all creditors unless formally documented and approved by all parties.

6.  **Members' Voluntary Liquidation (MVL)**:
    *   **Governing Rule:** Insolvency Act 1986 and Companies Act 2006.
    *   **Description:** This is a formal process for closing a *solvent* company (one that can pay all its debts, usually within 12 months). Assets are realised, debts paid, and remaining surplus distributed to shareholders.
    *   **Justification:** Not an alternative to administration for an insolvent company, but relevant for directors looking to close a debt-free or solvent company efficiently, for example, upon retirement or completion of its purpose.

Directors must prioritise creditors' interests once a company is considered insolvent. Seeking early professional advice from a licensed insolvency practitioner is crucial to determine the most appropriate route and avoid potential wrongful trading liability.
