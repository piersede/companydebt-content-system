For a UK company director, company administration, while aiming to rescue the business or achieve a better outcome for creditors than immediate liquidation, carries significant consequences, particularly regarding personal liability and future directorships.

The primary governing rule for company administration and directors' duties during insolvency is the **Insolvency Act 1986 (IA 1986)**, alongside the **Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986 (CDDA 1986)** and the **Companies Act 2006**.

Here are the key consequences and potential personal liabilities:

### 1. Loss of Management Control and Shift in Duties

Upon a company entering administration, directors immediately lose their power to make business decisions and control the company's affairs, assets, and strategic direction. An appointed administrator takes over these responsibilities. While directors may remain on the board, their role shifts to supporting the administrator, and they cannot enter into new contracts or make payments on the company's behalf.

Crucially, once a company becomes insolvent or faces the realistic prospect of insolvency, directors' duties shift from promoting the success of the company for its shareholders to prioritising the interests of the company's creditors.

### 2. Investigation into Conduct

The administrator (or a liquidator, if the company later moves into liquidation) is legally obliged to investigate the conduct of all directors who were in office during the three years leading up to the insolvency. They must submit a confidential report on this conduct to the Insolvency Service within six months of their appointment. This investigation assesses whether directors fulfilled their legal obligations and acted "unfitly" in the lead-up to insolvency.

### 3. Risk of Personal Liability

Despite the principle of limited liability, directors can face personal financial liability in several circumstances:

*   **Personal Guarantees:** Directors are personally liable for any company loans, leases, or supplier accounts for which they have provided a personal guarantee. The administrator or lender will pursue these guarantees.
*   **Overdrawn Director's Loan Account:** If a director owes money to the company through an overdrawn director's loan account, the administrator will require repayment, as this money is an asset of the company for the benefit of creditors.
*   **Wrongful Trading:**
    *   **Governing Rule:** Section 214 of the Insolvency Act 1986 (for liquidation) or Section 246ZB of the Insolvency Act 1986 (for administration).
    *   **Definition:** Wrongful trading occurs if a director continued to trade when they knew, or ought to have known, that there was no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvent liquidation or administration, and they failed to take every step to minimise potential losses to creditors. This does not require intent to defraud.
    *   **Consequence:** A court can order directors to contribute personally to the company's assets for the losses incurred from the point wrongful trading commenced. Directors are jointly and severally liable.
    *   **Timeline:** The court determines the date from which directors realised, or should have realised, that insolvency was unavoidable.
    *   **Qualifier:** Temporary suspension of personal liability for wrongful trading, introduced by the Corporate Insolvency and Governance Act 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic (1 March 2020 to 30 September 2020, and briefly in November 2020), has expired. Directors are now fully exposed to wrongful trading liability outside these periods.
*   **Fraudulent Trading:**
    *   **Governing Rule:** Section 213 of the Insolvency Act 1986 (for liquidation) or Section 246ZA of the Insolvency Act 1986 (for administration), and Section 993 of the Companies Act 2006.
    *   **Definition:** This is a more serious allegation involving carrying on the company's business with the intent to defraud creditors or for any other fraudulent purpose, requiring proof of dishonesty and actual intent.
    *   **Consequence:** Directors can be ordered to contribute personally to the company's debts. It is also a criminal offence, which can lead to imprisonment for up to 10 years, fines, or both.
*   **Misfeasance or Breach of Fiduciary Duty:**
    *   **Governing Rule:** Section 212 of the Insolvency Act 1986 and general directors' duties under the Companies Act 2006.
    *   **Definition:** This includes misapplying company money, acting outside the scope of their powers, failing to promote the company's success (now, creditors' interests), or making transactions at an undervalue or giving preferences to certain creditors.
    *   **Consequence:** Directors can be ordered to restore property or contribute to the company's assets to compensate for losses.
*   **HMRC Personal Liability Notices (PLNs) and Joint and Several Liability Notices (JLNs):**
    *   **Governing Rule:** Section 121C of the Social Security Administration Act 1992 (PLNs) and Finance Act 2020 (JLNs).
    *   **Definition:** HMRC can issue PLNs if a director intentionally or recklessly failed to ensure the company paid its PAYE, National Insurance, or Construction Industry Scheme deductions. JLNs target directors involved in patterns of repeated insolvencies ('phoenixism').
    *   **Consequence:** These notices make the director personally liable for the outstanding tax debts.
*   **Other Statutory Breaches:** Failure to comply with other statutory obligations, such as timely filing of accounts or confirmation statements with Companies House, can also result in personal fines or criminal prosecution.

### 4. Director Disqualification

*   **Governing Rule:** Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986 (CDDA 1986).
*   **Process:** If the Insolvency Service concludes a director's conduct makes them "unfit" to be involved in company management, they may initiate disqualification proceedings. Proceedings generally must be issued within three years of the company entering administration or liquidation.
*   **Period:** Disqualification periods range from **2 to 15 years**, depending on the severity of the misconduct:
    *   **2 to 5 years:** For reckless or negligent conduct.
    *   **6 to 10 years:** For serious misconduct, detrimental to public interest.
    *   **11 to 15 years:** For the most severe breaches, including fraudulent or criminal behaviour.
*   **Consequence of Disqualification:** A disqualified person is prohibited from acting as a director (whether formally appointed, a de facto director, or a shadow director), or being involved in the formation, promotion, or management of any UK-registered company. Breaching a disqualification order is a criminal offence and can lead to imprisonment and/or a fine.

### 5. Reputational Damage

Being associated with a company in administration, particularly if personal liability or disqualification proceedings arise, can significantly damage a director's professional reputation, making it challenging to secure future directorships or other business roles.

For a UK company director, proactive engagement with an insolvency practitioner at the earliest signs of financial difficulty is crucial to understand these risks and minimise potential personal liability.
