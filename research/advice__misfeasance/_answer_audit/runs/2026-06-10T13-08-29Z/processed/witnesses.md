As a UK company director, understanding misfeasance is crucial, especially when your company faces financial difficulties. Misfeasance refers to a director’s breach of their duties, leading to a loss for the company, and it's a claim often pursued by an insolvency practitioner (IP) when a company enters a formal insolvency procedure.

### What is Misfeasance?

In the context of UK company law, misfeasance specifically refers to a breach of trust or duty by an officer of the company, such as a director, which causes a loss to the company's assets. It isn't merely an error of judgment but rather an improper exercise of power or performance of duties. The claim usually arises under Section 212 of the Insolvency Act 1986 (IA 1986).

Directors owe various duties to their company, primarily governed by the Companies Act 2006 (CA 2006). These include:
*   Acting within their powers (Section 171 CA 2006).
*   Promoting the success of the company for the benefit of its members as a whole (Section 172 CA 2006).
*   Exercising independent judgment (Section 173 CA 2006).
*   Exercising reasonable care, skill, and diligence (Section 174 CA 2006).
*   Avoiding conflicts of interest (Section 175 CA 2006).
*   Not accepting benefits from third parties (Section 176 CA 2006).
*   Declaring any interest in a proposed transaction or arrangement with the company (Section 177 CA 2006).

When a company becomes insolvent or is bordering on insolvency, the duty to promote the success of the company shifts, and directors must consider the interests of the company's creditors. A breach of these duties that results in a quantifiable loss to the company can lead to a misfeasance claim.

Examples of acts that might constitute misfeasance include:
*   Authorising excessive or unjustified payments to themselves or connected parties.
*   Selling company assets at an undervalue.
*   Allowing the company to trade recklessly without any reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvency, even if not meeting the higher bar for wrongful trading.
*   Misusing company funds for personal benefit.
*   Preferring one creditor over others without a legitimate business reason, especially when insolvency is foreseeable.

### Who Can Bring a Misfeasance Claim?

A misfeasance claim can be brought by the company's office holder during a formal insolvency procedure. This typically includes:
*   A liquidator (in Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation or Compulsory Liquidation).
*   An administrator.
*   The Official Receiver (initially in Compulsory Liquidation).

The purpose of a misfeasance claim is to recover funds for the benefit of the general body of creditors.

### The Misfeasance Claim Process

1.  **Investigation:** Upon appointment, the liquidator or administrator will investigate the company's affairs, including the conduct of its directors. They will review financial records, bank statements, minutes, and other relevant documents to identify potential breaches of duty and losses to the company.
2.  **Report to The Insolvency Service:** In both Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation (CVL) and Compulsory Liquidation, the liquidator must submit a report on the conduct of all directors to The Insolvency Service. This report highlights any matters that indicate unfitness to act as a director, which can include instances of misfeasance.
3.  **Demand for Repayment/Compensation:** If the office holder identifies a valid misfeasance claim, they will typically issue a demand to the director(s) for repayment of the misapplied funds or compensation for the loss caused to the company.
4.  **Negotiation and Legal Action:** If a settlement cannot be reached, the office holder may initiate legal proceedings in court to recover the funds. This is done under Section 212 of the Insolvency Act 1986.

### Consequences for Directors

If a misfeasance claim is successful, the director(s) may be ordered by the court to:
*   Repay or restore money or property that was misapplied.
*   Contribute to the company's assets by way of compensation for the breach of duty.

Beyond financial liability, a finding of misfeasance can also lead to:
*   **Director Disqualification:** The Insolvency Service may apply to the court for a disqualification order against the director under the Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986 (CDDA 1986). The period of disqualification can range from 2 to 15 years, during which the individual cannot act as a director of any company, or be involved in the formation, management, or promotion of a company.
*   **Reputational Damage:** A successful claim or disqualification can severely damage a director's professional reputation.

### Related Director Liabilities in Insolvency

Misfeasance is one of several personal liabilities directors may face during insolvency. Others include:

1.  **Wrongful Trading (Section 214, Insolvency Act 1986):**
    *   **Definition:** This occurs when a director knew or *should have concluded* that there was no reasonable prospect of the company avoiding insolvency, but continued to trade, causing further loss to creditors. It applies from the "point of no return."
    *   **Process:** The liquidator or administrator investigates and can ask the court to declare that the director is personally liable to contribute to the company's assets.
    *   **Consequence:** Directors can be held personally liable for the increase in net deficiency of the company's assets from the date they should have ceased trading.
    *   **Distinction from Misfeasance:** Misfeasance focuses on specific breaches of duty (e.g., specific payments), while wrongful trading relates to the *decision to continue trading* when the company was clearly doomed.

2.  **Fraudulent Trading (Section 213, Insolvency Act 1986):**
    *   **Definition:** This is a more serious offense, involving carrying on the business of the company with intent to defraud creditors or for any fraudulent purpose.
    *   **Process:** Can be pursued by the office holder civilly or lead to criminal prosecution by the Serious Fraud Office or the Department for Business and Trade (DBT).
    *   **Consequence:** Directors can be held personally liable without limit for all or part of the company's debts. Criminal conviction can lead to imprisonment and/or fines.
    *   **Distinction:** Requires a higher burden of proof (intent to defraud) than wrongful trading or misfeasance.

3.  **Overdrawn Director's Loan Account (DLA):**
    *   **Definition:** If a director has taken more money out of the company (e.g., as drawings, expenses, or undocumented loans) than they have put in (e.g., as salary, dividends, or expenses owed to them), their DLA is overdrawn.
    *   **Process:** The liquidator will demand repayment of the overdrawn balance as it represents an asset of the company.
    *   **Consequence:** The director is personally liable to repay the full overdrawn amount to the company.

4.  **Personal Guarantees:**
    *   **Definition:** Directors often provide personal guarantees to secure company debts, particularly with banks, landlords, or key suppliers.
    *   **Process:** If the company enters insolvency and cannot pay the guaranteed debt, the creditor will pursue the director directly under the terms of the personal guarantee.
    *   **Consequence:** The director is personally liable for the guaranteed amount, irrespective of their conduct.

### Insolvency Routes and Misfeasance Claims

Misfeasance claims can arise in various insolvency procedures:

1.  **Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation (CVL):**
    *   **Process:** Initiated by the directors when the company is insolvent. Directors resolve to wind up the company, and shareholders agree. An IP is appointed as liquidator.
    *   **Relevance to Misfeasance:** The liquidator has a duty to investigate the directors' conduct and the company's affairs. Any identified misfeasance claims will be pursued to recover funds for creditors. Directors have a duty to cooperate with the liquidator.
    *   **Typical Cost:** Director pays for the liquidation if company assets are insufficient. Costs vary from £5,000 to £20,000+ depending on complexity.
    *   **Timeline:** Often quicker than compulsory liquidation, with investigations beginning immediately upon appointment.

2.  **Compulsory Liquidation (Winding-up Petition):**
    *   **Process:** Usually initiated by a creditor (often HMRC) petitioning the court to wind up the company because it cannot pay its debts. The Official Receiver (a civil servant) is initially appointed as liquidator, who may then appoint a private sector IP.
    *   **Relevance to Misfeasance:** The Official Receiver and any subsequent liquidator will rigorously investigate the directors' conduct, company assets, and liabilities. Misfeasance claims are a common outcome of these investigations.
    *   **Typical Cost:** The petitioning creditor often pays court fees and a deposit, which is usually reimbursed from company assets. The costs of the liquidation are typically met from company assets.
    *   **Timeline:** Can be lengthy, with court proceedings for the petition taking months, followed by investigations that can last for years.

3.  **Administration:**
    *   **Process:** Aims to rescue the company as a going concern, achieve a better result for creditors than liquidation, or realise property to make a distribution to secured or preferential creditors. An IP is appointed as administrator.
    *   **Relevance to Misfeasance:** While the primary goal is rescue or a better outcome, administrators also investigate director conduct. If the administration ultimately leads to liquidation (which is common), any misfeasance claims identified during administration will be passed to the liquidator to pursue.
    *   **Timeline:** Initial period is usually 12 months, extendable by court or creditor consent.

4.  **Company Voluntary Arrangement (CVA):**
    *   **Process:** A legally binding agreement between a company and its creditors to repay debts over a period, typically avoiding liquidation. Directors propose the arrangement, supervised by an IP (the nominee/supervisor).
    *   **Relevance to Misfeasance:** Misfeasance claims are less directly pursued *within* a CVA, as the focus is on ongoing trading and debt repayment. However, if the CVA fails and the company enters liquidation, the directors' conduct leading up to the CVA might be scrutinised for misfeasance or other breaches.

### Mitigation and Seeking Advice

To minimise your exposure as a director:
*   **Act Early:** Seek professional insolvency advice as soon as financial difficulties emerge.
*   **Document Decisions:** Ensure all significant decisions, especially those concerning company finances, are properly documented in board minutes, reflecting due consideration of directors' duties and the company's financial position.
*   **Prioritise Creditors:** When insolvency is probable, ensure that all actions are taken in the best interests of the company's creditors as a whole.
*   **Professional Advice:** Engage an experienced solicitor and/or licensed insolvency practitioner immediately to guide you through your duties and options. This demonstrates you are taking reasonable steps to understand and fulfil your obligations.

Understanding misfeasance and related liabilities is critical for UK directors. Early engagement with insolvency professionals can help you navigate these complex situations and potentially mitigate personal risk.

UK company directors face significant risks and potential personal liability, particularly when a company experiences financial distress or insolvency. Understanding these pitfalls and their legal underpinnings is crucial for responsible directorship.

Here are the main risks, pitfalls, and common mistakes UK company directors most often encounter, focusing on failure modes with real consequences:

### 1. Continuing to Trade While Insolvent (Wrongful Trading) and Personal Liability
**Risk/Pitfall:** A director continues to allow the company to trade when they knew, or ought to have known, that there was no reasonable prospect of the company avoiding insolvent liquidation or administration. This can lead to the company's financial position worsening, incurring further losses for creditors.

**Rule/Source:** Section 214 of the Insolvency Act 1986.
**Consequences:** If found guilty of wrongful trading, a director can be ordered by the court to contribute personally to the company's assets, covering the losses incurred by creditors from the point trading should have ceased. This can also be a ground for director disqualification for up to 15 years.

### 2. Preferring Some Creditors Over Others (Preferences)
**Risk/Pitfall:** When a company is facing insolvency, a director makes payments or transfers assets to one creditor, placing them in a better position than they would have been if the company had gone into insolvent liquidation. This often happens with connected parties (e.g., family members, other directors, or companies they control) or to avoid personal guarantees.

**Rule/Source:** Section 239 of the Insolvency Act 1986.
**Consequences:** An insolvency practitioner (liquidator or administrator) can apply to the court to have such transactions set aside, requiring the preferred creditor to return the funds or assets to the company for redistribution among all creditors. The relevant period for investigation is two years for connected parties and six months for unconnected parties before the onset of insolvency.

### 3. An Overdrawn Director's Loan Account Being Called In
**Risk/Pitfall:** A director takes more money from the company than they are entitled to (excluding salary or dividends properly declared with sufficient distributable profits), creating an overdrawn director's loan account (DLA).

**Consequences:** Upon company insolvency (liquidation or administration), an overdrawn DLA becomes a debt owed by the director to the company. The insolvency practitioner has a statutory duty to recover this debt for the benefit of creditors, making the director personally liable. Failure to repay can lead to legal action, charging orders, personal bankruptcy, and even director disqualification, especially if withdrawals occurred when the company was already struggling or insolvent. Additionally, HMRC may levy a Corporation Tax surcharge (Section 455 tax) if the DLA is not repaid within nine months of the company's year-end, and benefit-in-kind tax for interest-free loans exceeding £10,000.

### 4. Personal Guarantees Crystallising
**Risk/Pitfall:** A director provides a personal guarantee (PG) to a lender, landlord, or supplier, making them personally liable for a company debt if the company cannot repay it.

**Consequences:** When the company becomes insolvent and defaults on the loan or obligation, the personal guarantee bypasses the company's limited liability protection, making the director personally responsible for the debt. Lenders can pursue the director for the full amount, potentially leading to the sale of personal assets (e.g., property) or personal bankruptcy. If multiple directors have signed, they may be "jointly and severally" liable, meaning any one guarantor can be pursued for the entire amount.

### 5. Misuse of Dissolution / Strike-Off to Avoid Creditors
**Risk/Pitfall:** A director attempts to dissolve or "strike off" a company from the Companies House register to avoid paying debts to creditors, including HMRC and employees.

**Rule/Source:** The Rating (Coronavirus) and Directors Disqualification (Dissolved Companies) Act (2021) extended the powers of the Insolvency Service.
**Consequences:** The Insolvency Service can now investigate directors of companies that have already been dissolved. Directors found guilty of wrongdoing can face disqualification for up to 15 years, prosecution, and may be ordered to pay compensation to creditors. Creditors can also apply to the court to have the company reinstated to the register to pursue their claims.

### 6. Phoenix Company Restrictions on Reusing a Company Name
**Risk/Pitfall:** A director of a company that has gone into insolvent liquidation attempts to set up a new company (a "phoenix company") using the same or a very similar name to the liquidated company within a five-year period.

**Rule/Source:** Sections 216 and 217 of the Insolvency Act 1986.
**Consequences:** Breaching these restrictions is a criminal offence, punishable by imprisonment and/or a fine. Furthermore, the director can be made personally liable for the debts of the new company incurred during the period it used the prohibited name. There are limited exceptions, such as notifying all creditors and publishing a notice in the Gazette when acquiring the business from an insolvency practitioner, or obtaining court permission.

### 7. Director Disqualification
**Risk/Pitfall:** Engaging in "unfit conduct" as a director, which can encompass a wide range of actions or omissions in the management of a company, particularly leading up to or during insolvency.

**Rule/Source:** Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986 (CDDA).
**Grounds for Disqualification (examples):**
*   Wrongful or fraudulent trading.
*   Failure to keep proper company accounting records.
*   Failure to file financial accounts and/or confirmation statements at Companies House on time.
*   Not paying taxes owed.
*   Misuse of company funds, including overdrawn director's loan accounts.
*   Fraudulent activities, such as misapplication of Bounce Back Loan monies.
*   Failing to act in the best interests of the company or breaching fiduciary duties.
**Consequences:** A disqualified director is prohibited from acting as a director (directly or indirectly) or being involved in the formation, promotion, or management of any UK-registered company for a period typically ranging from 2 to 15 years. Disqualification can also lead to personal liability for company debts.

### 8. HMRC's Preferential Status and Personal Liability Notices
**a. HMRC's Preferential Status**
**Risk/Pitfall:** Directors failing to account for HMRC's enhanced position in insolvency proceedings, which can significantly impact the funds available for other creditors.

**Rule/Source:** Under the Finance Act 2020, for insolvencies commencing on or after 1 December 2020, HMRC regained its status as a "secondary preferential creditor" for certain taxes.
**Consequences:** HMRC's claims for taxes collected and held by the business on behalf of others (such as PAYE, VAT, employee NICs, and Construction Industry Scheme deductions) now rank above floating charge holders and other unsecured creditors. This means that in an insolvency, these specific tax debts are paid out earlier, potentially reducing the returns for other creditors.

**b. HMRC Personal Liability Notices (PLNs)**
**Risk/Pitfall:** Directors whose companies fail to pay certain tax liabilities due to their fraud or neglect can find themselves personally pursued by HMRC.

**Rule/Source:** PLNs are statutory notices issued by HMRC, often under the Social Security Administration Act 1992 (for NICs) and also applying to VAT.
**Consequences:** HMRC can transfer the company's liability for unpaid PAYE, National Insurance Contributions (NICs), VAT, and CIS deductions to the director personally. This action is typically taken where HMRC believes the failure to pay was due to the director's deliberate conduct or serious failure to take reasonable care, rather than just financial difficulty. Directors can become personally liable for the unpaid amounts, including interest and penalties.

For a UK company director, misfeasance refers to the improper performance of a lawful act or duty, or a breach of fiduciary duties owed to a company, especially when the business is or may be insolvent. It involves actions such as misapplying company assets, money, or authority, or failing to comply with legal duties. It's crucial to understand that misfeasance is a civil claim, not a criminal offence, though severe cases can have criminal implications.

The duties of a UK company director, enshrined in the Companies Act 2006 (CA 2006), include acting within powers, promoting the company's success, exercising independent judgment, exercising reasonable care, skill and diligence, avoiding conflicts of interest, not accepting benefits from third parties, and declaring interests in proposed transactions. A breach of these duties can lead to a misfeasance claim.

**Consequences for Company Directors of Misfeasance:**

The consequences for directors found guilty of misfeasance can be severe and include significant personal liability:

1.  **Personal Financial Liability:** Directors can be held personally liable for any losses incurred by the company or its creditors as a result of their misfeasance. A court may order a director to:
    *   Restore property or assets to the company.
    *   Repay misapplied money or funds to the company, often with interest.
    *   Compensate the company for losses caused by the breach of duty.
    In extreme cases, this personal liability can lead to the loss of all personal assets through bankruptcy. Examples of actions that may lead to personal financial liability include preferential payments to certain creditors, transactions at an undervalue, misusing company assets for personal benefit, or taking excessive salaries when the company is in financial distress.

2.  **Director Disqualification:** Directors can be disqualified from acting as a limited company director for a period ranging from **2 to 15 years**. Misfeasance is considered "unfit conduct" under the Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986 (CDDA 1986). The office-holder (liquidator or administrator) is legally required to submit a report to the Secretary of State regarding instances of misfeasance, which can initiate disqualification proceedings.

3.  **Criminal Liability:** While misfeasance itself is a civil matter, in serious cases, particularly those involving elements of fraud, theft, or false accounting, directors could face criminal prosecution.

4.  **Professional and Reputational Damage:** A finding of misfeasance can lead to diminished future employability, especially at senior levels, and potentially the loss of professional qualifications or memberships.

**Relevant Insolvency Route(s) and Governing Rules:**

Misfeasance claims predominantly arise when a company enters **liquidation** or **administration**. The appointed insolvency practitioner (liquidator or administrator) is legally obliged to investigate the director's actions leading up to the insolvency. Claims can also be brought by creditors, a contributory to the company's capital, or the Official Receiver.

The primary legal basis for misfeasance claims against directors is **Section 212 of the Insolvency Act 1986 (IA 1986)**. This provision grants the court the power to examine the conduct of directors and compel them to account for misapplied money or property, or to contribute to the company's assets as compensation for the misfeasance or breach. The **Companies Act 2006** also holds directors personally liable for losses to creditors in misfeasance cases, and the duties outlined in **Sections 171 to 177 of the CA 2006** define the standard of conduct expected from directors.

**Figures, Thresholds, and Timelines:**

*   **Disqualification Period:** If found unfit, a director can be disqualified for **2 to 15 years**.
*   **Limitation Period for Misfeasance Claims:** Generally, misfeasance proceedings must be issued within **six years** of the relevant conduct. However, different limitation rules may apply for breaches of trust or fiduciary obligations, and in cases where a director unlawfully transfers property to a company they control, there might be **no limitation period**. The six-year period can run from the date of the conduct or from when the company entered an insolvency process.
*   **Disqualification Proceedings Timeline:** Disqualification proceedings must be issued within **three years** of the company being placed into liquidation or administration.

Directors are expected to be aware of their company's financial position at all times. Being unaware or acting as a non-executive or part-time director is generally not a defence against a misfeasance claim. Early professional advice is crucial if financial difficulties arise or misfeasance allegations are made.

For a UK company director, a misfeasance claim is primarily the appropriate option when a company has entered an insolvency procedure, such as **liquidation** or **administration**, and there is evidence that a director (or other company officer) has misused company money, property, or authority, or has breached their duties owed to the company. The core purpose of such a claim is to recover money or assets that were improperly used or to compensate the company for losses caused by the misconduct, ultimately for the benefit of the company and its creditors.

### Governing Rule and Legal Basis

The legal basis for misfeasance claims against directors is most commonly found under **Section 212 of the Insolvency Act 1986**. This section grants the court the power to examine the conduct of individuals involved in the company's management (including current or past officers, liquidators, administrators, or anyone involved in the promotion, formation, or management of the company) if they have:

*   Misapplied or retained any company money or property.
*   Become accountable for any money or other property of the company.
*   Been guilty of any misfeasance or breach of any fiduciary or other duty in relation to the company.

The duties that directors are expected to uphold, and which if breached can lead to a misfeasance claim, are largely codified in **Sections 171 to 177 of the Companies Act 2006**. These general duties include:

*   To act within their powers.
*   To promote the success of the company.
*   To exercise independent judgment.
*   To exercise reasonable care, skill, and diligence.
*   To avoid conflicts of interest.
*   Not to accept benefits from third parties.
*   To declare an interest in a proposed transaction or arrangement.

When a company is insolvent or nearing insolvency, directors' duties shift to prioritise the interests of creditors.

### Figures, Thresholds, and Timelines

*   **Limitation Period:** Misfeasance claims are generally subject to a limitation period. In many cases, proceedings must be issued within **six years** from the date the company entered into the insolvency process (liquidation or administration). However, for claims involving breaches of trust or fiduciary obligations, different limitation rules may apply, with some sources indicating that claims based on a breach of fiduciary duty may not be subject to the same time limitations. It is possible for a breach that occurred years before liquidation to still be claimed, potentially extending the total window from the breach to the claim to up to **11 years**.
*   **Proof:** To succeed, the claimant (usually the insolvency practitioner) must demonstrate that the defendant was involved in the company's management, that company assets were misapplied or duties breached, and that the company suffered financial loss as a result. The standard of proof is the civil standard of 'balance of probabilities'.
*   **Consequences:** If misfeasance is proven, the court has broad discretion to order the director to repay, restore, or account for money or property to the company (with interest) or to contribute a sum by way of compensation to the company's assets. Directors may also face disqualification from acting as a director for a period of **2 to 15 years** under the Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986.

### Alternatives to a Misfeasance Claim

Misfeasance is one of several tools available to insolvency practitioners. Other claims that can be pursued against directors, often alongside or as alternatives to misfeasance, include:

1.  **Wrongful Trading (Section 214, Insolvency Act 1986):** This occurs when directors continue to trade when they knew, or ought to have known, that the company had no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvent liquidation or administration, thereby increasing losses to creditors. Directors can be ordered to contribute to the company's assets. Wrongful trading is a civil offence.
2.  **Fraudulent Trading (Section 213, Insolvency Act 1986, and Companies Act 2006/Fraud Act 2006):** This involves carrying on any company business with the intent to defraud creditors or for any other fraudulent purpose. It requires proving dishonesty and an intention to defraud. Fraudulent trading is both a civil claim and a criminal offence, carrying more severe consequences than wrongful trading.
3.  **Transactions at Undervalue (Sections 238-241, Insolvency Act 1986):** These claims arise when a company transfers assets for significantly less than their true value, or receives no consideration, particularly in the period leading up to insolvency. The court can set aside such transactions and restore assets. A misfeasance claim can sometimes be an easier route to challenge undervalue transactions compared to specific insolvency provisions, as the insolvency practitioner may not need to prove the insolvency element under misfeasance provisions.
4.  **Preference Claims (Sections 239-241, Insolvency Act 1986):** These occur when a company, at a relevant time, does anything that puts a creditor or guarantor in a better position than they would have been in if the company had gone into insolvent liquidation. An example is a director paying off a loan for which they provided a personal guarantee, in preference to other creditors.
5.  **Director Disqualification Proceedings:** While not a claim for monetary recovery, the conduct underlying misfeasance, wrongful trading, or fraudulent trading can also lead to proceedings under the Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986, preventing individuals from acting as directors for a specified period.

Misfeasance claims are often brought concurrently with other claims, particularly where financial records reveal multiple issues, as they cover specific breaches of duty and transactions, while wrongful trading covers the period of continued trading beyond the point of inevitable insolvency. Directors should seek professional advice when insolvency risks emerge to understand their duties and mitigate potential personal liability.
