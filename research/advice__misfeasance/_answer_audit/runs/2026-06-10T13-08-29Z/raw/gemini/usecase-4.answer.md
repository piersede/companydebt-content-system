For a UK company director, a misfeasance claim is primarily the appropriate option when a company has entered an insolvency procedure, such as **liquidation** or **administration**, and there is evidence that a director (or other company officer) has misused company money, property, or authority, or has breached their duties owed to the company. The core purpose of such a claim is to recover money or assets that were improperly used or to compensate the company for losses caused by the misconduct, ultimately for the benefit of the company and its creditors.

### Governing Rule and Legal Basis

The legal basis for misfeasance claims against directors is most commonly found under **Section 212 of the Insolvency Act 1986**. This section grants the court the power to examine the conduct of individuals involved in the company's management (including current or past officers, liquidators, administrators, or anyone involved in the promotion, formation, or management of the company) if they have:

*   Misapplied or retained any company money or property.
*   Become accountable for any money or other property of the company.
*   Been guilty of any misfeasance or breach of any fiduciary or other duty in relation to the company.

The duties that directors are expected to uphold, and which if breached can lead to a misfeasance claim, are largely codified in **Sections 171 to 177 of the Companies Act 2006**. These general duties include:

*   To act within their powers.
*   To promote the success of the company.
*   To exercise independent judgment.
*   To exercise reasonable care, skill, and diligence.
*   To avoid conflicts of interest.
*   Not to accept benefits from third parties.
*   To declare an interest in a proposed transaction or arrangement.

When a company is insolvent or nearing insolvency, directors' duties shift to prioritise the interests of creditors.

### Figures, Thresholds, and Timelines

*   **Limitation Period:** Misfeasance claims are generally subject to a limitation period. In many cases, proceedings must be issued within **six years** from the date the company entered into the insolvency process (liquidation or administration). However, for claims involving breaches of trust or fiduciary obligations, different limitation rules may apply, with some sources indicating that claims based on a breach of fiduciary duty may not be subject to the same time limitations. It is possible for a breach that occurred years before liquidation to still be claimed, potentially extending the total window from the breach to the claim to up to **11 years**.
*   **Proof:** To succeed, the claimant (usually the insolvency practitioner) must demonstrate that the defendant was involved in the company's management, that company assets were misapplied or duties breached, and that the company suffered financial loss as a result. The standard of proof is the civil standard of 'balance of probabilities'.
*   **Consequences:** If misfeasance is proven, the court has broad discretion to order the director to repay, restore, or account for money or property to the company (with interest) or to contribute a sum by way of compensation to the company's assets. Directors may also face disqualification from acting as a director for a period of **2 to 15 years** under the Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986.

### Alternatives to a Misfeasance Claim

Misfeasance is one of several tools available to insolvency practitioners. Other claims that can be pursued against directors, often alongside or as alternatives to misfeasance, include:

1.  **Wrongful Trading (Section 214, Insolvency Act 1986):** This occurs when directors continue to trade when they knew, or ought to have known, that the company had no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvent liquidation or administration, thereby increasing losses to creditors. Directors can be ordered to contribute to the company's assets. Wrongful trading is a civil offence.
2.  **Fraudulent Trading (Section 213, Insolvency Act 1986, and Companies Act 2006/Fraud Act 2006):** This involves carrying on any company business with the intent to defraud creditors or for any other fraudulent purpose. It requires proving dishonesty and an intention to defraud. Fraudulent trading is both a civil claim and a criminal offence, carrying more severe consequences than wrongful trading.
3.  **Transactions at Undervalue (Sections 238-241, Insolvency Act 1986):** These claims arise when a company transfers assets for significantly less than their true value, or receives no consideration, particularly in the period leading up to insolvency. The court can set aside such transactions and restore assets. A misfeasance claim can sometimes be an easier route to challenge undervalue transactions compared to specific insolvency provisions, as the insolvency practitioner may not need to prove the insolvency element under misfeasance provisions.
4.  **Preference Claims (Sections 239-241, Insolvency Act 1986):** These occur when a company, at a relevant time, does anything that puts a creditor or guarantor in a better position than they would have been in if the company had gone into insolvent liquidation. An example is a director paying off a loan for which they provided a personal guarantee, in preference to other creditors.
5.  **Director Disqualification Proceedings:** While not a claim for monetary recovery, the conduct underlying misfeasance, wrongful trading, or fraudulent trading can also lead to proceedings under the Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986, preventing individuals from acting as directors for a specified period.

Misfeasance claims are often brought concurrently with other claims, particularly where financial records reveal multiple issues, as they cover specific breaches of duty and transactions, while wrongful trading covers the period of continued trading beyond the point of inevitable insolvency. Directors should seek professional advice when insolvency risks emerge to understand their duties and mitigate potential personal liability.
