For a UK company director, misfeasance refers to the improper performance of a lawful act or duty, or a breach of fiduciary duties owed to a company, especially when the business is or may be insolvent. It involves actions such as misapplying company assets, money, or authority, or failing to comply with legal duties. It's crucial to understand that misfeasance is a civil claim, not a criminal offence, though severe cases can have criminal implications.

The duties of a UK company director, enshrined in the Companies Act 2006 (CA 2006), include acting within powers, promoting the company's success, exercising independent judgment, exercising reasonable care, skill and diligence, avoiding conflicts of interest, not accepting benefits from third parties, and declaring interests in proposed transactions. A breach of these duties can lead to a misfeasance claim.

**Consequences for Company Directors of Misfeasance:**

The consequences for directors found guilty of misfeasance can be severe and include significant personal liability:

1.  **Personal Financial Liability:** Directors can be held personally liable for any losses incurred by the company or its creditors as a result of their misfeasance. A court may order a director to:
    *   Restore property or assets to the company.
    *   Repay misapplied money or funds to the company, often with interest.
    *   Compensate the company for losses caused by the breach of duty.
    In extreme cases, this personal liability can lead to the loss of all personal assets through bankruptcy. Examples of actions that may lead to personal financial liability include preferential payments to certain creditors, transactions at an undervalue, misusing company assets for personal benefit, or taking excessive salaries when the company is in financial distress.

2.  **Director Disqualification:** Directors can be disqualified from acting as a limited company director for a period ranging from **2 to 15 years**. Misfeasance is considered "unfit conduct" under the Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986 (CDDA 1986). The office-holder (liquidator or administrator) is legally required to submit a report to the Secretary of State regarding instances of misfeasance, which can initiate disqualification proceedings.

3.  **Criminal Liability:** While misfeasance itself is a civil matter, in serious cases, particularly those involving elements of fraud, theft, or false accounting, directors could face criminal prosecution.

4.  **Professional and Reputational Damage:** A finding of misfeasance can lead to diminished future employability, especially at senior levels, and potentially the loss of professional qualifications or memberships.

**Relevant Insolvency Route(s) and Governing Rules:**

Misfeasance claims predominantly arise when a company enters **liquidation** or **administration**. The appointed insolvency practitioner (liquidator or administrator) is legally obliged to investigate the director's actions leading up to the insolvency. Claims can also be brought by creditors, a contributory to the company's capital, or the Official Receiver.

The primary legal basis for misfeasance claims against directors is **Section 212 of the Insolvency Act 1986 (IA 1986)**. This provision grants the court the power to examine the conduct of directors and compel them to account for misapplied money or property, or to contribute to the company's assets as compensation for the misfeasance or breach. The **Companies Act 2006** also holds directors personally liable for losses to creditors in misfeasance cases, and the duties outlined in **Sections 171 to 177 of the CA 2006** define the standard of conduct expected from directors.

**Figures, Thresholds, and Timelines:**

*   **Disqualification Period:** If found unfit, a director can be disqualified for **2 to 15 years**.
*   **Limitation Period for Misfeasance Claims:** Generally, misfeasance proceedings must be issued within **six years** of the relevant conduct. However, different limitation rules may apply for breaches of trust or fiduciary obligations, and in cases where a director unlawfully transfers property to a company they control, there might be **no limitation period**. The six-year period can run from the date of the conduct or from when the company entered an insolvency process.
*   **Disqualification Proceedings Timeline:** Disqualification proceedings must be issued within **three years** of the company being placed into liquidation or administration.

Directors are expected to be aware of their company's financial position at all times. Being unaware or acting as a non-executive or part-time director is generally not a defence against a misfeasance claim. Early professional advice is crucial if financial difficulties arise or misfeasance allegations are made.
