NOTE ON SCOPE: this was a FULL-PAGE cold read, not the opening-500-word read that
stranger_read.py generates by default. The reader was given the entire rendered page
(7,692 words as a reader sees it, tables and FAQs included) and a seven-question brief
covering drop-off point, unexplained terms, the question they arrived with,
contradictions and repeats, performance writing, unsupported claims, and specifically the
second half of the page from the cost section onwards. Persona: director of a small
building firm with four staff, no insolvency knowledge, holding an HMRC letter they do
not understand. The reader had no drafting context.

1. THE DROP-OFF

The opening four paragraphs were the best thing on the page. Four routes, laid out, no
waffle. I was still reading properly through the strike-off section.

Where I started skimming: the Members Voluntary Liquidation section, at the tax
paragraph, "The tax attraction is Business Asset Disposal Relief. From 6 April 2026,
qualifying disposals are charged at 18% ... against a lifetime limit of 1 million pounds
of qualifying gains per person". A million pounds. My company owes money, it does not
have a million pounds of anything. From there I skipped everything with a percentage in
it.

Where I would actually have stopped and put the phone down: the "Strike-Off or MVL: How
to Decide" section, at "A shareholder who used 900,000 pounds of the BADR lifetime
allowance on an earlier exit has 100,000 pounds of it left." That is not me. Nowhere near
me. Two whole sections written for somebody with a million quid in their company, sitting
in the middle of a page I found by searching how to close my company because HMRC has
written to me.

To be fair, I would have scrolled on with my thumb looking for the word "guarantee", and
I would have found the liability table. But I read maybe a third of what is between the
top and there.

3. THE QUESTION YOU CAME WITH

Do I know what to do first? Yes, roughly. Take the test, ring the number, dig out my
paperwork. That is clear and it is at the end where I expected it.

Do I know whether I personally have to pay? No. I know what it depends on, which is not
the same thing.

The page tells me twice, clearly, that company debt is not my debt. Good. It tells me the
thing that actually decides it: "Dig out the loan agreements, the lease and any finance
paperwork and look for your own signature on a guarantee." That is the single most useful
sentence on the page and it is over halfway down.

What is missing. My house: I came here worried about my house. The word house does not
appear. Nor home. Nothing tells me whether a guarantee I signed can be enforced against
the house I live in. The personal side stops at "the lender can pursue the guarantor".
Pursue how? Take what? What a guarantee looks like: I am told to hunt for my signature on
one. I do not know what one looks like on a page. Separate document? A paragraph inside
the lease? What is it headed? Tell me what to look for and I can go and look tonight. My
HMRC letter: nothing helps me work out which kind of letter I am holding, and the page
says by the morning of the hearing my options are gone, so the one thing I urgently need
is to know whether my letter is one of those. My four staff: the employees section never
tells me what happens if there is no money to pay them, from my angle. Am I off the hook
for their redundancy or not? Do they get paid? What it costs me: the cost section says
"Company; failing that, directors or third-party funder". So if the company is empty, I
pay. How much? Every number on this page is for the rich version. There is not one figure
for the route I am obviously heading for.

4. CONTRADICTIONS AND REPEATS

Objections: one place says they can be raised "at any point during that window or later
while the application is still active", another says the objection "tends to arrive before
the company is struck off at all". On a first read I was unsure whether I am safe once the
two months are up.

"At 13 pounds online it is the cheapest thing on this page." Then the cost table adds an
"Accountant fee for final accounts and returns", plus VAT. So it is not 13 pounds. 13
pounds is the only part that is 13 pounds.

Repeats I noticed as a reader, not as a nitpick: the 13 and 18 pound fee, seven times. The
25,000 pound rule, seven times, including an FAQ that repeats the section almost word for
word. "Personal liability needs a separate legal basis", six times in six phrasings; by
the sixth it sounds like a lawyer covering himself. 478 days and 1.8%, four times. Bounce
Back Loans had no personal guarantee, five times. "Take advice on your own numbers", three
times.

5. WRITTEN TO MAKE YOU FEEL SOMETHING

Not much, and the page mostly leaves my feelings alone. Thank you for that. It never tells
me how my evening is going. But some lines are built to land rather than to inform:
"Closing a company that could have traded on is the one decision on this page you cannot
undo" (reads like a closing line in a speech, and is arguable since the page later says a
dissolved company can be restored). "Nobody chooses it" (dramatic, says nothing). "The
reason is mechanical rather than moral" (clever, not information). "The liabilities that
catch directors out are the ones with no invoice attached to them" (nice sentence, then
properly explained in the next paragraph, so the sentence itself was decoration). "The
assets left behind are the invisible ones" (same trick, same shape). "And you paid the
accountant to file the return that generated it" (written for a wince). "It bites hardest
with...". "Nothing chases you for either, so both get left" (neat, and the writer telling
me about myself). "Conduct claims are the ones directors worry about" - this one assumes
what I am afraid of, and it is wrong. I am worried about my house. "The useful call is
todays" - a closing line for a sale phrased as timing advice.

Sales pitch dressed as advice: the solvent-company CTA box is an advert dropped into the
middle of the guide and it is aimed at the customer with money, not at me. "That is the
first thing we establish when a director calls us" and "We establish that at the start
rather than after a route has been picked" are both "we are thorough" wrapped in a fact.
Every section opens by mentioning that directors call them; I counted eight or nine. It
stops reading as experience and starts reading as advertising. Credit where due: the page
says outright "We are not independent of the process described on this page" and that they
get paid. That is straight, and more than most.

6. CLAIMS WITH NOTHING BEHIND THEM

"In practice, most of the directors we speak to have already decided they want the company
closed." "HMRC objects routinely." "Where HMRC is owed, usually yes." "Which for a
higher-rate taxpayer is normally the cheaper treatment." "The liquidator will usually
pursue it", twice. "Normally a company asset" and "normally a company liability", twice in
three lines with no when-not. "We steer them here rather than into a strike-off." "The
payment often covers a real slice of the CVL cost" - this bothered me most, because it is
money and it is vague, and the page then says do not budget on it, so which? "Banks often
restrict the company account." "Tends to protect the companys value." "That saving can
significantly outweigh the MVL professional fee." "Neither cheap nor fast" with no number
anywhere for restoring a company. "We have spoken to directors who paid tens of thousands
of pounds" - striking, and completely unsupported; how many, one or twenty? "The single
most common thing we have to unpick." "We see this trip up otherwise clean strike-offs."
"Any typical prices we give are broad ranges observed across recent cases" - the page then
gives no typical prices at all for a CVL, so the caveat covers nothing.

The MVL numbers are the exception: 478 days and 1.8% are sourced to a named June 2026
study and flagged as medians. That is how the rest should read.

7. THE MIDDLE AND THE END

From the cost section on, the sections stop feeling written and start feeling assembled.
Each one is the same shape in the same order every time: heading, one sentence of framing,
a table, then three or four short comment paragraphs under it. Cost, time, tax, liability,
all built the same way. By the time-section table I was reading the tables and skipping
the words around them, because I had learned the words around them were caveats.

The specific flat patches. "Costs vary by route and by case" opens a section I came to for
a number by telling me numbers vary. "Three separate clocks run here" is a good line, then
the section never gives me a straight answer for a CVL beyond "frequently over a year".
The tax section is the deadest writing on the page; I read "the sequence matters, because
the treatment of a distribution depends on whether it is paid ahead of a strike-off or
through a liquidator, and the route you pick decides that" twice and got nothing out of
it. The rhythm gets mechanical: short flat statement, then a "because" clause explaining
it, over and over, and four or five sections open with almost the same beat. The
personal-liability section is the one I needed most and it is a bulleted list of seven
legal mechanisms with no explanation of any of them; "misfeasance, where breach of duty
caused identifiable loss" - I do not know what that means and I am the person it is about.
The FAQs repeat the body almost verbatim in places, and none of the fifteen questions
mention a guarantee, a house, or four staff.

The employees section is the only bit in the second half that sounds like a person
talking: "We ask about the contract, the payroll and the drawings at the first
conversation, because those are what the claim turns on."

Does the last section tell me what to do? Yes, and it is the best part of the page. Five
steps, in order, plain. And then it tells me the truth I wanted, in the plainest sentence
anywhere on the page: closing "does not touch a guarantee you signed, a balance you owe
back on a director loan account, or conduct already in the past, and no practitioner,
ourselves included, can make those disappear."

That last sentence is the answer to the question I arrived with. It is at the very bottom,
after four thousand words about people richer than me. Put it near the top and I would
have trusted the rest.
