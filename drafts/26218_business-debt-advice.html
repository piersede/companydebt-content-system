The debt won't wait for you to feel ready. If your business is behind on HMRC, overdrawn with the bank, or chasing cashflow every Friday to cover payroll, the window for your best options narrows with every month you delay.

We see this in the cases that come to us. Directors who rang an insolvency practitioner in January, when the first HMRC letter arrived, had a full range of choices. Directors who rang in October, after three more letters and a statutory demand, had one or two at best.

The good news is that free, regulated business debt advice genuinely exists in the UK. Your job is to find the right channel for your situation and use it before creditors use theirs.

Why Business Debt Advice Matters Before It Becomes a Crisis

Most directors who contact us say the same thing: they knew something was wrong six months before they called. They kept hoping the next contract, the next quarter, would fix it. It rarely does, and waiting forecloses options.

A Time to Pay arrangement with HMRC is considerably easier to negotiate before HMRC has issued a distraint notice than after. A Company Voluntary Arrangement is available when a business has viable trading prospects; by the time it is clearly insolvent with no rescue path, liquidation becomes the only route.

You don't need certainty that your business is failing before you seek advice. A cashflow problem you can't solve internally, a creditor applying pressure you can't absorb, or numbers moving in the wrong direction: any of those is enough to make the call.

Free Business Debt Advice: The Regulated Options

Several organisations provide free, independent business debt advice in the UK. What they offer, who they serve best, and what they can and can't do varies.

Business Debtline (0800 197 6026) is run by the Money Advice Trust and is the most directly relevant service for small businesses and sole traders in Great Britain. Their advisers are trained specifically in business debt, not personal debt repackaged for business use.

They can help you work through your creditor list, prioritise which debts are most urgent, and identify formal options including Time to Pay, administration, and creditors' voluntary liquidation. The service is free and confidential. It is genuinely useful if your debts are manageable enough that a negotiated approach might resolve them.

Citizens Advice offers business debt guidance alongside its personal debt services. The depth of business debt expertise varies by location, but it's a practical first port of call if your situation involves a mix of business and personal liability. This is often the case for sole traders whose business and personal finances are intertwined.

Advice NI provides similar services in Northern Ireland for smaller businesses, covering debt and financial advice.

These services are regulated and free. None of them will try to sell you anything. The limit is that they cannot manage an insolvency process on your behalf; for that you need a licensed insolvency practitioner.

What Your Bank Can Offer for Business Debt

If your debt is primarily to your bank, the bank itself is often willing to discuss restructuring options, but you need to go to them before they come to you. A relationship manager who receives your call when you are two months behind but proactively seeking a solution is in a very different position from one who discovers the problem through a missed payment.

Banks can restructure loan terms, extend repayment periods, or offer short-term payment holidays, though none of these are guaranteed. All depend on your bank's internal credit assessment of your business.

Be realistic about what the bank's incentives are. They want to recover their money, not rescue your business. Bank restructuring buys time but does not address the underlying position. We've seen directors who spent eighteen months in bank discussions while the creditor position worsened, because the restructuring gave them false reassurance that the problem was solved.

HMRC Business Debt Advice and Time to Pay

HMRC is often the largest unsecured creditor for struggling SMEs, and it has its own debt management processes. A Time to Pay (TTP) arrangement allows you to repay overdue tax liabilities in monthly instalments, typically over six to twelve months for straightforward cases.

HMRC negotiates these directly through its Business Payment Support Service, and the standard process does not require a solicitor or accountant. Having accurate management accounts ready will help your case significantly.

HMRC's position on TTP hardened after Crown preference was reinstated in December 2020 under the Finance Act 2020. HMRC now ranks above ordinary unsecured creditors for certain tax debts including VAT and PAYE, which gives them less incentive to compromise than they once had.

A TTP will not write off what you owe; it spaces out the payments. If your business cannot sustain even a structured repayment, TTP is the wrong tool and seeking advice from a licensed insolvency practitioner about formal options is the more honest path.

When You Need a Licensed Insolvency Practitioner

Free advice services are valuable, but they stop at the point where formal insolvency law begins. If your situation has reached any of the following, you need a licensed insolvency practitioner (IP), not a debt helpline:

  • You've received a statutory demand for more than £750
  • A winding-up petition has been issued or is threatened
  • HMRC bailiffs have visited or sent enforcement notices
  • Your directors' loan account is overdrawn and you cannot repay it
  • Your business is cashflow insolvent and you're using new borrowing to service old debt
  • You're considering closing the company and want to know whether a CVL or a strike-off is the right route

Insolvency practitioners are licensed by recognised professional bodies including the Insolvency Practitioners Association, ICAEW, and the Law Society. The Insolvency Service maintains a public register. R3, the trade body for insolvency professionals, also provides a directory.

Avoid any firm that is not regulated by one of these bodies. Unregulated debt advisers in the business space can cause significant additional damage.

FCA and SRA-Regulated Business Debt Advice

Some debt advice services are regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) or the Solicitors Regulation Authority (SRA). FCA regulation applies to firms offering consumer credit and certain debt management services. SRA regulation applies where solicitors are providing legal advice on debt enforcement or insolvency proceedings.

For business debt specifically, the most important regulatory marker is whether an insolvency practitioner is licensed by a recognised professional body under the Insolvency Act 1986. FCA regulation alone does not qualify a firm to manage an insolvency process.

What Business Debt Advice Won't Fix

No advice service, free or paid, can fix a business whose underlying model is unviable. If your costs are structurally higher than your revenue with no realistic path to reversing that, the question shifts. It is no longer which debt advice channel to use; it is which formal process manages the exit with least damage to you personally.

Directors sometimes seek advice that confirms the business is salvageable rather than advice that honestly assesses whether it is. We understand the impulse. The honest assessment, delivered earlier, is always less damaging than the comfortable one delivered too late.

A Company Voluntary Arrangement (CVA) proposed when a business still has reasonable prospects has a chance of creditor approval. The same proposal when the company has been losing cash for two years rarely passes the 75% creditor vote threshold required by s.4(6) of the Insolvency Act 1986.

Your Next Step

If you have time, start with Business Debtline (0800 197 6026). It costs nothing, the advisers are trained in business debt, and you'll leave the call with a clearer picture of which options apply to your position.

If you're past debt management and into formal insolvency territory, call a licensed practitioner directly. The two conversations are not mutually exclusive; many directors speak to both.

What you shouldn't do is wait. The creditor who has been patient for six months tends not to stay patient after the seventh. Directors who act early keep more options. Directors who delay keep fewer, often none. If the cashflow conversation on Sunday night is becoming routine, Monday morning is a reasonable time to make the call.
