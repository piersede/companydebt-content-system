If you are sitting on a dormant limited company and wondering whether you can liquidate it, close it, or leave it dormant indefinitely, the answer depends on three things: whether the company is solvent, whether it has distributable assets, and whether it has traded at any point in the current accounting period.

We see directors come to us every week with dormant shells they want to tidy up, and the right route for your company is rarely the one you first assume.

What follows: what dormant actually means in UK company law, the four options open to you for closing or winding up a dormant entity, and which route gives you the best outcome depending on your circumstances.

The Quick Answer for Directors

Yes, you can liquidate a dormant company, but in many cases you will not need to. If the company is solvent and has no significant assets, a voluntary strike-off under Section 1003 of the Companies Act 2006 is the cheapest and simplest route.

If the company has more than a few thousand pounds of retained profit sitting on the balance sheet, a Members' Voluntary Liquidation (MVL) is usually more tax-efficient. If the company has debts it cannot pay, which is rare for a dormant entity but does happen, you need a Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation (CVL) instead.

Our advice is always the same: do not leave a dormant company sitting on the register indefinitely "just in case." The annual filing obligations continue, the risk of strike-off by the Registrar grows, and any assets held by a struck-off company vest in the Crown as bona vacantia.

We tell directors the question is not whether to close the company, but which closure route gives you the cleanest exit.

What Dormant Actually Means in UK Law

A company is dormant for Companies House purposes if it has had no "significant accounting transactions" during the relevant period.

Companies House defines this narrowly: payments for shares on incorporation, filing fees paid to Companies House, and late-filing penalties do not count as significant transactions. Anything else, including bank charges, a single invoice, or a director's loan movement, can take the company out of dormant status.

HMRC defines dormancy slightly differently for corporation tax purposes. A company can be dormant for HMRC without being dormant for Companies House, and vice versa.

In practice, if the company has filed dormant accounts (form AA02) and notified HMRC of dormancy, most directors we speak to assume the company has no ongoing obligations. That assumption is wrong. Confirmation statements must still be filed annually, directors must still be in place, and the company is still capable of being sued or pursued by creditors.

Why Solvency and Assets Matter More Than Dormancy

When we assess a dormant company for closure, we do not look at whether it is dormant. We look at whether it is solvent, and whether it has any value left on the balance sheet. These two factors decide the right route, and the answer flips completely depending on what we find.

Solvent, no material assets. Voluntary strike-off is usually the cleanest and cheapest option. The company is removed from the register for a £33 fee (£10 online) plus some director administration, and you save yourself the cost of a full liquidation.

Solvent, material retained profit. An MVL is almost always the better choice. The retained profit is distributed to shareholders as a capital distribution rather than a dividend, which usually qualifies for Business Asset Disposal Relief and a 14% capital gains tax rate on up to £1 million of lifetime gains. More on the mechanics in our guide to members' voluntary liquidation.

Insolvent. A CVL is the only proper route. Strike-off is not available if the company has outstanding creditors, and any director who strikes off an insolvent company risks objections from creditors, restoration, and personal liability claims.

Key Takeaway

Every week we speak to directors who have been sitting on a dormant shell for years because it felt like the safest thing to do. It almost never is. The filing obligations keep running, the bona vacantia trap stays open, and when the company is eventually struck off by the Registrar rather than by the director's own hand, there is no control over what remains inside it.

If the company has any retained profit, the MVL tax saving usually covers our fees and then some. If it has nothing, strike-off takes a few weeks and £10. Either way, acting is cheaper than leaving it.

Your Four Options at a Glance

  1. Leave it dormant. Cheapest ongoing, but requires annual filings, carries strike-off risk, and leaves assets exposed. Only sensible if you genuinely intend to revive the company within 12 to 18 months.
  2. Voluntary strike-off. Fast, cheap, and appropriate for a solvent company with no material assets and no creditors. Requires form DS01 and three months of no trading before you apply.

Keeping the Company Dormant: Obligations and Risks

If you decide to keep a company dormant rather than close it, you still carry obligations. Every year you must file a confirmation statement with Companies House (currently £34 online), file dormant company accounts (form AA02), and notify HMRC of any change in status.

You must maintain at least one director and keep the registered office address current. Miss any of these and Companies House can initiate strike-off action under Section 1000 of the Companies Act 2006, which puts any assets still in the company at risk of vesting in the Crown.

Our view is that keeping a company dormant is only worth the administrative effort if you have a concrete plan to use it again within the next year or two. Otherwise you are paying filing fees and carrying risk for no benefit.

Voluntary Strike-Off: The Low-Cost Route

Voluntary strike-off under Section 1003 is the route most directors reach for first. It is appropriate when the company meets all of the following: it has not traded or changed name in the last three months, it has no creditors, it has no assets (or negligible assets you are content to lose), and you have notified all relevant parties, including employees, creditors, shareholders, and HMRC.

The process is simple. You file form DS01, pay the fee, and the Registrar publishes a notice in the Gazette. After two months, if no objections are received, the company is struck off the register and ceases to exist.

We warn directors about two pitfalls. First, any bank balance left in the company at strike-off vests in the Crown as bona vacantia, and recovering it is slow and expensive. Second, HMRC can object to strike-off if they believe the company has outstanding liabilities, which blocks the process entirely.

Members' Voluntary Liquidation for Dormant Companies with Reserves

If the dormant company has retained profit sitting on the balance sheet (often because the owners parked cash there after a previous trading period), an MVL is almost always the better route than strike-off.

The reason is tax. Distributions from an MVL are treated as capital for the shareholders, which means they fall within the capital gains regime rather than the dividend regime. For most individuals, that means Business Asset Disposal Relief at 14% on gains up to £1 million lifetime, compared to dividend tax rates of up to 39.35%.

The rule of thumb we apply is: if the company has more than about £25,000 of retained profit, the MVL route usually saves more tax than the liquidator's fee. Below that threshold, strike-off is often simpler. We will run the numbers for any director at the first call so the decision is evidence-based, not guesswork.

Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation: When Dormant Still Means Insolvent

Occasionally we see dormant companies that are actually insolvent.

The company stopped trading years ago, but it still has unpaid creditors: an old director's loan balance owed to a former shareholder, a disputed HMRC assessment, an unresolved supplier dispute. In those cases, strike-off is not a legal option (Section 1003(3) prohibits strike-off where the company has creditors), and the right route is a CVL.

A CVL for a dormant company with modest debts is usually straightforward and affordable, because the liquidator's investigation has little to unpick.

Assets and the Bona Vacantia Trap

The most painful mistake we see is directors striking off a dormant company that still has assets. When a company is struck off, any assets (including bank balances, intellectual property, or unclaimed debtor balances) vest in the Crown as bona vacantia under Section 1012 of the Companies Act 2006.

The Treasury Solicitor (or in Cornwall and Lancaster, the respective Duchy) then holds the assets. Recovering them requires restoring the company to the register, which is slow, expensive, and not guaranteed.

Our rule for directors is blunt: if the company has any bank balance, any outstanding invoices, any stock, or any intellectual property at the point of closure, you need an MVL, not a strike-off. The cost of getting this wrong is almost always higher than the cost of getting it right.

Pre-Closure Compliance Checklist

  • Confirm the company genuinely has no outstanding creditors (including disputed or historic debts).
  • Close the company bank account and distribute any remaining cash to shareholders before strike-off.
  • File all outstanding Companies House returns and HMRC corporation tax returns up to the closure date.
  • Notify HMRC of the intention to close and request deregistration for corporation tax, VAT, and PAYE where applicable.
  • Write to any creditor, shareholder, employee, or pension trustee who might object to strike-off (required under Section 1006).
  • Keep company records for at least seven years after closure, as required by the Companies Act.

Common Misconceptions We Hear

"A dormant company has no obligations." It still has annual filing obligations and can still be sued. Directors are still liable for keeping records and filings up to date.

"If I do nothing, Companies House will strike it off eventually." Yes, but on the Registrar's timetable, not yours, and any assets in the company at that point vest in the Crown. That is rarely what you want.

"An MVL is only for trading companies." Not true. An MVL is a solvent-liquidation procedure available to any company with distributable reserves, including dormant shells holding retained profit. We run MVLs for dormant companies every month.

"I can strike off and then restore if I need to recover assets." You can, but restoration takes months, costs thousands, and requires court or administrative application. It is not a backup plan.
