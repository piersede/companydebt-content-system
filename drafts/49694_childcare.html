Nurseries are caught between the cost of caring for children safely and the funding that pays for much of it. Staff-to-child ratios set the wage bill and cannot be cut, wages and energy have risen, and a large share of income comes from government-funded hours that often do not cover the true cost of a place.

That gap is unforgiving. A nursery can be full, with a waiting list, and still lose money, because the funded rate for a child may fall short of what it costs to look after them, and covering staff absence with agency workers costs far more than the budget allows.

Owners often keep going by working in the rooms themselves, deferring maintenance or putting their own money in, none of which closes a structural gap between the cost of a place and the funding that pays for it.

The important question is not whether the rooms are full. It is whether the business can pay its debts as they fall due, and whether the fees and funding genuinely cover the cost of safe, compliant care.

This guide explains the warning signs of insolvency in a nursery or childcare business, the options open to directors, the duty of continuity you owe to the children in your care, and what may happen to the setting, staff and any debts you have personally guaranteed.

Settings classed under education add more again, so no single official figure captures childcare, and the closure numbers tell you more than the insolvency totals do on their own.

The pattern behind them is not a run of poor operators, and in the cases we handle it almost never is. Providers that fail describe the same structural problem you will recognise from your own accounts: the cost of delivering care rising far faster than the funded rate that now covers most of the income.

When Alpha Nurseries closed 21 settings in a single December 2023 liquidation, taking 250 jobs and 875 places with it, it was not a small or careless operator. In childcare, being full is not the same as being solvent, and that is the hardest lesson in the trade.

What's Driving Childcare Insolvencies

None of these will be new to you. What is worth being clear about is how they translate into insolvency, because a setting can look busy and well regarded right up to the point it can no longer meet its payroll. Three pressures do most of the work.

Funded rates below the cost of a place

The core of it is a rate that does not cover delivery. The National Day Nurseries Association put staffing costs up around 15% against a funded-rate rise of 4.6%, so a funded hour can be a loss before you have counted rent or food.

By 2025, up to 80% of a typical setting's income came from those government-paid hours, which means most of your revenue is now fixed by someone else at a number you cannot negotiate.

The expansion to younger children, in place from September 2025, tightened this rather than easing it. The cross-subsidy that used to make the model work, charging private-paying parents enough to cover the funded shortfall, only works while there are private hours to charge for.

As funded hours crowd them out, a full nursery can still run at a structural loss, and in our experience it is this quiet, place-by-place loss, rather than any single event, that eventually empties the account.

Payroll you cannot cut without breaking ratios

Staffing is most of your cost and, because of ratios, the part you can least reduce. Rises in the National Living Wage feed straight through, and from April 2025 higher employer National Insurance, with a lower starting threshold, landed on every setting's payroll on top.

The Early Years Alliance put the combined effect at around £18,600 a year for the average provider, with 40% of those it surveyed calling permanent closure a likely outcome. The relaxed ratio for two-year-olds was meant to help, but most owners held their staffing to protect quality and safety, and were right to.

That leaves very little you can trim before you are into a deficit, which is the corner we see owners backed into: cut staff and you break ratios or lose your Ofsted grade, hold them and you run out of cash.

Occupancy dips and the council payment lag

On margins this thin, small movements matter. A few empty places from a lower birth rate or a local shift, and fixed costs you cannot recover start to bite. You know this already; the point is that the tolerance for it has almost disappeared.

Then there is the timing, which is where a viable setting most often runs short on cash. With most income now arriving as funded-hours payments, you are exposed to how councils pay, and many that pay monthly release the money only in the third or fourth week.

Meeting a week-one payroll from your own resources while the council's money is still a fortnight away is exactly where an undercapitalised setting runs short, even when the annual figures would otherwise balance.

Warning Signs a Nursery Is in Trouble

These are the points at which a squeezed but solvent setting starts to become an insolvent one. You will see most of them in your own management figures before anyone outside does, and that early window is exactly when advice is worth the most and costs you the least.

  • Staff costs above 70% of income. Once payroll takes more than about seven pounds in every ten you bill, there is little left for rent, food and utilities, and the setting has almost no margin for error.
  • Almost entirely funded hours. If nearly all your children take only funded hours, with little private or wrap-around income, you are likely below cost on every place.
  • Spending deposits and advance fees. Using parent deposits or fees paid ahead to cover this month's wages. That is money owed against care you have not yet provided.
  • HMRC, rent or rates slipping. Missing VAT or PAYE, or falling behind on rent, is a clear sign cash has run short. A winding-up petition from HMRC means the position is already serious.
  • An Ofsted problem. A downgrade prompts parents to withdraw children, and a suspension stops you operating and earning, usually at the worst possible moment.

If more than one is true, the business may already be unable to pay its debts as they fall due, which is the test the law applies. That is the point to put the figures in front of a licensed insolvency practitioner, while the options are open and before a creditor or the regulator forces the timing.

Most of the owners we help wish they had made that call a term earlier, when there was still room to move.

Why Rescuing a Nursery Is Harder Than It Looks

This is the part that catches owners out, and the question we are asked most once a setting is in difficulty. A nursery is not just a lease and a set of assets that can be sold on; it is a registered, safeguarding-critical service for named children, and that turns what would be a straightforward rescue in another sector into something that has to be sequenced with real care.

Ofsted registration does not transfer

The registration is the sticking point. It cannot pass from one legal entity to another, so a buyer taking your setting on has to obtain their own, with the usual checks, and cannot lawfully operate until it is granted. Running an unregistered setting is a criminal offence, so there is no working around it.

That makes the clean pre-pack that works elsewhere far harder here. In practice the insolvent company usually cannot surrender its own registration until the buyer's is in place, so the whole rescue turns on managing that overlap.

We have seen a viable sale come close to collapsing purely on the timing of two registrations, because get it wrong and continuity of care breaks, at which point a going-concern sale can fall into a closure.

Parents' money and the council's stake

Parents who have paid deposits or fees in advance rank as unsecured creditors if the company fails, and unsecured creditors rarely recover much. For a family who has handed over real money and now needs an alternative place at a week's notice, that is a hard, personal outcome, and it is one reason an orderly process matters so much more here than the balance sheet alone would suggest.

Your local-authority funded-place contracts add another thread. They usually cannot simply be assigned to a buyer without the council's agreement, and the authority carries its own duty to keep enrolled children in a place.

In the cases we handle we bring the local authority in early, to manage either a safe handover or an orderly wind-down for the families involved, rather than leaving them to find out when the doors do not open.

Your Options if a Nursery Can't Pay

Once the company cannot pay its debts as they fall due, your duties shift towards creditors, and taking on new deposits to cover old bills is exactly the kind of step that can rebound on you personally. None of the routes below is a defeat, and we talk owners through each of them every week. Taken early, while there is still a viable setting, they protect both the children and your own position.

  • Time to Pay arrangement. Where the setting is viable and the issue is a defined HMRC arrears, a Time to Pay arrangement spreads VAT or PAYE over a manageable period and keeps you trading.
  • Company Voluntary Arrangement. A CVA can compromise historic debt, such as legacy pandemic borrowing, and let a viable nursery trade on, provided the underlying funding-versus-cost position can be made to work.
  • Administration and pre-pack sale. Administration holds off creditor action and can transfer a setting to a solvent operator, with staff moving across under TUPE, as long as the Ofsted registration handover is planned properly.
  • Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation. Where a nursery cannot be saved, a CVL closes it in an orderly way, works with the local authority on the children's placements, and deals with creditors including HMRC, though not any personal guarantees you have given.

In childcare the sequencing matters more than the label on the process. Safeguarding duties do not pause because a company is insolvent, so a practitioner who understands the sector will plan the Ofsted and local-authority steps alongside the insolvency itself, rather than leaving continuity of care to chance.

If you have signed a personal guarantee on the premises, which is common in this trade, say so at the first meeting, because it is the thread that most often ties your own home to the setting, and it changes the advice.
