Event businesses take much of their money long before the event happens and spend it just as early, which is what makes them fragile. Deposits and sponsor advances come in months ahead, suppliers and venues are paid before the date, and a single cancellation or a wet weekend can turn a full calendar into an empty account.

That can be deceptive. An events company can be fully booked for the season and already be insolvent, because the deposits for the summer are paying the invoices from the spring, and the money for events not yet delivered has already gone.

Owners often keep going by using the next booking's deposit to cover the last one's costs, stretching suppliers or leaning on a sponsor to pay early, none of which addresses a business that has already spent income it has not yet earned.

The important question is not how full the calendar is. It is whether the business can pay its debts as they fall due, and whether each event is still profitable once advance liabilities and the risk of cancellation are counted.

This guide explains the warning signs of insolvency in an events business, the options open to directors, and what may happen to customer deposits, supplier contracts and any debts you have personally guaranteed.

The named failures tell you more than the totals do, because the same story keeps repeating.

Most are creditors' voluntary liquidations and administrations rather than rescues, and the pattern we see is nearly always the same three things arriving together: a weekend that underperformed, costs that had climbed faster than ticket prices, and pandemic borrowing still sitting on the books.

An events company can be fully booked and already insolvent, because every date in the diary is money owed to a customer, not money the business has made.

What's Driving Events Insolvencies

You will know these pressures better than any guide can tell you. What turns them into an insolvency is the way they land together on a business that commits its costs before it knows what it will take. Three do most of the damage.

Income you commit before you have earned it

The economics of an event are front-loaded, and that is the fault line the rest sits on. Venue, staging, security and headline fees fall due long before the gates open, and you meet them from deposits and advance tickets for the event itself. The moment that money goes on last season's overheads, or on securing the next booking, the business is running on income it has not yet earned.

In the cases we handle, this is almost always where it started, long before anyone used the word insolvency. A single underperforming event then has nothing behind it, because the reserve that should have absorbed it was quietly spent forward months ago.

When Solfest's directors closed their long-running Cumbrian festival in early 2026, they pointed to exactly this: a difficult event meeting costs that were already committed.

Costs that have outrun ticket prices

Production, staging, security, insurance and staffing have all climbed hard, and cautious audiences have capped how far you can lift ticket prices to keep pace. The gap between the two is absorbed at your margin, which in events was thin to begin with. You will have felt it most in the line items you cannot negotiate: the security ratio the licence demands, the insurance premium, the crew rate.

Weather turns that gap dangerous. An outdoor event carries its full cost base whether the sky holds or not, and commercial cancellation cover is expensive and often narrower than organisers assume until they claim.

A washed-out bank holiday can take the surplus the whole year was built on, which is why so many of the 2025 and 2026 festival failures followed one bad weekend rather than a slow decline. Refreshing the forecast the week before an outdoor date, knowing what rides on it, is a particular kind of dread you will recognise.

Pandemic debt on a project balance sheet

Many operators are still repaying the facilities that carried them through the shutdowns. Servicing that debt now, on top of everything that has since got dearer, takes the cash you would otherwise hold back for a weak event, so the buffer is gone before the season even tests it.

Events companies also tend to hold very little in the way of tangible assets, so there is almost no balance sheet to fall back on when a date disappoints. In our experience it is this thin base, far more than any one cancelled show, that decides whether a hard year is survivable. The businesses that go under are rarely the worst-run; they are the ones that ran out of room.

Warning Signs an Events Business Is in Trouble

These are the signals that a cashflow squeeze is turning into a solvency problem. You will usually notice the first of them well before anyone outside the business does, and that early window is exactly when advice is worth the most and costs you the least.

  • You are funding this event from the next one. Using deposits and advance sales for a future date to settle suppliers from the last one. It arrives as cash, but it is money owed against a service you have still to deliver.
  • Suppliers moving you to pro-forma. Venues, staging or AV firms asking for payment up front rather than on terms. They have usually read the risk before you have said a word.
  • HMRC going unpaid. Missing VAT or PAYE, or holding it back to bridge a gap. A statutory demand or winding-up petition from HMRC means the position is already serious.
  • Margin gone from won work. Pitches you have secured no longer carry a real surplus once rising production and staffing costs are in, so a full order book stops meaning a solvent one.
  • No reserve behind a bad date. A single cancelled or rained-off event leaving you unable to meet commitments, because there was nothing set aside to absorb it.

If more than one of these is true, the business may already be unable to pay its debts as they fall due, which is the legal test that matters. That is the moment to put the numbers in front of a licensed insolvency practitioner, while the decisions are still yours to make and before a creditor takes them out of your hands.

Most of the directors we help wish they had made that call a season earlier.

Bringing someone in early is not an admission of failure; it is usually what keeps the choice on your side of the table.

Customer Money and Cancelled Events: What Makes Events Different

One feature shapes an events insolvency more than any other: you are holding a great deal of other people's money for events that have not yet happened. How that money is treated, and what your contracts say when a date is pulled, usually decides whether any rescue is possible at all.

It is the part that catches organisers out, and the question we are asked most often once a company is already in difficulty, so it is worth being precise about it.

The deposits and ticket money you are holding

Advance income is normal in events and you handle it every day. Ticket sales, exhibitor fees, sponsorship and client deposits all land before you provide anything. The danger is only that this money quietly funds today's costs rather than being held against the obligation it represents, and it comes due precisely when trade is at its weakest.

If the company then fails, those customers and clients rank as unsecured creditors, and unsecured creditors usually recover little or nothing. Ticket-holders and exhibitors who paid by credit card may be protected under section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act, and card payments can sometimes be recovered by chargeback, but a deposit paid by bank transfer generally is not.

It is worth being honest with yourself about that early, because the families and small exhibitors left out of pocket are the part of an events failure that does the lasting reputational damage, long after the numbers are settled.

Contracts that end the moment you stop

Venue and supplier contracts commonly let the other side walk the moment you become insolvent, and an event needs all of them holding at once. That is why a rescue almost always has to run through administration rather than an informal wind-down: it is the only way to move the value in the brand, the diary or the client relationships to a buyer before those contracts fall away.

Noble Events shows both the promise and the limit of that. The agency was bought out of administration in 2025, then placed back into administration barely a year later. A sale can preserve a name, a team and a client list, but it cannot fix a model that does not cover its costs.

That is the judgement to make honestly and early, because a rescue that only buys another loss-making year helps no one, least of all you.

Your Options if an Events Company Can't Pay

Once the company cannot pay its debts as they fall due, your duties shift: the interests of creditors start to come first, and taking further deposits for events you may not be able to deliver can deepen your own exposure rather than ease it. None of the routes below is a defeat, and we walk directors through each of them every week.

Each is simply a way of taking control back, and each works better the earlier it is used, while there is still a business worth protecting.

  • Time to Pay arrangement. Where the business is viable and the problem is defined HMRC arrears, a Time to Pay arrangement spreads VAT or PAYE over a manageable period and keeps you trading through it.
  • Company Voluntary Arrangement. A CVA lets a viable operator restructure debt and trade on, though it needs the support of creditors holding 75% by value, so it depends on being able to show a realistic forward book.
  • Administration and pre-pack sale. Administration holds off creditor action and, where value sits in the brand, the diary or the team, can achieve a going-concern sale rather than a total loss.
  • Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation. Where the business cannot be saved, a CVL closes it in an orderly way, realises the assets and deals with creditors including HMRC, though not any personal guarantees you have given.

In practice the deciding factor is where the real value sits. Where it lives in a recognised brand or a strong recurring event, a pre-pack can carry it across even when the wider company cannot be kept open. Where the model itself no longer covers its costs, an orderly closure protects you better than another season spent hoping a good weekend will finally settle the last one.

If you have signed a personal guarantee on a venue or a facility, say so at the first meeting, because it changes the advice, and it is the thing that most often keeps directors awake.
