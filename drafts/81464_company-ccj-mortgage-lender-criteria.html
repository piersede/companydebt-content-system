A County Court Judgment (CCJ) against your limited company does not go on your personal credit file. That is the general rule and it holds for most directors, so if that is the question you came with, the answer is the reassuring one.

If you are reading this late, with an application already moving and a letter on the desk you did not want, the other thing worth knowing is that this situation is common. Directors in it get mortgages. What costs them one is rarely the judgment. It is answering a question about the judgment wrongly on the application form.

The One Lender That Can Stop You

Barclays will decline the application where you hold more than 15% of a limited company and that company has unpaid judgments totalling more than £5,000. Of the 14 lenders we checked, it is the only one whose published criteria turn a company judgment into a refusal rather than a question.

The stated way out is documentary evidence that the dispute has been settled. Note the wording: Barclays asks about the dispute being settled, not simply the money being paid.

Paying the judgment and having it marked satisfied is the obvious evidence and is the right first step. It may not be the whole of what Barclays wants, so ask your broker exactly what that lender will accept before you assume a receipt is enough.

If you cannot pay it, that narrows your lender list rather than ending the search. Halifax and BM Solutions ask about company judgments and still lend.

If the company genuinely cannot pay the judgment at all, that is the more urgent problem. An unpaid judgment is one of the things a creditor can use to apply to court to close the company down, which is what a winding-up petition is.

A petition is a serious step, but it is not the end of the company and it is not the usual outcome of one unpaid judgment. Most creditors want paying rather than closing you down, and there are more options at this stage than most directors expect. It wants dealing with this week, though, rather than after the mortgage.

What to Check Tonight and Ask Tomorrow

First, make sure what you are holding is actually a judgment. A County Court Judgment says so on it, names the court that issued it, carries a case number, states an amount the court has ordered to be paid, and gives a date to pay by.

A demand from a creditor is not a judgment. Nor is a solicitor's letter threatening court, nor a claim form, which is the start of a case rather than the end of one. If you have a claim form and the time to respond has not run out, you do not yet have a CCJ and you have more control than this page assumes.

Look the judgment up tonight without waiting for anyone. It sits on the Register of Judgments, Orders and Fines, searchable at trustonline.org.uk for £6 for a single register search. What you want is the amount, the date, and whether it is marked satisfied, which means paid and recorded as paid.

Search on the company name together with its registered office address, and use the address that applied when the judgment was entered. There is no company-number box, so the address is what separates your company from a similarly named one, and a wrong address can return a clean result that means nothing.

Treat a clean result with care if the judgment is only days old. Court records reach the register through an update process rather than instantly, so a very recent entry may not be showing yet. TrustOnline lets you repeat the same search later at a discount, which is worth doing before you rely on a nil result.

Check your exact shareholding at Companies House, which is free. That number decides whether the Barclays rule touches you at all, and directors are often wrong about it where shares have been issued or transferred since incorporation.

Nobody has to approve the search and nobody is told you made it. You do not need the company's permission or anyone else's, and the search leaves no footprint on any credit record.

Then dig out anything you signed for the company's borrowing. A personal guarantee makes you personally liable for that specific company debt, and it is the one document that breaks the rule everything above rests on.

Overdrafts, asset finance and commercial leases are where guarantees usually hide. If one covers the debt behind the judgment, your position is different from the one described here, and our guide to company CCJs and director liability deals with it.

You may also be able to answer the lender question yourself tonight. The illustration your broker sent you names the lender it was produced for, so check that paperwork before you wait for a phone call.

Tomorrow, ask your broker which lender they are putting you to, and whether that lender's credit-history question covers judgments against a business you own. Your broker may have to go back to the lender for an answer, so allow a few days rather than an afternoon.

What the 14 Lenders Publish on Company CCJs

Three of the 14 lenders we checked ask about company judgments directly. A clean personal credit file is no answer to that question, because it asks about your company rather than about you.

The other eleven do not say either way. With Halifax, BM Solutions and Barclays the rule can be established before you apply. With the eleven there is nothing published to establish, and the question can still surface later, after you have paid for a valuation and agreed a purchase.

If the form asks the question, answer it, whatever the published criteria do or do not say. The criteria tell you what a lender has committed to in writing. The form is the thing you sign.

Which Lenders Ask About a Company CCJ

Two of the three sit inside the same banking group, so you are looking at two independent positions rather than three.

Halifax and BM Solutions are both Lloyds Banking Group and use near-identical wording. If you are self-employed and a CCJ or default has been registered against your business in the last six years, you must answer yes to the credit-history question on the mortgage application.

Neither threshold helps you get out of it. There is no minimum value, so a £300 judgment counts, and no shareholding floor, so it applies whether you own 5% of the company or all of it.

Declaring it to either lender rarely sinks an application. The answer feeds the credit score, and an underwriter, the person at the lender who decides your case, comes back for the background. Plenty of directors get mortgages by that route. A wrong answer does the damage, not the judgment.

Barclays is the outlier, and its rule is set out above. Where the shareholding and value thresholds are both met, the criteria place the case in the list that must be declined rather than the list an underwriter weighs up.

Where the Company CCJ Thresholds Cluster

Six of the 14 draw their line for your personal judgments at £500 or below: HSBC, Virgin Money, Skipton, Coventry, Leeds and The Mortgage Works. Barclays is tighter still at £200. Look-back windows bunch at three and six years.

One unpaid £600 invoice that reached judgment can put you outside six of these lenders on its own, whatever you earn. Personal judgments are being used as a character test rather than a solvency test, so the size of the debt barely enters into it.

Barclays sets its company-judgment threshold at £5,000, twenty-five times its own £200 personal limit.

Lenders evidently accept that a trading company collects the odd dispute, and that a small judgment against one proves very little. The rule is aimed somewhere specific: a company sued for real money that has not paid.

The one firm that has examined this question in public did not conclude company judgments were irrelevant. It concluded they start to matter at £5,000, which is worth weighing against the comfort of eleven lenders saying nothing.

Residential Versus Buy-to-Let Is Not the Clean Split It Looks

Company-judgment rules are not confined to buy-to-let lending, which is where almost everyone assumes they live. We checked this twice, because the first answer surprised us.

Barclays carries the 15% and £5,000 criterion in near-identical wording in both books. In the residential criteria it sits under the heading "Adverse credit history, Residential", inside the list of things that must be declined, with no buy-to-let wording anywhere near it.

Lloyds Banking Group reaches the same place by a different route, asking the business question in residential lending through Halifax and in buy-to-let lending through BM Solutions, its limited company range included.

If you are buying a home to live in and assumed none of this touched you, drop that assumption now.

We had it wrong ourselves until we looked. An earlier version of our main CCJ guide called the Barclays rule buy-to-let only. It is not, and we corrected the page the day we checked.

Satisfied Judgments and What Lenders Do With Them

Paying the judgment moves the dial a long way with one lender and not at all with another, which is worth knowing before the money goes out.

With Barclays it is decisive. The criterion is keyed to outstanding judgments, and evidence of a settled dispute is the stated route back into consideration. Clearing the debt there is the difference between a decline and an application.

With the Lloyds group question it changes nothing about the disclosure. The six-year question makes no distinction between satisfied and unsatisfied, so a company CCJ you paid in year two must still be declared in year five. Paying it will help once you have declared it. It does not remove the obligation.

By the time a director calls us, this is the detail they have most often got wrong. They clear the judgment, take that as the end of the matter, and answer the declaration honestly but incorrectly.

What a Clean Personal Credit File Does and Does Not Prove

Credit reference data is organised around people, and your company is a separate legal person. Its judgment is filed against the company and never reaches your file, so a lender searching your record finds nothing, correctly.

The application form is organised around something else entirely: what you know. Skipton's limit applies to "all CCJs on the credit report", which a company judgment is not. Halifax does not mention your credit report at all. It asks what has been registered against your business.

So the lender runs one test and asks a second, and a company CCJ sits in the space between them. The search cannot find it. Only you can answer it, in a box, under a declaration you sign.

An inaccurate declaration is the more serious problem, and it is not the one most directors are worrying about. A company CCJ is a fact you can explain to an underwriter. A mortgage obtained on a wrong answer outlives the judgment by years, and it is not a trade we would advise anyone to make.

What to Do Before You Apply, With a Company CCJ Outstanding

The fear here is rarely the paperwork. It is that a bad year in the business quietly costs you the house, and that nobody says so until the decline lands. Every one of these rules can be checked before an application goes in, which is what makes that outcome largely avoidable.

Ask your broker to pull the lender's criteria before you get anywhere near a declaration. The question is narrow enough to answer in one call: does the credit-history question cover judgments against a business you own, and over what period.

Look up the company's own position at Companies House and on the Register of Judgments before a lender does. Know the amount, the date, and whether it is marked satisfied.

If the judgment is unpaid and you can afford to pay it, pay it. That removes the Barclays trigger completely and improves every conversation that follows.

And if this judgment is one of several, or the company genuinely cannot pay it, the mortgage is the smaller problem. An unsatisfied judgment is one of the strongest pieces of evidence a creditor can take to a winding-up petition. Sort the company out first. The house depends on it more than it depends on the application.

FAQs on Company CCJs and Mortgage Applications

My application has already gone in and I think I answered the question wrongly

Tell your broker this week rather than waiting to see whether it surfaces. Correcting an answer on a live application, before an offer is issued, is an ordinary amendment and brokers handle them routinely.

Left alone, it gets worse rather than quieter. A lender that finds an inaccurate answer can withdraw the offer, and where it concludes the answer was deliberately false to obtain the loan, it can treat that as mortgage fraud and report it. Correcting it yourself, before anyone finds it, is a different conversation entirely.

We are insolvency practitioners rather than mortgage advisers, so the amendment is a conversation for your broker or the lender directly. What we can say is that we have never seen this improve by being left alone.

Will a mortgage lender find my company's CCJ if it does not ask?

Not through a standard personal credit search, because the judgment sits against the company and not against you. The Register of Judgments, Orders and Fines is public, so it is findable by anyone who looks, but a routine mortgage credit check on an individual does not look there.

Where a lender assesses business accounts as part of a self-employed application, the judgment or the dispute behind it can surface in the figures instead.

Discovery is the wrong thing to plan around in any case. The exposure that matters is the declaration, because an inaccurate answer is a problem long after a judgment has dropped off the register.

Does it matter how much of the company I own?

With Barclays, yes. Its criterion applies where you hold more than a 15% shareholding, so a small minority stake falls outside it.

With the Lloyds Banking Group question there is no shareholding floor at all. What matters there is self-employment and whether the judgment was registered against the business.

So a 10% stake sits outside the Barclays criterion and squarely inside the Lloyds one. Shareholding is not a general defence, it is a Barclays-specific threshold.

I am a sole trader, not a limited company. Is my position the same?

No, and your position is considerably worse. A sole trader is not a separate legal person, so a judgment against the business is a judgment against you. It goes on your personal credit file and every threshold in the table above applies to it directly.

Limited company protection is the only reason the distinction exists at all. A sole trader has no separate entity to stand behind.

Should I just apply to one of the eleven with no published rule?

Choosing a lender whose criteria you meet is ordinary, sensible mortgage broking. Choosing one specifically to dodge a question you would have to answer yes to is a different thing, and a broker is the right person to steer that.

The bigger flaw in the plan is that you would be relying on an absence. Those eleven lenders have not said they will not ask. They have simply not published a rule either way, and nothing stops an underwriter raising it on a self-employed case. Criteria also change, as this page's monthly re-check exists to catch.

The judgment against my company is wrong. What can I do before I apply?

A judgment entered in error or without the company having a chance to defend can sometimes be set aside, which is a matter for a litigation solicitor rather than for us. If it is set aside, it comes off the register.

Paying it in full within one calendar month of judgment also removes it from the register entirely. Miss that window and paying only marks it satisfied, and it stays visible for six years.

One calendar month is the deadline worth acting on, and it is the one most often missed. If the debt is genuinely owed and you can afford it, pay inside the month and the judgment disappears rather than sitting there marked satisfied for six years.
