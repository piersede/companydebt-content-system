The right way to close a limited company depends on whether it is solvent, meaning whether it can still pay what it owes in full and on time.

If it can, you have two options. The first is a simple strike-off, a short form filed with Companies House that dissolves the company. The second is a Members' Voluntary Liquidation, where members means the shareholders, used when the company holds cash or other assets to distribute.

If it cannot, you also have two options, but the window is narrower. You can start a Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation yourself. Or you can wait, in which case a creditor petitions the court and the court makes a winding-up order. That is compulsory liquidation, and control of the company passes out of your hands.

Two alternatives sit outside closing altogether. You can keep the company dormant if you may trade again. And you can look at rescue procedures if the business itself is viable but under pressure.

Which route is open to you depends first on whether the company is solvent, so start with the two-minute insolvency test. That is the first thing we establish when a director calls us, because the answer rules out two of the four closure routes immediately. This guide covers England, Wales and Scotland; Northern Ireland uses different procedures via the Department for the Economy NI.

Compare Your Closure Options

Between them, six positions cover just about every company that closes. These notes say what tips each choice, and the table at the end of the section lines them up side by side.

Solvent, clean, nothing much left in it. A voluntary strike-off on form DS01 is the standard route, and at £13 online it is the cheapest thing on this page. The word to watch is clean: one unresolved liability and the route stops working.

Solvent, with cash or assets to distribute. A Members' Voluntary Liquidation (MVL) passes the surplus to shareholders as a capital gain rather than as income, which for a higher-rate taxpayer is normally the cheaper treatment. The tax section below sets out how each is taxed.

Insolvent, and you want to act before a creditor forces the issue. A Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation (CVL) is the route directors take themselves. What it buys is the timing and the choice of practitioner, because a liquidator takes control of the company either way, whether you appoint them or the court does.

Insolvent, and a creditor has already obtained a winding-up order. The outcome is compulsory liquidation, run by the Official Receiver. Nobody chooses it. It is what a creditor's petition produces when no voluntary process has been started first.

Company may trade again. Keep it dormant. A dormant company holds on to its name, its registration and its filing history, none of which a fresh incorporation gives you back.

Business itself is viable, but under pressure. Look at rescue first: Time to Pay (HMRC's tax-instalment plan), a Company Voluntary Arrangement, administration, or refinancing. Closing a company that could have traded on is the one decision on this page you cannot undo.

Solvency is the first question because it settles the other two. A solvent company picks between strike-off and an MVL on tax grounds, at its own pace.

An insolvent company is picking between a liquidation it controls and one a creditor imposes, and a winding-up petition takes the first of those off the table.

In practice, most of the directors we speak to have already decided they want the company closed. What they have not settled is which of these six positions they are in, and that is the part that changes the answer.

First Check Whether Your Company Is Solvent

UK insolvency law applies two tests. If your company fails either one it is insolvent, which rules out strike-off in practice (because the debts are unresolved) and rules out an MVL in law (because the solvency declaration would be false).

  • Cash-flow test. Can your company pay debts as they fall due from the resources realistically available to it? Overdue trade invoices, HMRC arrears, or missed loan repayments may indicate the test has failed. The central question is whether the company can actually meet the debts on time.
  • Balance-sheet test. Do total liabilities (including prospective and contingent liabilities) exceed total assets? Contingent liabilities on the company's balance sheet include disputed claims, warranties, dilapidations, lease termination costs, and any guarantees given by the company. A personal guarantee you have signed is separate personal exposure; it does not itself put the company balance sheet under water.

Debt on its own does not make your company insolvent. A profitable company can carry a loan balance and still pass both tests comfortably. What matters is whether it can settle everything owed, in full, when it is due.

The liabilities that catch directors out are the ones with no invoice attached to them.

A dilapidations claim on premises you handed back years ago can be quantified long after you stopped thinking about the lease. The liability dates back to the lease itself, so a company can turn out to have been insolvent on the day the DS01 was filed.

What Counts as a Company Debt and What Does Not

  • Underlying borrowing sits with the company. The loan your company took out is the company's liability. A personal guarantee attached to it is a separate personal obligation on you as guarantor.
  • An overdrawn director's loan account is normally a company asset. You owe the balance back to the company. In a liquidation the liquidator will usually pursue it.
  • A director's loan account in credit is normally a company liability. You are a creditor and rank with other unsecured creditors under insolvency law.
  • Disputed and contingent company claims cannot be ignored. A lease dilapidations claim not yet quantified, a warranty on work delivered last year, an HMRC investigation still open; all of these count.

If any of that leaves doubt, work through the Company Debt insolvency test before you choose a route. It takes about five minutes, and nobody follows it up unless you ask us to.

How to Close a Solvent Limited Company

A solvent company closes through voluntary strike-off if there is little or nothing left in it, or through a Members' Voluntary Liquidation if it holds cash, property or other assets to distribute.

Your choice turns on how much surplus is left, how the shareholders want to be taxed on it, and how much unfinished business the company still carries.

Voluntary Strike-Off Using Form DS01

Strike-off is the administrative route. You file form DS01 with Companies House, wait through the two-month Gazette notice, and if no one objects the company is dissolved. No insolvency practitioner, no meetings, no ongoing case.

For a company that has genuinely settled everything, strike-off is the correct route. Where debts are still outstanding it is the wrong one, and the reason is mechanical rather than moral.

Dissolution does not extinguish a debt. It removes the debtor from the register, and a creditor who later wants paid can apply to have the company restored.

So will anyone actually chase it? Where HMRC is owed, usually yes, and the objection tends to arrive before the company is struck off at all.

Every condition below has to hold on the day you file, and the seven-day notification duty carries a penalty of its own.

  • The application must be signed by a majority of directors.
  • The current fee is £13 online or £18 by paper (verified 3 August 2026 against the Companies House fee schedule).
  • In the three months before you apply, the company must not have traded, carried on business, changed its name, or disposed of property or rights beyond permitted winding-down activity.
  • Voluntary strike-off is not available while the company is in specified insolvency proceedings or a qualifying creditor arrangement.
  • Within seven days of filing, you must send copies of the application to all members, creditors, employees, pension trustees or managers, and any director who did not sign. Miss that and you risk a fine and possible prosecution.
  • The dissolution is advertised in the Gazette, and any interested party can object. Objections may be raised at any point during that window or later while the application is still active; timing depends on when the creditor identifies the outstanding liability.

Assets left in the company can pass to the Crown

Anything your company still owns at dissolution (cash, unpaid tax refunds, domain names, IP, vehicles, receivables, contractual rights) can vest in the Crown as bona vacantia. Recovering it means paying to restore the company. Full detail is in the "cash and assets" section below; the operational rule is simple: clear the balance sheet before the DS01 goes in.

Eligibility is rarely what stops a strike-off. A creditor objection is, and HMRC is the creditor most likely to raise one.

HMRC objects routinely where Corporation Tax, VAT or PAYE arrears are outstanding. Your strike-off is suspended, the company stays on the register, and HMRC now knows you tried to close it owing tax.

Directors who reach us at that point usually want to know whether the objection can be lifted. It can, but only by dealing with the liability that prompted it. Our strike-off and dissolution guide sets out the objections process and the safer alternatives where debts are still open.

Members' Voluntary Liquidation

A Members' Voluntary Liquidation is a formal solvent liquidation. In the MVLs we handle, you and your co-directors sign a declaration confirming, after a full inquiry, that the company can pay all debts plus statutory interest within 12 months.

A licensed liquidator is appointed, all creditors are paid in full, and the surplus is distributed to shareholders as a capital receipt rather than as dividends. An MVL is not a way to escape debt: creditors are paid before shareholders see anything.

The tax attraction is Business Asset Disposal Relief. From 6 April 2026, qualifying disposals are charged at 18% (up from 14% in the prior tax year, and 10% before that), against a lifetime limit of £1 million of qualifying gains per person, less any BADR already used on earlier disposals.

The relief is conditional: you must have held qualifying shares and been an officer or employee for at least two years, and the company must have been a trading company or the holding company of a trading group throughout that period.

Compared with the standard capital gains rate of 24% for higher-rate taxpayers, BADR saves 6 percentage points on qualifying gains up to the lifetime cap. For companies with material retained profit, that saving can significantly outweigh the MVL professional fee.

The trade-off is time. An MVL finishes when HMRC gives clearance, which sits outside the liquidator's control and outside yours.

Two warnings sit alongside the tax attraction. First, the anti-phoenix rules under the HMRC company taxation manual (CTM36300 series) can reclassify a liquidation distribution as an income distribution, taxed at income tax rates, if you carry on the same or a similar trade within two years.

Second, the declaration of solvency carries criminal liability, and the directors sign it personally. Knowingly making a false declaration is an offence.

If the company then cannot pay within the stated period, the inquiry you made before signing becomes the issue. Civil contribution claims, disqualification or other consequences may follow, depending on what you knew, what you checked, and the loss caused. Strike-Off or MVL: How to Decide

Directors often ask whether an MVL becomes compulsory once retained profit passes £25,000. It does not.

The £25,000 figure is a specific HMRC tax rule under CTM36220: capital treatment for pre-strike-off distributions is only available where total distributions do not exceed £25,000. It is a tax threshold, not a break-even point for your route choice.

Your right decision usually turns on six things:

  • how much cash or assets sit in the company;
  • your marginal tax rate as shareholder;
  • whether you qualify for BADR at all;
  • how much of your BADR lifetime allowance is still available;
  • whether you plan to carry on the same or a similar trade;
  • the professional fee quoted for the MVL.

A shareholder who used £900,000 of the BADR lifetime allowance on an earlier exit has £100,000 of it left. Most of the next distribution is then taxed at the standard rate, and the MVL fee buys a good deal less than the headline saving suggests.

Take tax advice on the actual numbers wherever the retained balance runs materially above that £25,000 tax rule.

How to Close an Insolvent Limited Company

If your company cannot pay its debts in full and on time, your choice narrows. There are two closure outcomes, and only one of them is really a decision you get to make.

A Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation puts closure into a controlled legal framework, with a practitioner you have chosen. Compulsory liquidation is what arrives if a creditor acts and you have not.

Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation

A CVL is the standard route where an insolvent company decides to close before a creditor forces the issue.

In the cases we handle, the decision rarely follows the director's own reading of the accounts. It follows somebody else putting a figure on the position: the accountant finishing the year end, or a creditor setting out the arrears in writing.

Directors initiate the process and propose the licensed insolvency practitioner. Approval requires 75% by value of shareholders voting; the resolution is filed at Companies House within 15 days, and notice is placed in the Gazette within 14 days.

Once appointed, the liquidator takes control of the company and acts for the creditors. Your authority as director ends at appointment. From that point your job is to cooperate: hand over the records and answer questions about company transactions.

Company debts do not automatically become your debts in a CVL. Where the assets fall short, unpaid creditor claims stay against the company and are written off when it is dissolved.

Personal exposure arises only through the specific mechanisms set out in the liability table below: guarantees, overdrawn director loan accounts, conduct claims, or statutory liability notices.

Timing matters. Once a winding-up petition is advertised in the Gazette, control of the case starts moving away from you: banks tighten up, your IP choice narrows, and the Official Receiver is queued to take over. Our CVL guide covers the full process and the director-side protections.

What Happens If a Creditor Petitions First

Compulsory liquidation is a court-led outcome. A creditor (most often HMRC) presents a winding-up petition, the court hears it, and if granted a winding-up order is made and the Official Receiver takes over.

Directors regularly reach us at this stage without knowing a petition against their company has already been advertised. Nobody sends a copy. The Gazette notice is the public warning, and creditors and banks read it before you do.

The petitioner initially pays the court fee and Official Receiver's deposit; both may be recovered from company assets. Once the petition is advertised, banks that become aware of it often restrict the company account. Timing varies by bank and by what the bank knows; there is no automatic 24-hour freeze.

Once the order is made, the Official Receiver runs the case, investigates director conduct and submits a report to the Insolvency Service.

If a statutory demand or a winding-up petition has already arrived, you may still have time to start a CVL before the petition is heard. That path preserves your choice of IP and tends to protect the company's value. Our compulsory liquidation guide and winding-up petitions guide set out the timetable and defence options.

Could Your Company Be Rescued Instead?

Not every distressed company should close. The test is whether the trade itself still generates a surplus once the legacy debt is set aside.

Where it does, the debt is the problem rather than the business, and one of the rescue routes we work with may fit better than a CVL.

  • Time to Pay (TTP): HMRC's own instalment arrangement for tax arrears, used where the underlying trade is viable.
  • Company Voluntary Arrangement: a formal repayment agreement with your creditors, typically over three to five years, that keeps the company trading.
  • Administration: statutory rescue procedure, often used to sell the business as a going concern where the entity itself cannot be saved.
  • Refinancing: asset-based lending, invoice finance, director loan or equity injection to bridge a cashflow gap.

Every one of those routes needs somebody else to agree: HMRC to a Time to Pay arrangement, the creditor body to a CVA, a lender to refinancing.

So rescue turns on whether the trade can fund the arrangement out of what it actually earns. A plan resting on sales that have not happened yet does not get agreed. See our company rescue solutions hub for detail on each route.

The Pre-Closure Checklist

Whichever route you use, the operational work of closing your company runs on a similar sequence. This checklist covers a solvent strike-off; MVL and CVL cases follow the same principles under a licensed practitioner. Print it, tick off the boxes, keep the paper.

The two steps that cause the most trouble afterwards are the ones with no deadline attached: collecting what the company is owed, and closing the payroll properly. Nothing chases you for either, so both get left.

  1. Stop trading. Cease taking new orders and record the cessation date.
  2. Record the decision to close in a board minute.
  3. Identify every company debt and contingent company liability: HMRC, suppliers, lenders, landlords, disputed claims, dilapidations, warranties, any guarantees given by the company. Pay or resolve each one.
  4. Separately, list any personal guarantees you have signed or other personal exposures; those sit with you, not the company, and need their own resolution plan.
  5. Collect money owed to your company. Chase unpaid invoices before dissolution; after strike-off any residual debt vests in the Crown.
  6. Sell or transfer company assets at proper value. Domain names, IP, vehicles, plant, stock, contractual rights.
  7. Deal with employees: consult, terminate, pay accrued holiday, final wages, redundancy where owed.
  8. Deregister for VAT where appropriate. File the final VAT return.
  9. Submit final payroll (final FPS and EPS) to HMRC and close the PAYE scheme.
  10. Prepare final accounts and the final Company Tax Return.
  11. Pay outstanding Corporation Tax and any other taxes.
  12. Distribute remaining cash to shareholders correctly, respecting the £25,000 tax rule if a strike-off is planned.
  13. Close the company bank account only after all payments and refunds have cleared.
  14. Confirm the statutory three-month restrictions are satisfied.
  15. Obtain majority-director approval and file DS01 (£13 online, £18 paper).
  16. Within seven days of filing, notify members, creditors, employees, pension trustees or managers, and any non-signing directors.
  17. Monitor the Gazette notice and Companies House record; deal with any objections promptly.

What Happens to Company Cash and Other Assets

Anything your company still owns at dissolution can pass to the Crown as bona vacantia, meaning ownerless property. This applies to strike-off and to the tail end of any liquidation where an asset was missed.

The Treasury Solicitor administers Crown-held property in England and Wales; the King's and Lord Treasurer's Remembrancer handles Scottish estates. Recovering an asset means restoring the company, which is neither cheap nor fast.

In the cases we see, the assets left behind are the invisible ones: an unpaid invoice, or a tax refund nobody chased.

  • Cash and bank balances
  • Tax refunds not yet received (Corporation Tax, VAT, PAYE overpayments)
  • Money owed to your company by customers or third parties
  • Domain names and other intellectual property
  • Property, vehicles and equipment
  • Contractual rights and any unclaimed insurance proceeds

It bites hardest with a Corporation Tax refund that lands on HMRC's system a couple of months after dissolution. The refund becomes Crown property, the company no longer exists to receive it, and you paid the accountant to file the return that generated it.

Transfer, distribute, or claim before the DS01 goes in.

When Can You Become Personally Liable

Liability limited to unpaid share capital is a description of the shareholder's position. The director's position is governed separately, and being a director does not make you liable for company debt by itself. Personal exposure comes from the mechanisms in the liability table above.

The practical triggers, in short:

  • personal guarantees you have signed;
  • an overdrawn director's loan account;
  • wrongful trading, where the statutory test is met (no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvent liquidation, and no steps taken to minimise creditor loss);
  • misfeasance, where breach of duty caused identifiable loss;
  • specific statutory notices for PAYE or VAT (Personal Liability Notices, Joint and Several Liability Notices);
  • knowingly false declarations of solvency;
  • fraud, misuse or misrepresentation on a Bounce Back Loan (the scheme itself did not carry a personal guarantee).

Of that list, the two that come up most in the cases we handle are guarantees and loan accounts. Conduct claims are the ones directors worry about; a signature on a lease guarantee is the one that usually costs them money.

Where closure is unavoidable, acting promptly through a properly conducted CVL can prevent further creditor losses and show that the directors took responsible advice.

It does not clear liability for earlier conduct, for guarantees, or for a director loan balance. See what happens to directors in liquidation for the practical consequences by route.

Employees and Director Redundancy

Every closure route that follows a period of trading has an employee dimension. Even a solvent, planned strike-off carries the duty to consult employees, run a final payroll, and settle accrued holiday, final wages and any statutory notice pay.

We see this trip up otherwise clean strike-offs where the payroll close-down was rushed.

In an insolvent liquidation, employees are creditors of the company. Where the company cannot meet the sums owed, employees claim from the Redundancy Payments Service under the Employment Rights Act 1996; the IP files the required schedule as part of the liquidation.

Directors can be eligible to claim from the RPS too, and the payment often covers a real slice of the CVL cost. Eligibility is not automatic.

The test is genuine employee status, and no single factor decides it. A payroll record on its own does not establish employment, and its absence does not always defeat a claim. We ask about the contract, the payroll and the drawings at the first conversation, because those are what the claim turns on.

A written employment contract, PAYE payroll, regular hours, drawn salary and the ordinary marks of an employment relationship all count as evidence. Age, length of service and weekly pay then drive the amount actually owed.

The £751 statutory weekly cap and £22,530 statutory maximum from 6 April 2026 are theoretical ceilings, not expected payouts. Ask the IP to assess your position before you commit, and do not budget on an assumed RPS claim.

Can Your Company Stay Dormant Instead

Dormancy is the alternative to closure where your company might be reused. A dormant company has no significant accounting transactions. It keeps its name, registration, and history; you keep the ability to restart it later without setting up something new.

  • Notify HMRC that the company has become dormant for Corporation Tax purposes.
  • Once HMRC accepts dormancy, further Company Tax Returns are not normally required unless HMRC requests one or the company begins trading again.
  • Companies House filings still apply: annual accounts (dormant company accounts) and the confirmation statement remain due each year.
  • Keep the company bank account minimal or closed; transactions can lose the dormant status.

Dormancy carries a small annual filing cost and a modest amount of admin. Where a client tells us they intend to trade again within a couple of years, we steer them here rather than into a strike-off.

Where there is no realistic reuse in view, dormancy only delays the closure while the running cost continues. See our dormant companies guide for the operational rules.

Before any legal step, confirm whether your company is still solvent. Do not sign a declaration of solvency, file a DS01, or pass a winding-up resolution before you have that basic point settled.

  4. Work through the pre-closure checklist above in order. Stop trading, settle debts, notify employees, deal with tax. The formal application is the last step, not the first.

Time is the real constraint. Where a director calls us in the week a winding-up petition arrives, a CVL is usually still possible.

By the morning of the hearing the decision has moved to the court, and that option has generally gone. So if a petition against your company has been advertised, the useful call is today's.

Be clear about what closing achieves, though. It deals with the company's debts, and it ends the drain of running a business that cannot pay its way. It does not touch a guarantee you signed, a balance you owe back on a director's loan account, or conduct already in the past, and no practitioner, ourselves included, can make those disappear.
