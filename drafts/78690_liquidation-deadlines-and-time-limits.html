Liquidation runs on statutory deadlines, and most of them cannot be extended.

If nothing has started yet, have the company's position assessed before you fix a resolution date. That date drives every deadline on this page, and it is the last one you fully control.

Several of those deadlines fall due before a liquidator is appointed, which means they fall on you. The liquidator does not arrive and absorb the paperwork.

Late compliance is recorded as a conduct matter. One deadline, ignored without reasonable excuse, is a criminal offence.

One more carries no date at all: the wrongful trading clock, which starts at the point insolvent liquidation became unavoidable and may already be running.

Quick Answer on Liquidation Deadlines and Time Limits

Which deadlines bite depends on where you are standing today, so start there.

Nothing has started yet. No statutory clock is running except the wrongful trading one below, and you still choose the start date.

Before fixing that date, we would want the latest management accounts and a current creditor list. Without those, nobody can tell you honestly how much time you have.

The resolution has passed. Four obligations now run concurrently inside a single 14 days: notice to creditors, the statement of affairs, the Gazette advertisement, and the decision procedure, which is the process letting creditors confirm or replace the liquidator you nominated. A Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation is sequenced tightly and does not flex.

A statement of affairs is the sworn list of the company's assets and liabilities. You sign it, and its accuracy is your responsibility.

A petition has been served. In a compulsory liquidation the calendar belongs to the court. The hearing date is fixed for you, and the Official Receiver, a civil servant from the Insolvency Service, becomes liquidator the moment an order is made.

CVL Liquidation Deadlines: The Voluntary Liquidation Timeline

Every deadline in a Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation is measured from one date, so the date you fix matters more than anything that follows it.

Day 0: winding-up resolution. The shareholders pass a special resolution, by 75% majority, to wind the company up voluntarily.

Within 14 days: notice to creditors. You must give notice of the resolution to all known creditors and invite them to a decision procedure. The notice must enclose the statement of affairs or say where creditors can inspect it.

Missing that window breaches your obligations under the Insolvency Act 1986. In the cases we handle it is the notice, not the resolution, that slips.

By the business day before the decision date: statement of affairs. It lists every asset, every liability, every creditor and every secured charge, and it is verified by a statement of truth, meaning a signed declaration that the contents are true as far as you know.

It is not an estimate. It is a sworn document, and inaccuracies carry personal consequences.

On paper that leaves you a fortnight to compile it. In practice the rules set only three business days as the minimum gap after notice goes out, so the decision date can land almost immediately and you need the statement ready before you pass the resolution.

Within 14 days: decision procedure. Creditors get the chance to appoint their own liquidator, by virtual meeting or deemed consent. Under rule 6.14 the decision date must fall at least three business days after notice goes out, and no later than 14 days after the resolution.

If creditors do not respond in time, the liquidator you nominated is confirmed. Our liquidation documents checklist sets out what you need in place before that point.

Within 14 days: Gazette notice. Separately from all of the above, section 85 requires the resolution to be advertised in the Gazette. Banks monitor it, so this is frequently how your own bank learns what has happened.

Within 15 days of appointment: Companies House. The liquidator files notice of appointment on Form 600. Late filing goes on the liquidator's record and reflects on the conduct of the case.

Within 28 days of appointment: creditors and contributories. The liquidator sends formal notice of the appointment to every creditor and contributory.

Timeline Reality

The 14-day window can close in three business days

Rule 6.14 sets 14 days as the outer limit for the creditors' decision date, but only three business days as the minimum gap after notice goes out. A decision procedure convened at the earliest lawful moment leaves barely three days to finalise a sworn statement of affairs.

The 14 days is a ceiling, not an allowance. Directors who plan against the ceiling rather than the floor are the ones who end up signing a statement of affairs they have not properly checked.

Compulsory Liquidation Deadlines: The Court-Driven Timeline

In a compulsory liquidation the timetable stops being negotiable, because a creditor and the court are setting it between them.

Service of the petition. The petitioning creditor serves at the company's registered office. There is no fixed 21-day service period here.

The 21 days you may have in mind belongs to a statutory demand, which is a different document at an earlier stage. Confuse the two and you lose time you cannot get back.

7 business days after service: advertisement. The petition is advertised in the London Gazette, at least 7 business days after service and at least 7 business days before the hearing.

That advertisement is what sets your real deadline. Once it appears, your bank will almost certainly freeze the company's accounts, automatically, often within hours, because banks watch the Gazette.

Nobody is obliged to warn you first. The advertisement is the notice, and it is aimed at your creditors.

The freeze does more immediate damage than the petition. In our casework payroll is the first thing to stop, and it gets grim within days.

Hearing date: the winding-up order. If the court grants it, the Official Receiver becomes liquidator. Every later deadline is calculated from the date of the order, and any disposition of company property after it is void unless the court approves.

Statement of affairs: 21 days from the notice, where one is required. The Official Receiver may demand a verified statement of affairs. It is not automatic on every case.

Where it is demanded, section 131 gives you 21 days beginning with the day after you receive the notice. Failing to comply without reasonable excuse is a criminal offence, the Official Receiver can ask the court to compel you, and non-cooperation weighs heavily in any later disqualification proceedings.

If 21 days is genuinely too short, say so in writing before it expires. Asking for more time in advance is a different conversation from explaining a default afterwards.

What Most Directors Miss

The section 131 clock starts at the notice, not the winding-up order

Section 131 gives 21 days beginning with the day after the notice requiring a statement of affairs is received. It does not run from the date of the winding-up order, which is the date most directors have written in the diary.

This is the deadline we most often see counted from the wrong document, and the consequence of getting it wrong is criminal rather than administrative. Check which date the notice bears before counting anything.

Post-Liquidation Deadlines: From Appointment to Dissolution

After appointment the deadlines shift onto the liquidator, with one exception that still matters if you are a creditor.

Annual progress reports. The liquidator reports to creditors and Companies House at least once a year, covering what has been realised, what has been distributed, and what remains.

Final account and dissolution. The liquidator sends a final account to creditors and files it at Companies House. There is no longer any requirement to call a final meeting; the 2016 Rules replaced that with the account and report.

The company is dissolved automatically three months after the registrar registers that account, under section 201. That three-month tail cannot be shortened, which is why the full liquidation timeline always runs past the point the work actually finishes.

Dissolution removes the company from the register permanently. Our guide to what happens after liquidation covers what follows for you personally.

Creditor claims. A creditor must submit proof of debt to share in a distribution. No single statutory deadline governs this, but the liquidator sets a last date for proving in writing, and claims arriving after a distribution has been made are not paid from it.

If you are a creditor, file your proof as soon as the liquidator's notice arrives. A valid debt proved late and no debt at all produce the same dividend, which is nothing.

The Wrongful Trading Deadline: The One Not Written in Statute

Every deadline above has a date attached. The one creating the most personal exposure does not: it is the moment you knew, or ought to have known, that insolvent liquidation could not be avoided.

From that point, section 214 requires you to take every step a reasonably diligent person would take to minimise loss to creditors. Carry on trading past it without taking those steps and you face a wrongful trading claim, which can end in an order to contribute to the company's debts out of your own money.

We cannot pinpoint the exact date that duty crystallised for your company. The honest question is whether that date has already passed.

If it has, acting now beats acting next month. A liquidator reviewing your conduct looks at the gap between the trigger date and the date you took advice, and a short gap is far easier to defend than a long one.

It is the first thing we ask about when we take a case on, because the answer shapes everything after it.

Within hours. Once the petition is advertised in the London Gazette, banks that monitor the Gazette, which is most of them, freeze the company's accounts as a precaution.
