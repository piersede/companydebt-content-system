Liquidation runs on statutory deadlines, and most of them are not negotiable. Miss one and you face personal liability, regulatory action, or a creditor who moves before you are ready.

The timetable is measured in days, not weeks.

In a Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation, the gap between passing the winding-up resolution and the creditors' decision date can be as little as three business days. Filing deadlines at Companies House and the Insolvency Service are fixed by law, and the liquidator's reporting obligations start the moment they are appointed.

Late filing is treated as a conduct matter, and it stays on the record.

By the time a director calls us, the working assumption is usually that there are a few weeks to get organised. Far more often, there are days.

Quick Answer on Liquidation Deadlines and Time Limits

In a Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation, the critical early deadlines cluster in the first 14 days. You must pass the winding-up resolution, notify creditors, deliver a statement of affairs, and hold (or initiate) a decision procedure, which is the process that lets creditors confirm or replace the liquidator you have chosen.

In a compulsory liquidation, you do not control the timetable at all. The court sets the date and the Official Receiver takes over from day one.

If you are still weeks away from acting, the deadline that matters most is not written in any statute. It is the point at which you knew, or should have known, that insolvent liquidation was unavoidable. Every day of trading beyond that point increases your personal exposure to a wrongful trading claim, which can end in an order to contribute to the company's debts from your own money.

If you have not started, none of the statutory deadlines below have begun to run. The wrongful trading date is the exception, and it does not wait for you to act.

So the practical first move is not a filing. It is getting the company's position assessed by a licensed insolvency practitioner, because the resolution date sets every other deadline on this page, and while you are still acting voluntarily you choose when that date falls.

CVL Liquidation Deadlines: The Voluntary Liquidation Timeline

A Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation follows a tightly sequenced set of statutory deadlines. The process does not drift along at your pace. Once you pass the resolution, the clock is running and the obligations are personal.

Directors who come to us mid-process often assume the liquidator picks all of this up on appointment. Several of the early duties are the directors' own, and they fall due before the liquidator is confirmed.

Day 0: Winding-up resolution. The shareholders pass a special resolution, by 75% majority, to wind up the company voluntarily. Every subsequent deadline is measured from that date.

Within 14 days: Notice to creditors. You must give notice of the resolution to all known creditors and invite them to a decision procedure. The notice must include a copy of the statement of affairs or tell creditors where they can inspect it.

Missing that window breaches your obligations under the Insolvency Act 1986. In the cases we handle, this is the deadline that slips, and it slips because the notice is a director's job rather than the liquidator's.

By the business day before the decision date: Statement of affairs. The directors must deliver a statement of affairs, verified by a statement of truth, listing every asset, every liability, every creditor and every secured charge.

It is not an estimate. It is a sworn statement, and inaccuracies can carry personal consequences.

The decision date can fall anywhere inside the 14 days, so in practice this one arrives sooner than the headline window suggests. Prepare it before you pass the resolution, not after.

Within 14 days: Decision procedure. Creditors must be given the opportunity to appoint their own liquidator, by virtual meeting or deemed consent. Under rule 6.14 the decision date must fall at least three business days after notice goes out, and no later than 14 days after the resolution.

If creditors do not respond within the decision period, the directors' nominated liquidator is confirmed. Our liquidation documents checklist sets out what has to be in place before that point.

Within 14 days: Gazette notice. Separately from all of the above, section 85 requires notice of the winding-up resolution to be advertised in the Gazette within 14 days of it being passed. Banks monitor the Gazette, so that is often how yours finds out.

Within 15 days of appointment: Notice to Companies House. The liquidator must file notice of their appointment using Form 600. Late filing goes on the liquidator's record and reflects on the conduct of the liquidation.

Within 28 days of appointment: Notice to creditors and contributories. The liquidator must send formal notice of the appointment to every creditor and contributory of the company.

Compulsory Liquidation Deadlines: The Court-Driven Timeline

In compulsory liquidation the timetable is not yours to set. A creditor petitions the court, the court fixes a hearing date, and if the petition succeeds the winding-up order takes effect immediately. From service onwards you are working to someone else's schedule.

Service of the petition. The petitioning creditor serves the petition on the company at its registered office. There is no fixed 21-day service period here. The 21 days directors often have in mind belongs to a statutory demand, which is a different document altogether.

What sets the minimum gap is the advertising rule below. Once served, you have a limited window to pay the debt, reach a settlement, or prepare to oppose the petition in court. In practical terms that window closes at the advertisement date rather than the hearing date, for the reason set out below.

7 business days after service: Advertisement. The petition must be advertised in the London Gazette at least 7 business days after service on the company, and at least 7 business days before the hearing.

Once it is gazetted, your bank will almost certainly freeze the company's accounts. The freeze is automatic, because banks monitor the Gazette, and it can land within hours of publication.

The freeze does more immediate damage than the petition itself. In our casework, payroll is the first thing to stop.

Hearing date: Winding-up order. If the court grants the order, the Official Receiver becomes the liquidator. Every subsequent deadline is calculated from the date of the order, and any disposition of company property after it is void unless the court approves it.

Statement of affairs: 21 days from the notice, if one is required. The Official Receiver may require a verified statement of affairs, but it is not automatic on every case. Where they do require one, section 131 of the Insolvency Act 1986 gives you 21 days beginning with the day after you receive the notice.

We most often see this one counted from the wrong date. Do not count 21 days from the winding-up order itself. The clock runs from the notice.

Failing to comply without reasonable excuse is a criminal offence. The Official Receiver can also apply to the court to compel you, and non-cooperation is treated as a serious conduct matter in any later disqualification proceedings.

Post-Liquidation Deadlines: From Appointment to Dissolution

Once the liquidator is in place, the case moves through asset realisation, investigation, distribution and final reporting. The deadlines below are the ones that still affect you after appointment.

Annual progress reports. The liquidator must report to creditors and Companies House at least once a year, setting out what has been realised, what has been distributed, and what work remains.

Final account and dissolution. When the liquidator has finished, they send a final account to creditors and file it at Companies House. There is no longer any requirement to call a final meeting of creditors; the 2016 Rules replaced that with the final account and report.

The company is then dissolved automatically three months after the registrar registers that account, under section 201 of the Insolvency Act 1986. That three-month tail is fixed and cannot be shortened, which is why the full liquidation timeline always runs past the date the work actually stops.

Dissolution removes the company from the register permanently. Our guide to what happens after liquidation covers what follows for you personally.

Creditor claims deadline. Creditors must submit proof of debt to share in any distribution. There is no single statutory deadline, but the liquidator sets one in writing, and claims that arrive after a distribution has been made are not paid from it.

If you are a creditor, file your proof early. Late filing is, in our experience, the most common reason a creditor with a perfectly good debt ends up with nothing.

The assumption behind it is nearly always the same: that there would be months to gather the paperwork.

The Wrongful Trading Deadline: The One Not Written in Statute

Every statutory deadline listed above is measurable and documented. The one that creates the most personal exposure is not: the moment when you knew, or ought to have known, that your company could not avoid insolvent liquidation.

From that point, section 214 of the Insolvency Act 1986 requires you to take every step a reasonably diligent person would take to minimise the potential loss to creditors. If the liquidator concludes that you continued trading beyond that point without taking those steps, you face a wrongful trading claim and a potential personal contribution order.

We cannot pinpoint the exact date that duty crystallised for your company. The honest question is whether that date has already passed.

If it has, acting now is better than acting next month. The liquidator will look at the gap between the trigger date and the date you took advice, and a shorter gap is always easier to defend.

FAQs on Liquidation Deadlines and Time Limits

Within 14 days of the winding-up resolution, you must notify creditors, deliver a statement of affairs to the liquidator, and initiate a decision procedure for creditors to confirm or replace the nominated liquidator. These obligations run concurrently, so you need to have the statement of affairs prepared before you pass the resolution.

Late filing is treated as a compliance failure and can be referenced in conduct reports.

In compulsory liquidation, where the Official Receiver has required a statement of affairs, failing to submit it within 21 days of receiving that notice without reasonable excuse is a criminal offence. In voluntary liquidation, late filings at Companies House can bring penalties and adverse findings in the liquidator's report.

Within hours. Once the petition is advertised in the London Gazette, banks that monitor the Gazette (which is most of them) will freeze the company's accounts as a precautionary measure. This can happen the same day the advertisement appears. Directors who do not anticipate this often find themselves unable to pay staff or cover essential costs, which accelerates the crisis.

Paying one creditor ahead of others when the company is insolvent creates a preference under section 239 of the Insolvency Act 1986. The liquidator can claw back preferential payments made in the six months before liquidation (or two years if the creditor is a connected party, such as a director or family member).

If you are considering paying specific creditors before entering liquidation, take professional advice first.

There is no statutory maximum duration. Simple cases can complete within 12 months. Complex cases with disputed claims, ongoing investigations, or difficult-to-realise assets can take several years. The liquidator must file annual progress reports, and creditors can apply to the court if they believe the process is being unreasonably delayed.

There is no single statutory deadline. The liquidator sets a "last date for proving" in writing when they intend to declare a dividend. Claims submitted after a distribution has been made will not be paid from that distribution. If you are a creditor, submit your proof of debt as early as possible to avoid missing out.
