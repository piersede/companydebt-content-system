Liquidation runs on statutory deadlines, and most of them cannot be extended. A few can, but only if you ask before they expire rather than after.

If nothing has started yet, have the company's position assessed before you fix a resolution date, meaning the day the shareholders formally vote to wind the company up. That date drives every deadline on this page, and it is the one date you still choose.

Several of those deadlines fall due before a liquidator is appointed, so they sit with the directors rather than with the practitioner. Filing late is recorded as a conduct matter, which feeds into the report every liquidator has to file on the directors' conduct.

Most of those reports lead to nothing further. It is a pattern of failures, or clear misconduct, that draws action rather than one late form.

One duty carries no date at all. It is called wrongful trading: once insolvent liquidation can no longer be avoided, directors are expected to act to limit further loss to creditors.

A court can order a director who carries on regardless to contribute to the company's debts personally. The liquidator has to show the point was passed and that the directors did nothing about it, so claims of this kind are not routine.

Nothing triggers that duty and no one writes to you about it. The date is worked out afterwards from the management accounts, the cash position, and what the board knew at the time, which is why we ask for those documents first.

Quick Answer on Liquidation Deadlines and Time Limits

Which deadlines apply depends on where the company is now, so start there.

Nothing has started yet. No statutory clock is running except the wrongful trading one below, and you still choose the start date.

Before fixing that date, we would want the latest management accounts and a current creditor list. Without those, nobody can tell you how much time you have.

The resolution has passed. Four obligations now fall due within the same 14 days: notice to creditors, the statement of affairs, the public advertisement, and the decision procedure, which is the process letting creditors confirm or replace the liquidator you nominated.

A Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation runs to a tight sequence, and the dates are set by statute rather than by agreement.

A statement of affairs is the sworn list of the company's assets and liabilities. You sign it, and its accuracy is your responsibility.

A petition has been served. In a compulsory liquidation the court sets the timetable. The hearing date is fixed for you, and the Official Receiver, a civil servant from the Insolvency Service, becomes liquidator the moment an order is made.

CVL Liquidation Deadlines: The Voluntary Liquidation Timeline

Every deadline in a Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation is measured from one date, so the date you fix matters more than anything that follows it.

Day 0: winding-up resolution. The shareholders pass a special resolution, by a 75% majority of the votes cast, to wind the company up voluntarily.

Within 14 days: notice to creditors. You must give notice of the resolution to all known creditors and invite them to a decision procedure. The notice must enclose the statement of affairs or say where creditors can inspect it.

Missing that window breaches your obligations under the Insolvency Act 1986. It is the notice, rather than the resolution itself, that tends to be overlooked.

By the business day before the decision date: statement of affairs. It lists every asset, every liability, every creditor, and every secured charge, meaning any debt that carries a legal claim over a particular company asset.

It is verified by a statement of truth: a signed declaration that the contents are correct as far as you know.

It is a sworn document rather than an estimate, so the figures are worth taking time over.

On paper that leaves you a fortnight to compile it. In practice the rules set only three business days as the minimum gap after notice goes out, so the decision date can land almost immediately and you need the statement ready before you pass the resolution.

Within 14 days: decision procedure. Creditors get the chance to appoint their own liquidator, either at a virtual meeting or by deemed consent, which means the proposal goes through automatically unless enough of them object in time. Under rule 6.14 the decision date must fall at least three business days after notice goes out, and no later than 14 days after the resolution.

If creditors do not respond in time, the liquidator you nominated is confirmed. Our liquidation documents checklist sets out what you need in place before that point.

Within 14 days: Gazette notice. Separately from all of the above, section 85 requires the resolution to be advertised in the Gazette, the government's official journal of public notices. Banks monitor it, so this is frequently how your own bank learns what has happened.

Within 15 days of appointment: Companies House. The liquidator files notice of appointment on Form 600. Late filing goes on the liquidator's record and reflects on the conduct of the case.

Within 28 days of appointment: creditors and contributories. The liquidator sends formal notice of the appointment to every creditor, and to every contributory, meaning any shareholder who could still be asked to pay up on their shares.

Timeline Reality

The 14-day window can close in three business days

Rule 6.14 sets 14 days as the outer limit for the creditors' decision date, but only three business days as the minimum gap after notice goes out. A decision procedure convened at the earliest lawful moment leaves barely three days to finalise a sworn statement of affairs.

The 14 days is a ceiling rather than an allowance. Planning against the floor instead leaves time to check the statement of affairs properly before anyone signs it.

Compulsory Liquidation Deadlines: The Court-Driven Timeline

In a compulsory liquidation the timetable is set between a creditor and the court, so it is not open to negotiation.

Service of the petition. The petitioning creditor serves at the company's registered office. There is no fixed 21-day service period here.

The 21 days you may have in mind belongs to a statutory demand, which is a different document at an earlier stage. The two are easy to confuse, and the difference matters when you are working out how much time is left.

7 business days after service: advertisement. The petition is advertised in the London Gazette, at least 7 business days after service and at least 7 business days before the hearing.

In practice the advertisement date matters more than the hearing date. Once the notice appears, banks that monitor the Gazette will usually freeze the company's accounts, often within hours.

The advertisement is the formal notice, and it is directed at creditors rather than at the company, so there is no separate warning to the directors.

In the cases we handle, the freeze causes more immediate disruption than the petition itself, because payroll is normally the first payment to fail. There is real value in taking advice before the advertisement date rather than after it.

Hearing date: the winding-up order. If the court grants it, the Official Receiver becomes liquidator. Every later deadline is calculated from the date of the order, and any disposition of company property after it is void unless the court approves.

Statement of affairs: 21 days from the notice, where one is required. The Official Receiver may require a verified statement of affairs, though it is not automatic on every case.

Where one is required, section 131 gives you 21 days beginning with the day after you receive the notice.

Failing to comply without a reasonable excuse is a criminal offence, and the Official Receiver can ask the court to compel you. Co-operation, or the lack of it, is also taken into account in any later disqualification proceedings.

If 21 days is genuinely too short, say so in writing before it expires. Asking for more time in advance is a different conversation from explaining a default afterwards.

What Most Directors Miss

The section 131 clock starts at the notice, not the winding-up order

Section 131 gives 21 days beginning with the day after the notice requiring a statement of affairs is received. It does not run from the date of the winding-up order, which is the date most directors have written in the diary.

This is the deadline we most often see counted from the wrong document, and the consequences of missing it go beyond a late-filing penalty. Check which date the notice bears before counting from it.

Post-Liquidation Deadlines: From Appointment to Dissolution

After appointment the deadlines shift onto the liquidator, with one exception that still matters if you are a creditor.

Annual progress reports. The liquidator reports to creditors and Companies House at least once a year, covering what has been sold, what has been paid out, and what is left to finish.

Final account and dissolution. The liquidator sends a final account to creditors and files it at Companies House. There is no longer any requirement to call a final meeting; the 2016 Rules replaced that with the account and report.

The company is dissolved automatically three months after the registrar registers that account, under section 201. That three-month tail cannot be shortened, which is why the full liquidation timeline always runs past the point the work actually finishes.

Dissolution removes the company from the register permanently. Our guide to what happens after liquidation covers what follows for you personally.

Creditor claims. To get a share of whatever money the liquidator recovers, a creditor has to send in a proof of debt, which is the form setting out what they are owed and why.

No statutory deadline governs this, but the liquidator sets a cut-off date in writing. Claims arriving after the money has gone out are not paid from it.

If you are a creditor, send your claim in as soon as the liquidator's notice arrives. A late claim and no claim produce the same result, so the notice is worth acting on straight away.

The Wrongful Trading Deadline: The One Not Written in Statute

Every deadline above has a date attached. The one creating the most personal exposure does not: it is the moment you knew, or ought to have known, that insolvent liquidation could not be avoided.

From that point, section 214 requires you to take every step a reasonably diligent person would take to minimise loss to creditors. If trading continues past that point without those steps, the liquidator can bring a wrongful trading claim, which can end in an order to contribute to the company's debts personally.

We cannot pinpoint the exact date that duty crystallised for your company. The honest question is whether that date has already passed.

If it has, the sooner advice is taken the better the position looks. A liquidator will compare the date the duty arose with the date the directors took advice, and a short gap is far easier to explain than a long one.

It is the first thing we ask about when we take a case on, because the answer shapes everything after it.

FAQs on Liquidation Deadlines and Time Limits

Within 14 days of the winding-up resolution, you must notify creditors, deliver a statement of affairs, and initiate a decision procedure so creditors can confirm or replace the liquidator you nominated. Those obligations run concurrently rather than in sequence, so you need the statement of affairs prepared before you pass the resolution.

Late filing is treated as a compliance failure and can be referenced in conduct reports.

In a compulsory liquidation, where the Official Receiver has required a statement of affairs, failing to submit it within 21 days of receiving that notice without reasonable excuse is a criminal offence. In a voluntary liquidation, late filings at Companies House can bring penalties and adverse findings in the liquidator's report.

Within hours. Once the petition is advertised in the London Gazette, banks that monitor the Gazette, which is most of them, freeze the company's accounts as a precaution. This can happen on the day the advertisement appears. Directors who do not anticipate it often find they cannot pay staff or cover essential costs, which accelerates the crisis.

Paying one creditor ahead of others while the company is insolvent creates a preference under section 239 of the Insolvency Act 1986. The liquidator can claw back preferential payments made in the six months before liquidation, extending to two years where the creditor is a connected party such as a director or family member.

Take professional advice before you pay any specific creditor at this stage.

There is no statutory maximum duration. Simple cases can complete within 12 months. Complex cases involving disputed claims, ongoing investigations, or assets that are hard to sell can run for several years. The liquidator must file annual progress reports, and creditors can apply to the court if they believe the process is being unreasonably delayed.

There is no single statutory deadline. When the liquidator is ready to pay out whatever money has been recovered, they set a cut-off date in writing and tell creditors what it is. Anything that arrives after the money has been shared out is not paid from it, so send your claim in as soon as that notice reaches you.
