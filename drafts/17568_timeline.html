Ask how long it takes to liquidate a company and you will be told anything from ten days to five years. Both answers are given in good faith. They are answering different questions, and nobody tells you which one you asked.

Here is the short version. You can usually hand a company over within two to three weeks. The legal case behind it takes, on the government's own figures, closer to two years to finish.

In our experience advising directors, the question underneath is simpler than either: when does this stop being the thing you lie awake thinking about? For most people that comes far sooner than the paperwork does.

Quick Answer on How Long Liquidation Takes

For a voluntary liquidation, three separate clocks run. How quickly you can hand the company over. How long the liquidator's work goes on. And the fixed statutory wait before the company disappears from the register.

Compulsory liquidation breaks that pattern, because the opening stage runs on the court's timetable rather than yours.

We keep official research and our own practitioner estimates in separate columns below. They are not the same kind of fact, and most of the confusion on this topic comes from pages that mix them.

Read down the third column, never across the row. The two voluntary figures are measured over different stretches of the process, and comparing them directly is the mistake this page exists to stop you making.

The Honest Answer: Liquidation Is Slow, and That Is Not Your Liquidator's Fault

Most guides on this topic are careful not to say the obvious thing. The Insolvency Service was less coy. Reviewing 2,717 completed cases, its researchers wrote that on the time measure, the process "does not seem to be efficient".

Their comparison is worth sitting with. A UK administration runs for an initial twelve months, and going beyond that needs creditors or a court to agree. The equivalent Australian procedures, the same report notes, run between five months and a year.

The median UK creditors' voluntary liquidation in that sample took 712 days.

We are not blaming practitioners for that, and neither did the report. Progress reports, claim adjudication, conduct investigation and the statutory wait at the end are all built into the procedure. Your liquidator cannot opt out of them any more than you can.

But you should go in knowing the shape of it, because the alternative is checking Companies House every few months and assuming something has gone wrong.

Entering Liquidation Is Not the Same as Completing It

Nearly all the confusion here comes from one word doing two jobs. "Liquidation" means both the moment control passes to a liquidator and the legal case that grinds on afterwards.

  1. Clock one: getting into liquidation. From your first call to the liquidator taking office. In a voluntary liquidation this is the stage you have most control over, and the one that changes your daily life.
  2. Clock two: the active liquidation. Selling assets, agreeing creditor claims, investigating conduct, distributing whatever is left. The liquidator runs this. You are a witness to it, not a participant.
  3. Clock three: dissolution. A fixed statutory wait. Three months after the final account is registered at Companies House, the company stops existing.

Directors almost always ask about clock three and mean clock one.

So what does a two-year case actually cost you in practice? Usually less than you fear. Once the liquidator is appointed, company creditors deal with the liquidation through them, and for most directors that is the point the pressure drops.

You are also not generally barred from starting again while the case runs, though the rules on reusing a similar company name are strict and worth taking advice on before you register anything.

The real exceptions are personal guarantees and an overdrawn loan account. Neither is part of the liquidation, and neither waits politely for it to finish.

How Long a Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation Takes

A Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation, usually shortened to CVL, is the route most insolvent companies take. It is a closure you choose rather than one a creditor forces on you.

From First Instruction to the Liquidator Taking Office

10 to 21 days is the working range across cases handled by the licensed insolvency practitioners in our network, from your first instruction to the liquidator formally taking office. That is a practitioner estimate, not a statutory promise.

Where you land in that range comes down to something dull: whether the bookkeeping is current, whether you can produce a creditor list with real addresses, and whether somebody has to rebuild nine months of bank statements first.

You will see adverts promising liquidation in seven days. That is not quite a lie, but it is measuring clock one only, and it quietly assumes your paperwork is already immaculate. Ask any firm making that claim what happens on day eight.

Two statutory dates sit inside that window. Notice of the winding-up resolution must appear in the Gazette within 14 days. The creditors' decision on who acts as liquidator must also fall no later than 14 days after the resolution, with at least three business days' notice to creditors.

What Official Data Shows About CVL Duration

Most pages on this subject quote a range and leave you to trust it. There is better material available, and we would rather show you where our numbers come from.

Insolvency Service case data. Across 2,717 completed CVLs that began in 2017, the median was 712 days from the liquidator's appointment to dissolution. The quickest took 122 days. The slowest took 2,460, which is very nearly seven years.

Take out the fixed three-month wait at the end and the researchers put the live casework at a median of 620 days, around 1.7 years.

A smaller follow-up set of 400 cases starting in 2020 and 2021 came in at 436 days. Treat that one carefully: the report warns it only counts cases that had already finished, so it is weighted towards the quick ones.

Two things to hold on to. These are companies that failed in 2017, not the market you are trading in now. And a median means half of them ran longer.

What the evidence settles is the gap this page is about. A liquidation you can enter within weeks can still be open two years later, and that is normal rather than a sign of trouble.

The CVL Timeline From Appointment to Dissolution

  1. Weeks 1 to 2: preparation. Records gathered and the resolution drafted, alongside a statement of affairs, the formal statement of the company's assets and liabilities. Rush this and it resurfaces a year later.
  2. Day 0: winding-up resolution. Shareholders pass it, the creditors' decision procedure follows within 14 days, and the liquidator takes office.
  3. Months 1 to 12: realisation and investigation. Assets sold, book debts chased, director conduct reviewed. Property or a disputed loan account stretches this stage further than anything else.
  4. Months 12 to 24: claims agreed, any distribution paid. Progress reports go out annually. On the official data, most cases are still open at eighteen months.
  5. Closure: final account. The liquidator sends a final account to creditors and files it at Companies House. There is no final meeting; that requirement was abolished.
  6. Plus 3 months: dissolution. Three months after the registrar registers that account, the company is dissolved automatically.

If a page tells you your liquidator must call a final meeting of creditors, it is describing a process that ended in 2017. The 2016 Rules replaced it with a final account and report. Sadly that wording is still all over the internet, and on more than one solicitor's website.

How Long a Members' Voluntary Liquidation Takes

A Members' Voluntary Liquidation, or MVL, closes a solvent company and hands what is left to shareholders. Different work, different pressures, and unusually good evidence since March 2026.

What the Latest Official MVL Data Shows

Insolvency Service case data, March 2026. Across 2,309 cases from 2016 to 2024, the median from winding-up resolution to dissolution was 478 days.

The 2024 group was much faster: a median of 338 days, with 56% dissolved inside 12 months. The report links the improvement to HMRC dropping MVL tax clearance in December 2023.

For MVLs beginning in 2024, then, the median time to dissolution was 338 days, about 11 months. That is historical case data rather than a forecast for a new MVL, but it is far fresher than the CVL evidence and points the right way.

Unsurprisingly, that is the one change to insolvency practice in recent years that made things quicker rather than slower.

When Shareholders Actually Receive Their Money

Now the distinction that actually matters, and the reason the dissolution figure above can mislead. Getting your money and closing the company are not the same event, and the first usually comes a long way before the second.

In a straightforward MVL the liquidator can often make an interim distribution within weeks of appointment. We have seen shareholders funding a house purchase receive the bulk of the cash months before the company formally disappears.

Outstanding tax matters are the common reason the final distribution waits. HMRC stopped issuing formal MVL clearance letters in December 2023, so nobody is sitting on a certificate any more, but the liquidator must still settle the company's known and reasonably expected tax position.

An unfiled Corporation Tax return, an open enquiry or a VAT position nobody reconciled will each add months. Tidy those before you start and you are effectively buying yourself a shorter case.

How Long Compulsory Liquidation Takes

In a compulsory liquidation you are not driving. A creditor has petitioned the court, and the timetable belongs first to the court and then to the Official Receiver.

From Winding-Up Petition to Winding-Up Order

The rules set a floor rather than a fixed period. A winding-up petition cannot be advertised in the Gazette until at least seven business days after service, and must be advertised at least seven business days before the hearing.

That puts a minimum of roughly a fortnight of business days between service and hearing. Beyond that it depends on the court's listing, so treat any firm quoting you a precise figure with caution.

The 21 days directors usually have in mind belongs to a statutory demand, not the petition. We flag it because confusing the two costs people the window in which they still had options, and we have seen that happen more than once.

From the Order to Dissolution

The Official Receiver becomes liquidator the day the order is made and investigates director conduct as a matter of course. That investigation cannot be hurried along, whoever asks.

One correction worth making, because it is widely repeated. You do not automatically owe a statement of affairs within 21 days of the order. The Official Receiver may require one, and if they do, you have 21 days from the day after you receive that notice.

No official median exists for total compulsory liquidation duration. We looked, and could not find one. Anyone quoting you a figure is estimating, and in our experience these cases run longer than an equivalent CVL, because the investigation is more formal and relations with creditors are already hostile.

The overdrawn loan account is the one that catches people out.

Mention it at the first meeting and you are having a repayment conversation. Let the liquidator find it in month seven and you are having a conduct conversation instead.

That one is worth a great deal more than the few uncomfortable minutes it costs you.

Can You Speed Up a Liquidation?

Partly, and not as much as anyone selling you a fast liquidation implies. The statutory stages are fixed and the three-month dissolution wait is not negotiable by anybody.

What you can strip out is avoidable delay, and there is more of that than most directors expect when they first call us.

  • Get your records into one place before the first meeting. Our preparation guide lists what a liquidator will ask for.
  • Reply to requests within a few days, even when the answer is that you are still looking.
  • Raise the awkward items yourself: the loan account, the van sold to a brother-in-law last spring, the one supplier paid in full while the others waited.

None of this shortens the statutory timetable. It stops you lengthening it, which on the evidence is where most of the extra months come from.

How Much of It Will You Actually Be Involved In?

This is the question behind the question, and it deserves a straight answer. For most directors, involvement is heaviest in the opening weeks, while records are handed over and explanations given.

That stage is demanding. The statement of affairs, and questions about transactions you had half forgotten and now have to explain to somebody writing it down.

After appointment, contact is usually occasional, though your duty to cooperate runs until the case is finished. Personal guarantees, director loan accounts or conduct enquiries will ask more of you than that.

How Long Until the Company Leaves the Companies House Register

The company is dissolved three months after the liquidator's final account is registered at Companies House. Statute sets that period and nothing shortens it.

Which is why a timeline that stops when the work stops is only telling you half the story. Directors sometimes come to us eighteen months on, having seen the company still listed and assumed something has gone wrong. Usually nothing has. The tail is simply longer than anyone told them.

It is also why the claim that a no-asset company runs from instruction to dissolution in a few weeks cannot be right. A no-asset case genuinely shortens the middle clock. It cannot touch the last three months, and we corrected our own page to say so.

Methodology and Disclosure: How We Verified These Liquidation Timelines

This guide is written by the Company Debt editorial team for UK directors trying to plan around realistic timescales. The procedural detail follows England and Wales; Scotland and Northern Ireland run separate regimes with different timetables.

Statutory deadlines come from the Insolvency Act 1986 and the Insolvency (England and Wales) Rules 2016, cited by section below.

Those are: the 14-day Gazette requirement (s.85), the 14-day creditors' decision window and three business days' notice (rule 6.14), and the advertising window that sets the minimum gap between service of a petition and its hearing (rule 7.10).

Also the Official Receiver's discretion over a statement of affairs and the 21 days running from receipt of notice (s.131), and the three months from registration of the final account to dissolution (s.201).

Official case data comes from two Insolvency Service research reports. The CVL research report published 17 December 2024 covers 2,717 completed CVLs that began in England and Wales in 2017.

The Members' Voluntary Liquidations statistical review published in March 2026 covers 2,309 cases across cohorts from 2016 to 2024. Both describe past cohorts of completed cases. Neither forecasts how long a liquidation starting today will run.

Practitioner estimates are labelled as such throughout. The 10 to 21 day range from instruction to appointment reflects cases handled across our advisory referral network and is not drawn from a published dataset.
