Ask how long it takes to liquidate a company and you will get answers ranging from ten days to five years. Both can be true. They are measuring different things.

A voluntary liquidation can usually be arranged inside a fortnight. The liquidator's work on that same company often runs for well over a year. Dissolution at Companies House comes later still.

By the time a director calls us, the question underneath is usually simpler than the legal one: when does this stop being the thing you lie awake thinking about? That answer normally arrives long before the case file closes.

Quick Answer on How Long Liquidation Takes

Three clocks run on every liquidation. How fast you can hand the company over. How long the liquidator's work continues. And when the company is finally struck from the register.

Entering Liquidation Is Not the Same as Completing It

Most of the confusion around this question comes from a single word doing two jobs. "Liquidation" describes both the moment control passes to a liquidator and the years-long legal case that follows it.

Each clock measures something different, and only the first one is yours to influence.

  1. Clock one: getting into liquidation. From your first call to the liquidator taking office. This is the one you control, and the one that ends the daily creditor pressure.
  2. Clock two: the active liquidation. Selling assets, agreeing creditor claims, investigating conduct, paying out whatever is left. The liquidator runs this, not you.
  3. Clock three: dissolution. A fixed statutory tail. Three months after the final account is registered at Companies House, the company ceases to exist.

Directors almost always ask about clock three and mean clock one.

The day the liquidator is appointed, the company stops being yours to run. Creditors have to deal with them instead of you, and the calls to your mobile stop.

Your own involvement is heaviest at the start and thins out quickly after that. We cover what that looks like in practice further down.

How Long a Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation Takes

A Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation, usually shortened to CVL, is the route most insolvent companies take. It is a director-led closure rather than one forced on you by a creditor.

From First Instruction to the Liquidator Taking Office

10 to 21 days is the working range across the cases we handle, measured from your first instruction to the liquidator formally taking office. Treat that as our practitioner estimate, not a statutory promise.

What decides where you land in that range is dull and entirely within your control: whether the bookkeeping is current, whether you can produce a creditor list with real addresses, and whether anyone has to reconstruct nine months of bank statements first.

Two statutory dates sit inside that fortnight and are worth knowing. Once shareholders pass the winding-up resolution, notice must appear in the Gazette within 14 days. The creditors' decision on who acts as liquidator must also happen no later than 14 days after that resolution, with at least three business days' notice to creditors.

What Official Data Shows About CVL Duration

Most competitor pages quote a range drawn from experience. There is something better available, and it is worth being precise about what it does and does not tell you.

Insolvency Service case data. A study of 2,717 completed CVLs that began in 2017 found a median of 712 days from the liquidator's appointment to dissolution. The shortest ran 122 days, the longest 2,460.

Strip out the fixed three-month dissolution tail and the researchers put the live casework at a median of 620 days, roughly 1.7 years.

A smaller follow-up set of 400 cases starting in 2020 and 2021 showed a median of 436 days. The report warns that this sample only contains cases that had already finished, so it is skewed towards the quicker ones.

Two cautions before you plan around those numbers. They describe companies that failed in 2017, not the market you are trading in now.

And a median means half of all cases ran longer.

What the data does settle is the argument this page exists to resolve. A liquidation you can enter in a fortnight will still, on the evidence, take somewhere near two years to close.

The CVL Timeline From Appointment to Dissolution

  1. Weeks 1 to 2: preparation. Records gathered and the resolution drafted, alongside a statement of affairs, the formal statement of the company's assets and liabilities. Rushing this stage creates work that resurfaces a year later.
  2. Day 0: winding-up resolution. Shareholders pass it, the creditors' decision procedure follows within 14 days, and the liquidator takes office.
  3. Months 1 to 12: realisation and investigation. Assets sold, book debts chased, director conduct reviewed. Property or a disputed loan account stretches this stage more than anything else.
  4. Months 12 to 24: claims agreed and any distribution paid. Progress reports are filed annually. On the official data, most cases are still open at the eighteen-month mark.
  5. Closure: final account. The liquidator sends a final account to creditors and files it at Companies House. There is no final meeting; that requirement was abolished.
  6. Plus 3 months: dissolution. Three months after the registrar registers that account, the company is dissolved automatically.

If a page tells you the liquidator must call a final meeting of creditors, it is describing a process that ended in 2017. The 2016 Rules replaced it with a final account and report.

How Long a Members' Voluntary Liquidation Takes

A Members' Voluntary Liquidation, or MVL, closes a solvent company and distributes what is left to shareholders. Different work, different pressures, and a much better evidence base since March 2026.

What the Latest Official MVL Data Shows

Insolvency Service case data, March 2026. Across 2,309 cases from 2016 to 2024, the median time from the winding-up resolution to dissolution was 478 days.

The 2024 group was markedly faster: a median of 338 days, with 56% dissolved inside 12 months. The report links that improvement to HMRC stopping MVL tax clearance in December 2023.

That is a rare thing in insolvency guidance: an official figure recent enough to plan against. If you are closing a solvent company now, roughly eleven months to dissolution is a realistic expectation.

When Shareholders Actually Receive Their Money

Here is the distinction that matters to you, and it is the reason the dissolution figure above can be misleading. Money and dissolution are not the same event.

In a straightforward MVL the liquidator can often make an interim distribution within weeks of appointment. We have seen shareholders funding a house purchase get the bulk of the cash months before the company formally disappears.

What holds the rest back is almost always tax.

HMRC stopped issuing formal MVL clearance letters in December 2023, so nobody is waiting on a certificate any more. The liquidator must still settle the company's known and reasonably expected tax position before the final distribution goes out.

An outstanding Corporation Tax return, an open enquiry, or a VAT position nobody reconciled before the resolution will each add months. Get those tidy before you start and the timeline looks entirely different.

How Long Compulsory Liquidation Takes

In a compulsory liquidation you are not driving. A creditor has petitioned the court, and the timetable belongs to the court and then to the Official Receiver.

From Winding-Up Petition to Winding-Up Order

Expect roughly 8 to 10 weeks between a winding-up petition being issued and the hearing. The petition must be served at least 21 days before that date.

The damage in that window comes from the Gazette advertisement rather than the passage of time. Banks monitor it, and accounts are commonly frozen within days, taking the wages run down with them.

Directors lose this window regularly, and the reason is rarely dramatic. The petition arrived at the registered office while somebody was on holiday.

It is the most winnable stage of the whole process, and the one most often wasted.

From the Order to Dissolution

The Official Receiver becomes liquidator on the day the order is made and investigates director conduct as a matter of course. That investigation cannot be sped up.

One correction worth making, because it is widely repeated. You do not automatically owe a statement of affairs within 21 days of the order. The Official Receiver may require one, and if they do, you have 21 days from the day after you receive that notice.

No official median exists for total compulsory liquidation duration. Anyone quoting one is estimating. On the cases we see, it generally runs longer than an equivalent CVL, because the investigation is more formal and the creditor relationships are already hostile.

The overdrawn loan account is the one that catches people.

A director who mentions it at the first meeting is having a repayment conversation. A director whose liquidator finds it in month seven is having a conduct conversation, and those take far longer to close.

Can You Speed Up a Liquidation?

Partly. The statutory stages are fixed and the three-month dissolution period is not negotiable. What you can remove is avoidable delay, and there is more of it than most directors expect when they first call us.

  • Get your records into one place before the first meeting. Our preparation guide lists what a liquidator will ask for.
  • Reply to the liquidator's requests within a few days, even if the answer is that you are still looking.
  • Raise the awkward items yourself: the loan account, the van sold to a brother-in-law last spring, the one supplier who got paid in full while the others waited.

None of this shortens the statutory timetable. It stops you adding to it, which on the evidence is where most of the extra months come from.

How Much of It Will You Actually Be Involved In?

This is the question behind the question, and it deserves a direct answer. Your heavy involvement is measured in weeks, not years.

The first fortnight is intense: records, the statement of affairs, questions about transactions you had half forgotten and now have to explain to somebody who will write them down.

Then it drops away sharply. Most directors we act for have a handful of contacts with the liquidator across the whole of the following year.

Your duty to cooperate continues for the whole case, and it is a real duty. But cooperating with occasional requests is a different life from running a failing company, and directors consistently underestimate how quickly that shift happens.

Two things keep you involved longer: a personal guarantee, which is a separate matter from the liquidation and follows its own timetable, and an unresolved director's loan account.

How Long Until the Company Leaves the Companies House Register

The company is dissolved three months after the liquidator's final account is registered at Companies House. That period is set by statute and cannot be shortened.

It is why no honest liquidation timeline can end at the point the work finishes. Directors sometimes call us eighteen months on, having seen the company still listed and assumed something has gone wrong. Usually nothing has; the tail is simply longer than anyone told them.

This is also why claims that a simple no-asset case runs from instruction to dissolution in a few weeks do not hold up. The no-asset route can shorten the middle clock considerably. It cannot touch the last three months.

Methodology and Disclosure: How We Verified These Liquidation Timelines

This guide is written by the Company Debt editorial team for UK directors trying to plan around the realistic timescales for closing a limited company. The procedural detail describes England and Wales; Scotland and Northern Ireland run separate regimes with different timetables.

Statutory deadlines come from the Insolvency Act 1986 and the Insolvency (England and Wales) Rules 2016, cited by section in the list below.

Those are: the 14-day Gazette requirement (s.85), the 14-day creditors' decision window and three business days' notice (rule 6.14), the Official Receiver's discretion over a statement of affairs and the 21 days running from receipt of notice (s.131), and the three months from registration of the final account to dissolution (s.201).

Official case data comes from two Insolvency Service research reports. The CVL research report published 17 December 2024 covers 2,717 completed CVLs that began in England and Wales in 2017.

The Members' Voluntary Liquidations statistical review published in March 2026 covers 2,309 cases across cohorts from 2016 to 2024. Both describe past cohorts of completed cases. Neither forecasts how long a liquidation starting today will run.

Practitioner estimates are labelled as such on the page. The 10 to 21 day range from instruction to appointment reflects cases handled across our advisory referral network and is not drawn from a published dataset.
