Pub Closures: What’s Behind the Decline of British Pubs?

The UK had around 45,000 pubs in 2024, down from 60,800 in 2000 and 55,400 in 2010. That is the long-run picture, and it has not levelled off. In England and Wales, 366 pubs were lost for good in 2025, and the British Beer and Pub Association counted 161 closures across Britain in the first quarter of 2026 alone, almost two a day.

How to read the data

How Many Pubs Have Closed in the UK

Behind those numbers sits a hospitality sector under more financial pressure than any other part of the economy. This page pulls the current data together and separates three things that often get blurred into one headline: how many pubs exist, how many close for good, and how many pub companies become insolvent.

There is no single official dataset that counts every pub closure across the UK in one place. Different bodies measure different things, so the honest short answer uses three numbers, not one.

The long view

UK Pub Numbers Over Time

The long decline is the clearest part of the story. On the BBPA series quoted by the House of Commons Library, the UK has lost roughly a pub in four since the turn of the century.

This is a structural shift, not a blip. It runs through boom years and recessions alike, which is a useful corrective to any single-cause explanation. The pressures have changed over 25 years, but the direction has not.

Permanent closures

Permanent Pub Closures in England and Wales

The sharpest recent measure of pubs lost for good comes from the rating list. Ryan’s analysis of government data put permanent losses in England and Wales at 366 in 2025, taking the counted total from 38,989 to 38,623. Permanent loss here means demolition or conversion to another use, a tighter test than a pub simply going dark for a season.

The rating-list total also includes pubs that are vacant or being offered to let, so the counted stock and the permanent-loss figure are not the same number measured twice. They answer slightly different questions, which is why we show both rather than picking one.

Financial distress

Pub Insolvency and Financial Distress

Site closures and company failures are different systems, and reading one as the other is the most common mistake in coverage of this subject. The insolvency data tells you how much financial distress sits behind the closures.

Two points stand out. Accommodation and food service has had the highest company insolvency rate of any sector every year since 2015, peaking at 314 per 10,000 businesses in 2023.

And while the sector still opens more businesses than it loses, at 30,360 births against 26,195 deaths in 2024, the churn is brutal. The survival odds for a new venue are worse here than in most of the economy.

The cost stack

Why Pubs Are Closing

The measurable drivers matter more than the cultural commentary. What the current data shows is a cost stack that has risen sharply on several fronts at once, hitting labour-heavy, property-heavy, alcohol-selling businesses hardest of all.

The ONS Business Insights and Conditions Survey captures how widely these pressures are felt. In June 2026, 83% of accommodation and food service businesses said turnover was being affected by at least one challenge, with the cost of materials and the cost of labour tied at the top.

Rising staff costs

The National Living Wage rose 4.1% to £12.71 an hour from April 2026. From April 2025, employers also pay National Insurance at 15% rather than 13.8%, and from a much lower starting point of £5,000 rather than £9,100. If you run a pub with a dozen part-time staff, both changes land directly on your wage bill.

Business rates, tax and duty

At the 2026 revaluation in England, the rateable value of public houses and pub restaurants rose by an average of 30%, and by 70% for pubs with a lodge. That is a valuation change, not the final bill, which the multipliers and reliefs then adjust.

Eligible pubs get 15% relief in 2026/27, and the retail, hospitality and leisure multipliers are 38.2p for rateable values below £51,000 and 43p from £51,000 to £499,999. Even after relief, your rates remain one of the heaviest fixed costs a pub carries.

On top of rates, alcohol duty was uprated by 3.66% in line with RPI from 1 February 2026. There is a lower draught rate to protect on-trade sales: qualifying draught products between 3.5% and less than 8.5% ABV are charged at £19.45 per litre of alcohol, against a main beer rate of £22.58. Duty is a wholesale-level cost, but it feeds through to what a pint has to sell for.

The demand side

Are Pubs Closing Because People Drink Less

Demand has genuinely softened, and the evidence is stronger than the usual anecdotes about smartphones and streaming. NHS England found that 24% of adults had not drunk any alcohol in the previous 12 months in 2024, up from 19% in 2022.

Younger adults are leading the shift. People aged 16 to 24 are the least likely of any age group to drink weekly, at 30%. If your trade depends on a young weekend crowd, that is a structural change in the customer base, not a passing mood.

Affordability compounds the demand shift. On the ONS retail prices series, the average price of a pint of draught lager rose from 200p in 2000 to 477p in 2024. That is a real change in what a round costs, separate from the question of whether people want to drink at all.

Getting help if your pub business is in distress

If you run a pub or hospitality company and the figures above describe your own position, the important thing is to act while you still have choices. The cost stack is real, but distress is not the same as failure, and several of these businesses are viable once the debt is restructured or the lease renegotiated.

Where your pub or hospitality company reaches that point, the route out depends on whether the business is viable underneath the debt. Options range from a Time to Pay arrangement with HMRC, through a Company Voluntary Arrangement or administration where the business can be rescued, to a Creditors’ Voluntary Liquidation where it cannot. If you are facing a winding-up petition from a landlord, brewery or HMRC, that narrows your options fast, so advice earlier is worth more than advice later.

Behind the numbers

Methodology and Definitions

This page uses different primary sources for different metrics, because no single dataset counts every UK pub closure consistently.

For long-run UK pub totals we use BBPA figures as cited by the House of Commons Library. For permanent loss of pub premises we use rating-list analysis for England and Wales. For financial distress we use ONS business demography and Insolvency Service data, and for the cost stack we use GOV.UK, HMRC and VOA material. For drinking behaviour we use NHS England.

We keep three definitions separate throughout. Pub stock is the number of pubs that exist. Permanent loss is a pub demolished or converted away from pub use. Company insolvency is a formal insolvency event for a company, which may operate one site or many.

Geography also varies: some figures are UK, some are Great Britain or Britain, and some are England and Wales or England only. Each table states its own geography and period. We refresh closures and insolvency figures at least quarterly, and update policy figures when rates, duty, National Insurance or wage rules change.
