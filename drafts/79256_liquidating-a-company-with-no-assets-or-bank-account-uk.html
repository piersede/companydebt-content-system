You have wound the business down. The stock is gone, the bank account is closed, and the only thing left is a list of creditors you cannot pay. It feels like there should be nothing left to do. Close the company, walk away, move on.

In our experience, it is not that simple. A company with no assets still has legal obligations, and some of those obligations sit with you personally. The debts do not disappear because the company is empty. How you close this business matters, and getting it wrong can follow you for years.

What follows: the formal options available to UK directors who need to liquidate a company that has nothing left, what actually happens during the process, what it costs, and where the personal risk sits.

Quick Answer

A company with no assets and outstanding debts cannot simply be struck off the register.

If the company owes money to creditors, the proper route is usually a Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation (CVL), which is a formal insolvency process managed by a licensed insolvency practitioner. Even with no assets in the company, the director typically contributes towards the cost of liquidation. The process protects you from personal liability claims and ensures debts are dealt with lawfully.

Why You Still Need a Formal Process

The question our team hears most often from directors in this position is blunt: "There's nothing left, why can't I just close it?" It makes intuitive sense. If there is no money, no equipment, no debtors, and no bank account, what is there to wind up?

The answer is that the company's debts still exist. They belong to the company, and the company is a separate legal entity from you. Until that entity is formally wound up through the correct procedure, creditors can still pursue it. And in some cases, they can pursue you.

A formal liquidation does several things that an informal wind-down cannot. It draws a legal line under the company's affairs. It gives creditors a structured process for registering their claims. And it provides you, as a director, with a degree of protection against future personal claims, provided you have acted properly.

We tell every director the same thing: walking away from an insolvent company without going through a proper process is one of the more expensive mistakes a director can make. HMRC does not lose interest in unpaid tax just because you stopped trading.

Can You Just Strike Off the Company?

Striking off (sometimes called dissolution) is the cheap and quick way to close a company. You apply to Companies House using a DS01 form, and after a notice period the company is removed from the register. It costs around £10.

But there is a catch. You can only use this route if the company has no outstanding debts. The DS01 form requires you to confirm that the company is not indebted. If you sign that declaration knowing the company owes money, you are making a false statement to Companies House.

Even if you manage to get the company struck off, any creditor can apply to have it restored to the register within six years. If that happens, you are back where you started, except now there is a record of you trying to close the company without paying its debts. That looks bad in any subsequent investigation.

Strike-off is the right tool for solvent companies with nothing owing. For a company with debts, it is the wrong tool entirely.

CVL With No Assets: How It Works

A Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation is the standard route for closing an insolvent company. The word "voluntary" means the directors are choosing to put the company into liquidation, rather than being forced into it by a creditor through a court order (which would be compulsory liquidation).

The process works like this. You contact a licensed insolvency practitioner (IP), who reviews the company's position. If the company is insolvent, meaning it cannot pay its debts as they fall due, the IP will recommend a CVL. The directors pass a resolution to wind up the company, creditors are notified, and the IP is formally appointed as liquidator.

In a normal CVL, the liquidator would gather in the company's assets, sell them, and distribute the proceeds to creditors. When there are no assets, the liquidator's job shifts. They are still required to investigate the company's affairs, report on the directors' conduct to the Insolvency Service, and deal with any claims. But there is no asset-realisation phase.

The whole process typically takes between three and six months for a company with no assets, though the formal dissolution at Companies House can add a few weeks after that.

Who Pays for Liquidation When There Is No Money?

This is the practical question that stops most directors. Liquidation costs money. An insolvency practitioner needs to be paid. There are legal fees, advertising costs, and filing fees. In a company with assets, those costs come out of the asset pot before creditors receive anything. In a company with no assets, there is no pot.

We are always upfront about this: the cost falls on you, the director. This is usually called the director's contribution. For a CVL with no assets, the typical cost ranges from around £3,000 to £6,000 plus VAT, depending on the complexity of the company's affairs and the IP firm you use.

That feels hard. You are already dealing with a failed business, and now you have to find several thousand pounds to close it properly. But the alternative is worse. Leaving an insolvent company dormant on the register leaves you exposed to creditor action, HMRC investigation, and potential disqualification proceedings. The contribution buys you a clean exit.

Some IP firms, including ours, offer payment plans for the director's contribution. We always recommend getting quotes from at least two firms, because pricing varies significantly.

The Director's Contribution

The director's contribution is a personal payment made by one or more directors to fund the liquidation process. It is not a debt of the company. It is money you pay directly to the insolvency practitioner's firm to cover the cost of the work.

The amount depends on several factors: how many creditors the company has, whether there are any contentious issues to investigate, how clean the company's records are, and the IP's own fee structure.

Directors sometimes resist this. It can feel like throwing good money after bad. But as we explain to every director, the contribution is not a penalty. It is the cost of closing a company lawfully when that company cannot pay for its own closure. The Insolvency Service expects directors of insolvent companies to take responsibility for the wind-up process.

What the Liquidator Investigates, Even With No Assets

The liquidator's investigation does not stop just because there is nothing to sell. In fact, some of the most important work happens in no-asset cases, because the liquidator is looking for reasons why there are no assets.

They will examine the company's books and records, bank statements, and transactions in the period before liquidation. They are looking for several things:

  • Preferences: did you pay one creditor ahead of others in the run-up to insolvency? Paying yourself, a family member, or a connected company before paying HMRC or trade creditors can be challenged.
  • Transactions at undervalue: did the company sell or give away assets for less than they were worth? If the company had assets six months ago but has none now, the liquidator will want to know where they went.
  • Wrongful trading: did you continue trading when you knew, or should have known, that the company could not avoid insolvent liquidation? Under section 214 of the Insolvency Act 1986, directors can be held personally liable for debts incurred after the point they should have stopped.
  • Director conduct: the liquidator is required to file a report with the Insolvency Service on every director's conduct. This feeds into the disqualification regime. Honest directors with clean records have nothing to fear here, but sloppy bookkeeping or unexplained transactions will attract scrutiny.

Risk Warning

Under section 214 of the Insolvency Act 1986, directors who continue trading after the point they knew (or should have known) that insolvent liquidation was unavoidable can be held personally liable for the company's debts incurred from that point. There is no fixed deadline: the trigger is the moment a reasonable director would have recognised insolvency was inevitable.

From what we see in practice, the investigation protects you as much as it protects creditors. A clean report from the liquidator is your evidence that you acted properly.

Personal Guarantees and Director's Loan Account Exposure

This is where the phone calls start. You might think the company is empty, but then the bank rings about the personal guarantee you signed when you took out the business loan. A personal guarantee (PG) is a promise you made as an individual to repay the company's debt if the company cannot. Liquidation does not cancel that promise. The debt transfers to you personally.

If you signed PGs for business loans, credit cards, property leases, or supplier accounts, those creditors can chase you for the outstanding amounts after the company is liquidated. This is a personal debt, not a company debt, and it survives the liquidation process.

Then there is the director's loan account (DLA). If you borrowed money from the company over the years and the DLA shows a balance owing to the company, that is technically a company asset.

The liquidator can demand repayment. Many directors are surprised to discover their DLA is overdrawn. Years of small withdrawals, fuel expenses put through the company, personal bills paid from the business account. They add up, and the liquidator will notice.

We see this regularly: an overdrawn DLA in a no-asset liquidation changes the picture entirely. The company does have an asset: your debt to it. The liquidator, as we would in that position, has a duty to recover that money for the benefit of creditors.

What Happens to the Outstanding Debts

Once the CVL is complete and the company is dissolved, any debts that remain unpaid are written off. The company ceases to exist, and its unsecured creditors cannot pursue it further.

This is the core benefit of a formal liquidation for directors. It draws a definitive line. HMRC, trade creditors, utility companies, and other unsecured creditors lose the right to chase the company because the company no longer exists.

But notice the word "unsecured." Debts covered by personal guarantees survive. Tax liabilities where HMRC has issued a personal liability notice survive. And any claim arising from director misconduct, such as a wrongful trading claim, sits with you individually.

In our experience, for most directors of small companies with no assets and honest trading histories, the CVL clears the slate. The debts go, the company goes, and you can start again without the old company's liabilities following you.

Key Takeaway

A completed CVL writes off unsecured company debts permanently. But personal guarantees, overdrawn directors' loan accounts, and any wrongful trading liability are not extinguished by the liquidation and remain enforceable against you personally.

Practical Steps to Liquidate a Company With No Assets

If your company has no assets, no bank account, and debts it cannot pay, here is the practical sequence:

  1. Stop trading. If you have not already, cease all trading activity. Do not incur new debts.
  2. Gather your records. Collect bank statements, management accounts, tax returns, creditor lists, and any personal guarantee documents. The cleaner your records, the quicker and cheaper the process.
  3. Get professional advice. Speak to a licensed insolvency practitioner. Most offer a free initial consultation. Be honest about the company's position and your own personal exposure.
  4. Understand your personal liability. Before proceeding, make sure you know which debts are covered by personal guarantees and what your director's loan account shows. These do not go away in liquidation.
  5. Agree the director's contribution. Get quotes from at least two IP firms. Confirm the total cost, what it covers, and whether payment plans are available.
  6. Pass the winding-up resolution. The directors resolve to place the company into liquidation. The IP handles the statutory notices and creditor communication.
  7. Cooperate with the liquidator. Provide all requested documents promptly. Answer questions about the company's history. The more cooperative you are, the smoother the process.
  8. Wait for dissolution. Once the liquidator has completed their work and filed their final reports, the company is dissolved and removed from the Companies House register.
