HMRC is not like other creditors. They file more winding-up petitions than any other single creditor in the UK, they have preferential status on a range of tax debts since December 2020, and they can transfer company tax liabilities directly to you personally through a mechanism most directors have never heard of.

One director we worked with described his first HMRC enforcement letter as "the one where I realised the company's problem had become my problem." He was right. When HMRC is the creditor pressing hardest, the rules change, the timeline accelerates, and the personal exposure to you as director is higher than most directors expect.

You need to know four things, in order: how HMRC behaves as a creditor in a UK liquidation, where they sit in the distribution waterfall, the personal-liability mechanisms they can use against directors, and the practical steps to manage the relationship before it becomes enforcement.

Risk Warning

HMRC Can Transfer Company Tax Debt to You Personally — Even After Liquidation

Since the Finance Act 2020, HMRC can issue Personal Liability Notices (PLNs) and Joint and Several Liability Notices (JSLNs) that transfer company PAYE, NICs, and CIS debts directly to directors as personal liabilities.

Unlike ordinary creditor claims, PLNs are not discharged by the company's liquidation. They survive dissolution and are enforceable against the director personally through the courts.

Directors with significant PAYE or VAT arrears who assume that a CVL ends all your HMRC exposure are wrong. The PLN/JSLN risk must be assessed before, not after, the liquidation begins.

The Quick Answer for Directors

HMRC is almost always the largest single creditor in your insolvent company. Since 1 December 2020, a portion of HMRC's claim (VAT, PAYE, employee NICs, and CIS deductions) has secondary preferential status, which means it ranks ahead of floating-charge holders and unsecured trade creditors in the distribution waterfall.

On top of that, HMRC can file a winding-up petition to force your company into compulsory liquidation, and since the Finance Act 2020, they can issue Personal Liability Notices (PLNs) and Joint and Several Liability Notices (JSLNs) that transfer the company's tax debt to you personally, bypassing the liquidation entirely.

For you as director, the practical message is: do not ignore HMRC. They have more tools, more patience, and more enforcement budget than any trade creditor you face.

But they also have a formal process for negotiation (Time to Pay), and in our experience, directors who engage early and honestly almost always get a better outcome than those who wait for the petition. HMRC's New Preferential Status: What Changed in December 2020

Before December 2020, HMRC ranked as an ordinary unsecured creditor in your liquidation, alongside trade suppliers, landlords, and everyone else. The Finance Act 2020 changed that.

Since 1 December 2020, certain taxes that the company collected on HMRC's behalf (VAT, PAYE income tax, employee National Insurance contributions, and Construction Industry Scheme deductions) are classified as secondary preferential debts. They rank behind the liquidator's expenses and employee preferential claims, but ahead of floating-charge holders and unsecured creditors.

This was the single biggest change to insolvency distribution in a generation, and most directors we speak to have never heard of it before you brought it up. The practical effect is that HMRC recovers more from every liquidation, and unsecured trade creditors recover less.

In a typical insolvent CVL, the shift means HMRC's secondary preferential claim can absorb a significant portion of whatever the liquidator realises from floating-charge assets, leaving trade creditors with an even smaller dividend than they would have received before 2020.

One case we handled: a company with £60,000 of realisable assets saw HMRC's secondary preferential claim take £38,000 before the floating-charge holder or any trade creditor saw a penny.

Which Taxes Get Priority and Which Stay Unsecured

Not all HMRC debts are preferential. Only the taxes that the company collected on behalf of others qualify for secondary preferential status:

  • Secondary preferential (ranks ahead of floating charge and unsecured): VAT, PAYE income tax deducted from employees, employee NICs, CIS deductions. These are taxes the company collected from employees or customers and was supposed to pass on to HMRC.
  • Ordinary unsecured (ranks with trade creditors): Corporation tax, employer NICs, interest and penalties, student loan deductions (pre-April 2023), and any HMRC debt that is not a "tax collected on behalf of others."

The distinction matters because it changes how much money is available for trade creditors.

In a company with a large PAYE or VAT arrears, the secondary preferential claim can be enormous, and it eats into the pool before anyone else (except the liquidator and employees) gets a distribution. We tell directors to check their PAYE and VAT position before any liquidation conversation, because the secondary preferential number is the one that determines what everyone else gets.

How HMRC Can Force Liquidation

HMRC can petition the court to wind up your company under Section 122(1)(f) of the Insolvency Act 1986, on the ground that the company is unable to pay its debts.

The process follows a standard pattern: HMRC issues a statutory demand for the outstanding tax debt, waits 21 days for payment, and if the debt remains unpaid, files a winding-up petition at court. The petition is served on the company and advertised in the London Gazette, typically within seven days of service.

Timeline Reality

Three Months from First Enforcement Letter to Winding-Up Order Is Realistic

HMRC's enforcement timeline is predictable: statutory demand issued → 21 days → petition filed → petition served and advertised within 7 business days → court hearing 6–8 weeks later → winding-up order. From first enforcement letter to order, the total timeline can be as short as 10–12 weeks.

HMRC's internal policy requires offering Time to Pay before petitioning, but once the enforcement team takes over from debt management (typically after the first missed statutory demand deadline), TTP availability drops sharply. Directors who engage within the first statutory demand window consistently get better outcomes than those who wait.

HMRC files more winding-up petitions than any other creditor in the UK, accounting for over half of all petitions in most years.

They are patient (they will negotiate before petitioning if you engage), but once the decision to petition is made, they do not bluff. The petition deposit is £2,600, the court fee is £343, and HMRC's in-house legal team handles the work at no external cost. For HMRC, petitioning is an administrative step, not a commercial decision. They will spend the money.

The moment the petition is advertised in the Gazette, the consequences cascade.

Your bank freezes the company account (usually within 24 hours of advertisement), trade insurance is withdrawn, Section 127 voids post-petition transactions, and other creditors can substitute themselves as petitioner if HMRC withdraws. We tell directors that the advertisement is the point of no return. See our guide on supplier petitions for the wider mechanics of winding-up petitions.

Personal Liability: PLNs, JSLNs, and Direct Pursuit

This is the section most directors need to read twice. Since the Finance Act 2020, HMRC has the power to issue two types of notice that transfer company tax debt directly to directors and other individuals:

  • Personal Liability Notices (PLNs): HMRC can issue a PLN to a director or officer of the company, making them personally liable for a specified amount of PAYE, NICs, or CIS deductions that the company failed to pay. The PLN must be issued within a specific window and must follow HMRC's procedural rules, but once issued, it creates a personal debt that survives the company's liquidation.
  • Joint and Several Liability Notices (JSLNs): HMRC can issue a JSLN to a director who was involved in tax fraud or who knew (or should have known) that the company would fail to pay its tax liabilities. JSLNs are broader than PLNs and can cover a wider range of tax debts. They are used less frequently but carry the same personal-debt effect.

Both mechanisms bypass the liquidation process entirely. The debt becomes personal to the director, enforceable through the courts, and not discharged by the company's liquidation.

One director we advised received a PLN for £42,000 of unpaid PAYE six months after the CVL was completed. He had assumed the liquidation ended his exposure. It did not. The PLN sat alongside the D-report and the personal guarantee on the overdraft as the three things the liquidation had not wiped clean.

We tell directors with significant PAYE or VAT arrears to take advice on PLN/JSLN exposure before the liquidation begins. The assessment takes an hour and can save tens of thousands in unexpected personal liability.

Time to Pay: HMRC's First Preference

Before HMRC reaches for the petition, they almost always offer Time to Pay (TTP).

A TTP arrangement is a formal agreement between the company and HMRC to pay the outstanding tax debt in instalments over a fixed period, usually 6 to 12 months. HMRC's internal guidance says TTP should be offered before enforcement in most cases, and in our experience, HMRC's TTP team is more flexible than directors expect, provided you engage before the first enforcement letter escalates.

The catch is timing. Directors who pick up the phone after the first statutory demand letter usually get a TTP.

Directors who ignore three letters and then call after a petition has been filed usually do not. HMRC's patience has a shelf life, and once the enforcement team takes over from the debt management team, the tone changes. We have seen TTP offers disappear between a Tuesday letter and a Thursday phone call because the director waited two days too long.

If liquidation is the right route despite the TTP option, entering a CVL before HMRC petitions gives the director control of the process and usually produces a better outcome for everyone, including HMRC.

HMRC's Voting Power in the Liquidation

As the largest creditor by value in most insolvent companies, HMRC's proxy vote at the creditors' decision procedure effectively determines who is appointed as liquidator.

In our caseload, HMRC votes by proxy in virtually every case, almost always in favour of the director's nominee. HMRC rarely challenges the choice of liquidator unless there is a specific concern about independence or connected-party transactions.

HMRC can also form or join a liquidation committee, and they sometimes do in larger cases where the tax debt is significant. A committee with HMRC on it tends to scrutinise the liquidator's fees more closely, which usually benefits the estate (and therefore benefits HMRC's own recovery).

For directors, the practical point is: keeping HMRC informed and cooperative before the decision procedure is more important than worrying about trade creditors. See our guide on creditor meetings in liquidation for the full decision-procedure mechanics.

Common Misunderstandings We Hear

"HMRC is just another creditor." They are not. They have preferential status on collected taxes, they file more petitions than anyone else, and since 2020 they can transfer tax debts directly to you personally via PLNs and JSLNs. No trade creditor has any of those tools.

"If I liquidate, my HMRC debt disappears." The company-level debt is extinguished in the liquidation. But PLNs and JSLNs survive, personal guarantees on tax facilities survive, and any wrongful-trading claim related to unpaid HMRC debt survives. HMRC's reach extends beyond the company.

"HMRC won't really petition over a small amount." HMRC's petitioning threshold is £750 (the statutory demand minimum), and they regularly petition over five-figure debts. The decision is administrative, not commercial. If the debt is undisputed and the 21 days have passed, the petition follows.

"I can negotiate with HMRC after the liquidation." There is nothing to negotiate after the liquidation. The company is gone. If HMRC wants to pursue you personally (via PLN, JSLN, or wrongful-trading claim), the negotiation happens before or during the liquidation, not after. Once the company is dissolved, HMRC deals with you directly.
