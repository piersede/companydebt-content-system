A winding-up petition is a creditor's application to the court asking for your company to be closed down and its assets sold to pay what it owes. It is not a winding-up order. Until the court makes that order, you are still the director, you still control the company, and you still have choices.

The hearing date is printed on the sealed petition you were served with. That date governs your case. No general rule of thumb does, and any page that tells you a hearing is a fixed number of days away is guessing at something the court decides case by case.

Act now rather than at the hearing. Once the petition is advertised in the Gazette it becomes public, your bank may see it, and other creditors may join in. Most of the damage happens in that gap, not in the courtroom.

This Guide Covers England and Wales

Scotland and Northern Ireland run separate court procedures with their own rules and terminology. If your company is registered there, use the Scottish Government guidance or the Northern Ireland Department for the Economy guidance instead.

Find Your Stage First

Directors call us unsure whether the document on the desk is a threat, a court proceeding, or the end of the road. The three are treated very differently, and the answer changes who you should be ringing this afternoon.

If you have spent a couple of days not opening the envelope, you are not unusual and you are not the first. It is worth knowing that the position is often less final than it feels at the point of reading it.

A Petition Has Been Served: Do These Five Things Now

  1. Read the sealed petition properly. Note the hearing date, the court, the petitioning creditor, and the exact amount claimed.
  2. Stop unusual payments. No asset transfers, no paying off a connected creditor, no clearing a director's loan, until you have taken advice.
  3. Work out which this is: a debt you can genuinely dispute, a debt you can pay, or a debt beyond the company's means.
  4. Instruct an insolvency solicitor if the debt is disputed or the advertisement may need to be restrained.
  5. Instruct a licensed insolvency practitioner on viability, negotiation, rescue, or closure.

What a Winding-Up Petition Is

A winding-up petition is a formal application to the court asking it to wind up a company compulsorily. The grounds sit in section 122 of the Insolvency Act 1986, the insolvency tests in section 123, and the question of who may petition in section 124.

A creditor generally needs to be owed at least £750 and to show that the company cannot pay its debts. An unpaid statutory demand is one common way of showing that. It is not the only one, and it is not compulsory: an unpaid judgment or a failed execution can do the same job.

That matters, because directors who never received a demand sometimes assume the petition cannot be valid and lose the window in which they could have acted.

Petitions are heard in the Insolvency and Companies List of the High Court, or in a District Registry or County Court hearing centre with insolvency jurisdiction. You will still see the old name, Companies Court, in older guidance and correspondence.

How to Check Whether a Petition Exists

  • Search The Gazette using the company name and company number.
  • Check Companies House and the official insolvency information via GOV.UK's guidance on finding out if a company is in financial trouble.
  • Compare anything you find against the sealed petition and the hearing details on it.
  • Do not treat absence from one public source as proof that no petition exists. A petition can be presented and served well before it is advertised.

What Happens After the Petition Is Served

The sequence below is the shape of the process. Some points in it are fixed by the rules. Others depend entirely on your case, your court's list, and your bank, and nobody can tell you those in advance.

The gap between service and advertisement is the part most often wasted, typically on chasing the petitioning creditor by phone while the advertisement window opens. That is the period in which options are widest.

The Advertisement Rule, in Full

Unless the court directs otherwise, the Gazette notice for a creditor's petition must normally be published:

  • at least seven business days after the petition has been served on the company; and
  • at least seven business days before the hearing.

Source: Insolvency (England and Wales) Rules 2016, rule 7.10. Both limbs matter. The first is the gap in which urgent work to restrain advertisement is realistically possible.

Two other timings matter. The petitioner must file a certificate of compliance with the advertisement requirements at least five business days before the hearing, and if an order is made, an application to rescind it should be made within five working days.

What nobody can fix in advance: when the court lists the hearing, when your bank becomes aware, whether it restricts the account, and how long any settlement, restraint, validation, or rescue application takes to run. Treat anyone offering you a day-by-day timetable for those with caution.

Your Options After Receiving a Petition

There are two different jobs here, and conflating them is the most common mistake we see. One is dealing with the petition itself. The other is keeping the lights on while you do it. They need different remedies and often different professionals.

Spend the time on the validation order alone and you can arrive at the hearing having paid the wages and no closer to keeping the company. Both jobs need running at once.

Paying the Debt Is Not the End of It

Do not assume that settling the original petition debt automatically closes the case. The petition costs still have to be dealt with, formal withdrawal or dismissal steps may be required, and another creditor may support the petition or seek to take it over.

We have seen directors clear a debt, exhale, and discover the hearing is still listed because a second creditor stepped into the petitioner's shoes.

Keeping Essential Payments Moving

A validation order is a court order authorising specified payments or transactions despite the petition. It is a cashflow remedy, not a resolution. It does not settle the debt, dismiss the petition, or make the hearing go away.

What Happens to the Company Bank Account

This is the question directors ask first, and it is almost always because payroll runs on Friday. The honest answer is that it depends on your bank, and the timing is not something anyone can promise you.

A restriction often shows up as a declined payment before any letter arrives, which means the first sign is usually operational rather than formal. Assume the wages run is at risk as soon as that happens.

The petition itself contains no instruction to any bank to freeze anything. What happens in practice is that banks monitor the Gazette, and a bank that becomes aware of a petition will often restrict the account to protect itself. Some act quickly. Others take longer to pick it up.

The reason sits in section 127 of the Insolvency Act 1986. If an order is later made, dispositions of company property after the deemed commencement of the winding up are void unless the court validates them, and under section 129 that commencement is generally backdated to presentation of the petition.

So the effect is retrospective and conditional. Nothing is void on the day you were served; it becomes void later, if an order follows, and the bank does not want to be the party that has to account for the money.

A validation order is the remedy. It can be sought after presentation and, commonly, after an account has already been restricted. Do not wait for a freeze before taking advice, but equally do not assume the door has closed because one has already happened.

An application normally needs evidence showing which transactions should be authorised and why they are consistent with creditors' interests: the financial position, a cashflow forecast, the specific payments, and supporting documents. A broad request simply to continue trading is unlikely to be sufficient.

What Can Happen at the Hearing

OutcomeWhat it means
Winding-up orderThe court orders compulsory liquidation. The Official Receiver normally becomes liquidator initially.
DismissalThe petition fails, commonly where the debt is genuinely disputed on substantial grounds or the process has been misused.
AdjournmentThe hearing is put back, usually because something concrete is in progress. Adjournments are not granted for hope.
WithdrawalThe petitioner is given permission to withdraw, where the procedural requirements for that have been met.
Substitution or supportAnother creditor supports the petition, or is substituted as petitioner, and the case continues without the original creditor.
Other directionsCase-specific orders, including where another insolvency process is in play.

A winding-up petition is not defended the way an ordinary claim is, and there is no standard response form to post back. Opposing it means evidence filed and served in the proper form, plus attendance or representation at the hearing.

An email to the petitioner's solicitors the night before is not a defence. Whether your case needs a witness statement, counsel, or simply an attendance depends on the facts, and that is a judgement for your solicitor rather than something to work out from a website.

Many petitions are dealt with in busy court lists and the hearing itself may be brief, so properly prepared and filed evidence matters more than anything said on the day. The precise procedure depends on the case and the court.

If an order has already been made, an application to rescind is possible and GOV.UK indicates it should be made within five working days, using Form IAA and a witness statement. That is a short window and it needs legal advice immediately, not next week.

What Directors Should and Should Not Do

Do

  • Preserve every record, including the ones that do not flatter you.
  • Note the hearing date and check whether the petition has been advertised.
  • Get current management accounts and a short cashflow forecast together. You will need both for almost any route.
  • Separate what the company admits from what it can properly dispute.
  • Keep minuting board decisions. The record of what you considered, and when, is worth having later.
  • Take advice before moving money or assets anywhere.
  • Run IP and solicitor advice in parallel where court action is involved, rather than one after the other.

Do Not

  • Ignore the petition. The process runs to its date whether or not you engage with it.
  • Sell, transfer, or conceal assets without advice. Depending on the facts these can be set aside as transactions at an undervalue, treated as misfeasance, or in some cases give rise to criminal liability.
  • Pay connected or favoured creditors. Selective payments may later be challenged as preferences under section 239, or as void dispositions under section 127, depending on timing, relationship, and purpose.
  • Promise creditors money the company has no realistic means of paying.
  • Take new credit without a reasonable basis for repaying it.
  • Rely on a proposed CVA, CVL, or administration as though it automatically stops the court process.

On wrongful trading: continuing to trade after there is no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvent liquidation may expose directors to a claim for a contribution under section 214.

It depends on what you knew, when, and what steps you took to minimise loss to creditors. It is not automatic. A court has to be satisfied of the statutory elements before any contribution is ordered.

In the cases we handle, the directors who come out of this best are rarely the ones who did everything right. They are the ones who can show what they considered and why. That is a paper trail, and it cannot credibly be reconstructed afterwards.

What a Petition Means for Employees and Directors

Employees

A winding-up order normally brings employment to an end where the business stops and the Official Receiver takes control. For most directors we deal with, this is the part they have actually been losing sleep over, rather than the debt itself.

Two separate systems then matter, and they are routinely confused, including on other insolvency websites.

The first is the Redundancy Payments Service. Eligible employees can claim statutory redundancy pay, arrears of wages, holiday pay, and notice pay from the National Insurance Fund, subject to service requirements and category limits. The statutory weekly cap is £751 for relevant claims from 6 April 2026. GOV.UK sets out what employees can get.

The second is priority in the liquidation itself, where certain employee remuneration claims rank as preferential debts under Schedule 6, with their own separate limits. Two different regimes: a figure from one does not describe the other.

Directors

Your powers end when the winding-up order is made, not when the petition is served. Until then you are still running the company, and still carrying the duties that go with it.

Compulsory liquidation takes control away from you and puts the Official Receiver in charge initially. That is a real difference from a voluntary route, where you choose the practitioner and have more say over timing and how staff and customers are told.

What it is not is a shortcut to a softer outcome. Director conduct is reported on in a CVL just as it is in a compulsory liquidation, and the Insolvency Service may investigate further where the report or other evidence raises concerns. Anyone selling you a CVL on the basis that it means less scrutiny is selling you something they cannot deliver.

Disqualification for up to 15 years is possible under the Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986 where misconduct is established, and personal liability where the statutory tests are met. Neither follows from the petition or from liquidation on its own. Both depend on what you did.

Information to Gather Before You Call

The first conversation goes considerably further if you have this to hand. It is also the fastest way for us to tell you whether your position is better than you fear, and reasonably often it is.

  • The petition itself and the hearing notice
  • The date and method of service
  • Whether the petition has been advertised in the Gazette, and when
  • Any statutory demand or judgment behind the debt
  • Correspondence with the petitioning creditor
  • The amount admitted and the amount genuinely disputed
  • Current bank access: working, partly restricted, or frozen
  • Latest management accounts

  • Secured lending and debenture details
  • Payroll dates and any urgent payments due
  • Details of any asset sale or unusual payment made since the petition was presented

That last item is not a trap. It is the thing most likely to need dealing with first, and you want to be the one who raises it.

None of this says anything about your case, which turns on your debt, your evidence, and what you do before advertisement and the hearing.

If You Are the Creditor Considering a Petition

A petition is a collective insolvency process, not a debt-collection tool with sharper teeth. Winning does not mean getting paid.

GOV.UK shows a £352 court fee and a £2,600 petition deposit, checked 29 July 2026, with legal, service and Gazette costs on top. The petitioner normally files Forms Comp 1 and Comp 2 with evidence such as an unpaid demand or judgment.

What you recover depends on what assets exist and where you rank. Secured and preferential claims come ahead of unsecured creditors, and the costs of the process come out of the estate first.

Petitioning a company with no assets is an expensive way of closing it down for the benefit of nobody in particular. The deposit is spent either way.

Weigh that against ordinary enforcement before spending the deposit.
