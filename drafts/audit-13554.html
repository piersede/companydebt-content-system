Pre-pack administration and a company voluntary arrangement are not really alternatives to each other. They answer different questions you face as a director. A pre-pack answers, “how do we save the trade when the company itself cannot survive?” A CVA answers, “how do we keep the company alive when your underlying business is still viable?”

Confuse the two and you make the wrong choice fast. The wrong choice is usually a CVA proposed by a company that should have been pre-packed three months earlier.

This guide walks through what each procedure actually does, the legal framework behind it, where each one fails, and which directors should pursue which route. We see both procedures on a near-weekly basis and the wrong choice is usually a CVA proposed by a company that should have been pre-packed three months earlier.

What a Pre-Pack Administration Actually Does

A pre-pack administration is a sale of the business and its assets, negotiated before the administrator is formally appointed and executed within hours of the appointment taking effect.

The legal vehicle is administration under Schedule B1 of the administration under Schedule B1 of the Insolvency Act 1986. The point of the procedure is preserving the trade, the staff, the contracts, and the goodwill you have built, while the old company is left to be wound down and distributed to creditors.

A few features matter for the decision:

  • No creditor vote. The sale completes on the strength of the administrator’s judgement that it achieves the statutory purpose of administration. Creditors find out after the fact.
  • SIP 16 disclosure. The administrator must publish a SIP 16 statement explaining marketing, valuation, the connected-party position, and why a sale was preferred to other routes. Connected-party sales since 2021 also require referral to the Pre-Pack Pool.
  • Conduct review attaches. Administration triggers an investigation into directors’ conduct under the Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986, even where the directors are the buyers in the pre-pack.
  • Unsecured creditors rarely see a dividend. The sale price typically only covers secured debt and administration costs. That is the trade-off for speed and certainty.

The day-one picture for you as the buyer is a phone ringing on the new company’s line, your staff arriving at the same building under TUPE protection, and your suppliers being asked to open accounts with the new entity. The continuity from the customer’s side can be invisible, while the legal break from the old company’s debts is clean.

What a Company Voluntary Arrangement Actually Does

A CVA is a formal agreement between the company and its unsecured creditors to write off some debt and pay the balance over a fixed term, usually three to five years. The framework sits in Part I of the Insolvency Act 1986.

The directors stay in control of the company throughout. There is no administrator, no sale, no closing of the trading entity. From our seat, a CVA only works when the directors can prove operating viability before we even start drafting the proposal.

Key features:

  • 75% creditor vote by value. The CVA proposal becomes binding only if at least 75% of voting creditors by value agree, subject to the 50% connected-creditor rule.
  • Supervised by a licensed nominee. An insolvency practitioner drafts the proposal, chairs the vote, and then supervises performance for the duration of the arrangement.
  • No automatic conduct investigation. Because the company keeps trading and is not in liquidation or administration, the conduct-review trigger under CDDA 1986 does not fire in the same way.
  • Trading continues under existing contracts. Licenses, leases, and supplier accounts survive unchanged, provided the counterparty does not exercise an insolvency-termination clause.

A CVA only works where the operating business is genuinely viable and the legacy debt is the obstacle. Where the underlying trade is loss-making, the CVA will fail mid-term, the company will tip into liquidation anyway, and the directors will have paid 18 months of monthly contributions to no useful purpose.

How the Two Procedures Differ

Where Each Procedure Fails

Pre-Pack Failure Modes

A pre-pack fails when creditors challenge it as an undervalue transaction under section 238 of the Insolvency Act 1986, when the SIP 16 statement cannot justify the marketing process, or when the Pre-Pack Pool refuses to give a connected-party purchase a clean review.

We have seen pre-packs unwound by a later-appointed liquidator where the marketing period was too short and the buyer was a director’s spouse’s company. The transfer of trade survived; the personal cost to the director did not. If you are the connected party, you need to assume the SIP 16 statement will be read by your creditors and challenged.

CVA Failure Modes

A CVA fails when the company cannot meet its monthly contribution schedule, when a major creditor (often HMRC) votes against the proposal, or when a critical contract counterparty exercises its insolvency clause once the CVA is filed at Companies House.

The breach triggers either a fresh proposal or, more commonly, liquidation under section 84 of the Insolvency Act 1986. By that point you have paid the contributions, lost the company, and inherited the same conduct review you would have faced anyway.

Your Next Step

If your operating business is profitable but your company is carrying legacy debt it cannot service, a CVA is the better route for you. We would expect to see a 12-week cash-flow forecast showing positive operating margin, a creditor list dominated by trade and HMRC (not secured lenders likely to vote no), and a realistic proposal from you for monthly contributions out of forecast profit.

If the trade has value but your operating company is loss-making, or if creditor pressure is too far gone for a 4-week proposal-and-vote timeline, a pre-pack is your right tool. The decision should be framed around what survives. The trade, your staff, and your contracts can be saved in a pre-pack; the old company itself cannot.

If neither describes your position, the honest answer is that neither procedure is appropriate. Creditors’ Voluntary Liquidation is the right route when your company has no viable trade and no buyer interest in the assets. Our preference is always to give you the cleanest exit, not the most expensive one.
