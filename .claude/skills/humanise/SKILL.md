# Humanise

Most pages on this site have the same fault. They are accurate, well sourced,
correctly structured, and they read as though nobody with any experience wrote
them. This skill fixes that page by page.

Full reference: `runtime-packs/stages/humanise.md`. Read this file for the
method; open the pack when you need the detail behind a rule.

**This skill needs judgement, not rule-following.** Every instruction below can
be complied with mechanically and produce a worse page. The two ways that
happens are named as they arise. Watch for them in yourself.

## The one rule that matters

**Make the writing human through insight, not decoration.**

The failure is imitating human *prose* when the target is imitating human
*thought*. Every marker the gate checks for can be satisfied by decoration, and
decoration is what gets pages sent back.

There are two ways to fail. **Under-humanised** prose dresses up thin content
with hooks and flourishes. **Over-humanised** prose manufactures anecdotes and
emotional detail at a rate that makes the technique itself the pattern. Both are
the same fault: reaching for a writing device where an observation should be.

An expert usually writes more plainly, because the interesting thing is what
they know.

> Prefer: "There are actually two different timelines here."
> Over: "You may hear anything from ten days to five years, and both answers are right."

The first sounds like somebody explaining something. The second sounds like
somebody writing content.

## The unit of humanisation is the page, not the paragraph

Read this before anything else, because it governs how the rest is applied.

**Do not spread humanity evenly across the article.** Real expert writing has
quiet passages, technical passages, strong judgements, simple explanations, and
occasional moments of personality. If every section carries an insight, a
warning, a punchline and a line of reader-address, the pattern is artificial no
matter how good each one is.

A page that passes a humanity test paragraph by paragraph will fail as a whole.
Modulation is the point: the quiet factual stretches are what give the sharp
observations around them credibility.

## Practitioner insight must be earned

Before writing a paragraph, ask what an experienced insolvency practitioner
would notice here that a generic writer would miss.

**Never manufacture the answer.** This is the failure mode that replaces
invented anecdotes once you ban them, and it is more dangerous because it is
harder to spot. Practitioner insight must be supported by one of:

- SME knowledge supplied for this page
- verified Company Debt experience
- primary source or research already done for the page
- existing approved material on the site
- a conclusion that follows directly from those facts

If you do not have the evidence to know what practitioners "usually", "often" or
"typically" see, do not invent it. Write the factual explanation plainly
instead. **A plain accurate paragraph beats a vivid paragraph containing
synthetic expertise.**

**Do not force an expert observation into every paragraph.** Some paragraphs
exist to establish a necessary fact: what a Statement of Affairs is, when a
statutory deadline falls, what happens on appointment. Write those plainly. The
expert layer belongs where judgement, interpretation or practical consequence
genuinely adds something.

## The insight hierarchy

When you remove a writing device, this is what to reach for instead. Prefer in
this order:

**Best**

1. A distinction the reader probably has not realised
2. A practical consequence of a fact
3. A common misunderstanding corrected
4. A variable that materially changes the answer
5. A practitioner judgement with evidence behind it
6. A useful warning about what not to do
7. Plain factual explanation

**Worst**

8. Rhetorical cleverness
9. Generic empathy
10. Invented anecdote

Note that plain factual explanation sits at 7, above everything in the worst
list. When in doubt, explain the thing accurately and move on.

The material at the top usually comes from asking: what do directors commonly
misunderstand, which apparently important issue rarely matters, which small
detail changes the advice, what does a practitioner ask about first, what
actually delays a case, where do directors worry unnecessarily, and where are
they too relaxed.

## The "So what?" pass

Run this over every important factual paragraph. Ask: **does the reader know why
this matters to them?**

> "The creditors' decision date must be no more than 14 days after the
> shareholders pass the resolution."

Accurate, and not yet expert writing. With the interpretation attached:

> "That 14-day limit is one reason a CVL cannot be left half-arranged while
> directors decide what to do."

Now the rule means something. This is not humanising; it is interpretation, and
it is what makes editorial writing feel authored. It is also the safest source
of expert voice, because the consequence follows from the fact rather than from
claimed experience.

## The two tests

Apply both to every colourful sentence.

**Could a generic copywriter have written this?** If it is only a metaphor, a
dramatised scenario, an emotional observation, a rhetorical flourish or a clever
contrast, cut it unless it genuinely improves comprehension.

**Could an informed practitioner disagree with this sentence?**

- If no, because it is merely colourful wording, it is probably adding nothing.
- If yes, because it contains a judgement about what matters, what changes an
  outcome, what directors misunderstand or what should be done first, then check
  the judgement is supported. If it is, that is the material you want.

That is close to the real distinction. **Expert writing contains defensible
judgement. AI humanisation contains indefensible colour.**

## The opposite failure: over-humanising

Fixing thin prose too enthusiastically produces this, and it is harder to see
because every individual sentence looks good.

**Every paragraph tries to be memorable.** "It buys you no credit on conduct."
"The arithmetic is not close." "The £13 buys a filing, not an outcome." Fine
sentences individually; as a habit, a tell. A real expert does not continually
produce quotable lines.

**Fictional detail passed off as insight.** Do not invent a representative
director, a month, a phone call, a supplier, a father-in-law's loan, a fit-out,
or a midnight search. Invented colour is not knowledge, and claiming casework
the firm cannot stand behind is an evidence failure as much as a voice one.

> Not: "The bank which financed the fit-out starts ringing the home phone rather than the office."
>
> But: "Personal guarantees sit outside the liquidation. Once the company can no longer meet the guaranteed debt, the lender can pursue the director personally. It is why we ask about guarantees at the first conversation rather than once the liquidation is under way."

**Emotional empathy used as proof of humanity.** Telling the reader what they
dread, fear or ring about at midnight reads as engineered. Answer the question
the worried person actually has instead:

> Not: "Telling the staff is the part directors dread."
>
> But: "Directors usually want to know whether to tell employees before or after the liquidator is appointed. It depends on whether the business is still trading and how the appointment is being handled, so agree the timing with the proposed liquidator first."

**Working target on a rewrite: cut the deliberate humanising devices by about
half and replace them with the top of the insight hierarchy.** Do not add colour
where knowledge does the job.

## Diagnosing a page

Read it and mark every instance. These are the tells:

| Tell | Example |
|---|---|
| Manufactured hook | "Ask X and you'll hear anything from..." |
| Provocative line, neat explanation, clever closer | the most recognisable AI paragraph shape |
| Neat reversal / clever final clause | "and nobody tells you which one you asked" |
| Dramatic one-line paragraph used for effect | a one-liner is fine carrying a fact, not landing a point |
| Borrowed empathy | "the thing keeping you awake at night" |
| Invented specificity | a named month, relative, supplier or scene the firm cannot evidence |
| Synthetic expertise | "what we usually see is..." with nothing behind it |
| Decorative adverbs | "Sadly", "Unsurprisingly" dropped in for personality |
| Repeated rhetorical shape | the same construction three or more times |

Borrowed empathy is the hardest to see. The test: would this sentence sit
unchanged on a debt, legal, medical or divorce page? Then it is filler, however
kind it sounds.

Then count the devices across the whole page. Frequency is itself a tell. If
most sections carry one, the page is over-humanised even where each instance
survives on its own.

## The rewrite method

For each flagged sentence, find the observation it was dressing up and write
that instead. Worked examples from the liquidation timeline page:

- "Ask how long it takes and you'll be told anything from ten days to five
  years" became "There are really two answers to how long a company liquidation
  takes", followed by what each measures. The insight was always the two
  timelines; the hook was in the way.
- "When does this stop being the thing you lie awake thinking about?" became
  "most directors are asking about the first date, not the second". Naming what
  the reader actually wants to know is more empathetic than describing their
  feelings.
- "You are a witness to it, not a participant" became "the liquidator makes the
  decisions in it; your role is to answer questions when asked".

Flat-rewrite test: rewrite the clever sentence plainly. If nothing is lost but
the flourish, keep it flat. Usually only the writer loses anything.

## What must survive the pass

Voice work does not touch substance. Facts, figures, citations, structure,
headings, call-outs and links come out unchanged. If a rewrite needs a new
claim, that is a revise-stage change and it needs a source.

- **A point of view is not a licence for unsupported absolutes.** "Always",
  "never", "the single biggest factor" get pages sent back. Where a source
  supports the judgement, quote the source and let it carry the weight. The
  strongest line on the timeline page is the Insolvency Service's own conclusion
  that the process "does not seem to be efficient", not anything invented.
- **Keep first person inside Company Debt's actual role**: an advisory firm that
  refers to licensed practitioners, not the appointed practice.

## Running the pass

One page at a time. Register the page first if it is unknown to the pipeline.

```bash
python scripts/editorial_task_entry.py --page <slug> --task rewrite
```

Take the baseline before touching anything, so you know which failures you
inherited and which you caused:

```bash
python scripts/article_audit.py --slug <slug>
```

Then rewrite, re-gate, and record the voice audit. The gate will not pass
without a current record, and any prose change invalidates the last one:

```bash
python scripts/voice_audit.py --slug <slug> --record --by claude-opus-5 --scenes N --bite N --tone pass --rhythm pass --read-aloud pass --verdict pass --notes "..."
```

Write the notes properly. Name the tells you found and what replaced them.
"Passed voice audit" is worthless six months from now.

**Patch twice, then redraft.** Layered corrections accumulate an AI signature of
their own, and softening an absolute usually deletes the judgement instead of
re-earning it. Past the second pass, rewrite from spec rather than editing the
edits. `--redraft-now` marks that honestly.

Then push and verify what landed, via the `staging-push` skill. A 200 response
is not proof.

## Gate constraints

Technical requirements, not writing targets. Bake them in during the rewrite so
the fixes do not flatten the voice afterwards.

**Never write towards the pronoun numbers.** `you` at or above 8 per 1,000 words
and `we/our/us` at or above 5 are a diagnostic floor, not a goal. Do not insert
"we" or "you" to satisfy the audit, and never manufacture an "in our
experience..." to lift a count. A page under the floor with genuine practitioner
voice is a better page than one padded up to it; if the density is low because
the expert layer is missing, fix that instead.

- **No em dashes.** House style rather than an AI tell, but the rule is absolute.
- Keep every `<p>` to 2 or 3 rendered lines, roughly 150-250 characters. The
  gate's 400-character limit is a ceiling, not a target.
- Lead paragraph carries no `<strong>`; the theme bolds it automatically.
- Gloss every acronym and statutory reference at first mention in prose. Tables
  and headings do not count as an introduction.
- FAQ `panelTitle` attributes need `<` escapes, single backslash. Raw
  `<strong>` renders as visible junk.
- Related Guides must be the final H2, after the FAQ accordion.

## Rolling this out across the site

Work by cluster rather than alphabetically. Pages in the same cluster share
phrasing, and fixing them together stops the survivors contradicting the
rewritten ones.

1. Baseline every page in the cluster first and record the scores. Some pages
   fail checks that predate this work; do not silently inherit the blame.
2. Rewrite one page at a time, all the way through gate, audit record and push,
   before starting the next.
3. Watch for shared claims across the cluster. Voice work surfaces factual
   contradictions between sibling pages surprisingly often. Fix them as you go.
4. Where a page needs practitioner insight the evidence does not support, stop
   and ask rather than inventing it. A list of questions for the SME is a
   legitimate output of this pass.
5. Before starting, check nobody else is working the same cluster: `git status`
   and file modification times. This has collided before.
