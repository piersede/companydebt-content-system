Construction fails more companies than any other industry in the UK, and it is not close. The reasons are structural, not bad luck: fixed-price contracts signed before costs rose, cash held back up the chain as retention, and a supply model where one contractor's collapse takes a dozen subcontractors down with it.

This page sets out what is driving construction insolvencies, the warning signs directors miss, and the options if your firm cannot pay, with the sector-specific detail that generic advice leaves out.

Insolvency in the Construction Sector

In the 12 months to May 2026, 3,803 construction companies entered insolvency. That is around 17% of every UK company insolvency where the sector is recorded, the largest share of any single industry, from a sector that makes up roughly 6 to 7% of the economy. Construction has held that unwanted top spot for years.

The numbers rose sharply after 2021 and have stayed high. Annual construction insolvencies ran at about 2,500 a year before 2020, peaked at 4,389 in 2023, and were still 3,949 across 2025. Against roughly 384,830 construction enterprises on the ONS register, that is close to a one-in-a-hundred failure rate every year, before you count the firms that quietly wind down without a formal insolvency.

Construction insolvency at a glance

Volume: 3,803 insolvencies in the year to May 2026, about 17% of all UK company insolvencies, the highest of any sector.

Trend: 4,389 in 2023, 4,040 in 2024, 3,949 in 2025. Well above the pre-2020 baseline of around 2,500 a year.

Where it bites hardest: specialist trade subcontractors (SIC 43) account for the majority of failures, far more than main contractors (SIC 41) or civil engineering (SIC 42).

Payment: construction firms take an average of 34.7 days to pay an invoice, and only about a third of payments in the sector are made within the agreed terms.

The failures are not spread evenly. In the three months to January 2026, specialised trades, the electricians, groundworkers, plasterers and plumbers classified under SIC 43, recorded roughly 2,351 insolvencies against 1,487 for building firms and just 202 for civil engineering.

Subcontractors sit at the bottom of the payment chain, so they absorb the shocks that start further up it. Civil engineering, backed by long government infrastructure pipelines, fails least.

What's Driving Construction Insolvencies

Ask why a construction company failed and the answer is almost always cash, not orders. Profitable firms with full order books run out of money because of how the industry is paid. Three structural pressures do most of the damage.

Fixed-price contracts against rising costs

Most construction is priced as a lump sum agreed months, sometimes years, before the work is done. When input costs jump, the contractor absorbs the difference.

The BCIS Materials Cost Index peaked at 26% annual inflation in June 2022, and construction cost inflation has stayed above general CPI since, running at around 4.4% into late 2025. Firms that had signed fixed-price deals in 2020 and 2021 were left delivering projects at prices that no longer covered the materials, with no contractual way to pass the increase on.

On margins that sit below 2% for many main contractors, that is enough to end a business. It was cited in the collapses of both ISG and Buckingham Group.

Retentions and the payment chain

Construction routinely withholds 3 to 5% of a subcontractor's payment as retention, half released at practical completion and the balance often a year or more later at the end of the defects period. For a subcontractor that retained sum is frequently the entire profit on the job, sitting in someone else's bank account.

Around £4.5 billion is tied up in retentions across the UK at any time, and an estimated 44% of contractors have lost retention money when a firm above them failed. Add payment terms that only about a third of the sector meets, per Build UK data, and even a well-run subcontractor can be starved of cash by clients who are perfectly solvent.

The VAT reverse charge cash squeeze

Since 1 March 2021 the VAT domestic reverse charge has changed how VAT moves through the supply chain. Instead of a subcontractor charging VAT, collecting it, and holding it until the quarterly return, the main contractor now accounts for it. The subcontractor never sees that 20%.

For firms that had, knowingly or not, been using the VAT they held as working capital between returns, the buffer vanished overnight. Many subcontractors also became repayment traders, paying VAT on materials but collecting none on sales, which leaves them waiting on HMRC refunds to keep cash moving. Any delay lands straight on the bank balance.

Underneath all three sits a labour problem. The Federation of Master Builders and CIOB State of Trade survey for the second half of 2025 found 72% of small building firms struggling to recruit skilled trades, up from 61% earlier in the year, with carpenters and bricklayers hardest to find.

Almost half reported delayed jobs and around a fifth cancelled work outright, while 57% raised wages to hold on to staff. Delay and wage inflation both eat the margin that fixed prices already squeezed.

Warning Signs a Construction Business Is Insolvent

Construction insolvencies rarely arrive without notice. The cash problems usually show three to six months before anything formal happens. The signs below are the ones we see repeatedly in this sector.

  • Funding old jobs with new deposits. Using the mobilisation payment or deposit from a new project to pay suppliers and subcontractors on a previous one. It works until the new work slows, then the whole structure fails at once.
  • Falling behind with HMRC. Missing VAT payments, or holding on to CIS deductions and PAYE rather than paying them over. Treating HMRC as an overdraft is one of the clearest signals a construction firm is in trouble.
  • Stretching supplier terms. Quietly pushing payment from 30 to 60 days or beyond, and merchants moving you to pro-forma or cash on delivery.
  • Retentions and final accounts written off. A spike in disputed final accounts or adjudication, or writing off retention money lost when a contractor above you failed, wiping out the equity on the balance sheet.
  • Reliance on one main contractor. If a single client is most of your turnover, their financial health, not yours, decides whether you survive.

If more than one of these is true, the company is likely already insolvent on a cash-flow basis. That is the point at which a director's duty shifts towards protecting creditors, and the point at which taking advice early makes a real difference to the outcome.

When a Main Contractor Fails: The Subcontractor Cascade

The defining risk in construction is that you can be solvent, well run and fully booked, and still be pulled under by a failure above you. When a tier-one contractor collapses, unpaid invoices and lost retentions cascade down the chain. This is the sector's most distinctive insolvency route, and it is worth understanding before it happens to you.

What happens to your money when a contractor collapses

When ISG failed in September 2024, the largest construction collapse since Carillion, it went down owing more than £1.1 billion, over £280 million of it to trade suppliers, and left more than 600 sites stopped.

A year earlier Buckingham Group entered administration owing around £302 million, with more than £108 million due to trade contractors and no return expected for unsecured creditors. In both cases subcontractors who had done nothing wrong lost retentions and unpaid invoices and, in several cases, followed the contractor into administration.

Unless retention money is genuinely ring-fenced in a project bank account or trust, it falls into the failed contractor's estate and you rank as an unsecured creditor, near the back of the queue.

We act for subcontractors caught in exactly this position, and the pattern is usually the same: the work was done, the money was owed, and a collapse further up the chain turned a good year into a fight for survival.

Bonds, step-in rights and chasing what you're owed

Some protections do exist, and they are worth checking before you sign, not after a collapse. Employers often hold a performance bond, commonly around 10% of the contract value, which they can call to fund a replacement contractor. Collateral warranties may carry step-in rights that let a funder or employer keep a project running by employing subcontractors directly through novation.

And since the 2020 Supreme Court decision in Bresco v Lonsdale, a company in liquidation can still use adjudication to pursue money it is owed, which gives insolvency practitioners a real route to recover unpaid construction accounts for creditors. Whether any of this helps you depends on the contract you signed, which is exactly why the paperwork matters.

Your Options if a Construction Company Can't Pay

If your construction company cannot pay its debts, the worst thing you can do is keep trading in the hope the next valuation clears it. Once a firm is insolvent, continuing to run up liabilities can expose you personally through wrongful trading. In our experience the right move is to get the position assessed by a licensed insolvency practitioner while options are still open.

Which option fits depends on whether the business is viable underneath the cash problem.

  • Time to Pay arrangement. If the core business works and the problem is a specific HMRC arrears, such as VAT, PAYE or CIS, a Time to Pay arrangement spreads the tax debt over a manageable period and keeps the company trading.
  • Company Voluntary Arrangement. A CVA lets a viable company repay creditors a share of what it owes over time while continuing to work, which can protect ongoing contracts and retained staff.
  • Administration. Administration gives breathing space from creditors and can be used to rescue the business, complete or novate live contracts, or achieve a better return than an immediate closure.
  • Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation. Where the company cannot be saved, a CVL closes it in an orderly way, deals with creditors including HMRC, and draws a line under the debts, provided you have not given personal guarantees.

Two construction-specific points matter here. Personal guarantees to merchants and lenders are common in the trade and survive the company's closure, so it is worth auditing what you have signed before choosing a route. And live contracts, bonds and retentions all need handling as part of any process, which is one reason construction insolvencies are rarely as simple as closing a company down.
