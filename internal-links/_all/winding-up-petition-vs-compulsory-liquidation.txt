<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A <a href="/winding-up-petitions/">winding-up petition</a> is not the same thing as compulsory liquidation, but directors use the terms interchangeably and the confusion costs them options. The petition is the application. Compulsory liquidation is the outcome.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Between those two points sits a window where you can still pay the debt, negotiate, or convert to voluntary liquidation. Once the court grants the order, that window closes.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We explain this distinction to directors every single week, often on the phone with someone who has just received a petition and does not understand what it means. Getting it wrong leads to either panic (assuming the company is already in liquidation when it is not) or paralysis (assuming nothing can be done when it can).</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The petition is serious, but it is not the end. The winding-up order is the end. Understanding the difference tells you exactly how much time you have and what you can do with it.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:group {"className":"cd-callout cd-callout\u002d\u002drisk-warning"} -->
<div class="wp-block-group cd-callout cd-callout--risk-warning">
  <p class="cd-callout__label">Risk Warning</p>
  <p class="cd-callout__title">The Bank Account Freeze Happens Before the Court Hearing; Not After</p>
  <!-- wp:paragraph -->
  <p>Under section 127 of the Insolvency Act 1986, all property dispositions after the petition presentation date are void unless court-approved. Banks enforce this by monitoring the London Gazette and freezing company accounts immediately on advertisement; typically hours before most directors realise what has happened.</p>
  <!-- /wp:paragraph -->
  <!-- wp:paragraph -->
  <p>The 6 to 10 week window between petition service and court hearing is an opportunity to act, but the bank freeze can cut that window short the moment the Gazette advertisement is published. Directors who wait for the court hearing to take action have usually already lost access to their company's funds.</p>
  <!-- /wp:paragraph -->
</div>
<!-- /wp:group -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We have written this page to set out the key differences between a winding-up petition and <a href="/liquidation/compulsory-liquidation/">compulsory liquidation</a>, explain what happens at each stage, and clarify what options remain available to you at each point.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What a Winding-Up Petition Actually Is</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A winding-up petition is a formal application to the court, filed by a creditor, asking the court to order the compulsory winding up of your company. It is governed by section 124 of the Insolvency Act 1986.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The most common ground is that the company is unable to pay its debts (section 122(1)(f)), which the creditor proves by showing an unsatisfied statutory demand or a county court judgement that has not been paid.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The petition must be served on the company at its registered office. After service, the petitioning creditor must advertise the petition in the London Gazette at least 7 business days after service and at least 7 business days before the hearing date.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We stress this because the Gazette advertisement is the trigger event that causes your bank to freeze the company's accounts. Most directors do not realise this happens before the court hearing, not after.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Between service and the hearing, there is typically a 6 to 10 week window. This is your last opportunity to resolve the situation without the company entering compulsory liquidation.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We find that directors who use this window effectively often avoid the worst outcome. We also find that directors who do nothing during this window almost always end up in compulsory liquidation wondering why nobody told them they had a choice. We are telling you now.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What Compulsory Liquidation Actually Is</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Compulsory liquidation begins when the court grants the winding-up order. From that moment, the Official Receiver is appointed as liquidator and takes control of the company's affairs. Your authority as a director ends completely. Any disposition of company property made after the date of the winding-up order is void unless the court approves it.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The Official Receiver has a statutory duty to investigate the conduct of every director. This investigation is mandatory in compulsory liquidation, unlike in a CVL where the scope is at the liquidator's discretion.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The Official Receiver will examine your bank statements, payment decisions, director's loan account, filing history, and compliance with your statutory duties. They will file a conduct report with the Insolvency Service, and if your conduct was unfit, disqualification proceedings may follow.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We tell directors: compulsory liquidation is the most invasive form of insolvency process. You do not choose the liquidator, you do not control the timeline, and the investigation is more thorough than in any voluntary process. If you have any choice at all, avoiding compulsory liquidation should be your priority.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The Critical Difference: Your Window to Act</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The most important practical difference between a petition and compulsory liquidation is the window between them. During that 6 to 10 week gap, you have options that disappear entirely once the order is granted.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Option 1: Pay the petition debt.</strong> If you pay the full amount owed to the petitioning creditor (including their legal costs), the petition must be dismissed. This is the cleanest resolution, but it requires having or raising the funds.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We see directors borrow from family, sell personal assets, or arrange emergency finance to pay off petition debts. If the company is otherwise viable, this can be the right move.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Option 2: Negotiate with the petitioner.</strong> Some petitioning creditors will accept a payment plan or partial settlement in exchange for withdrawing the petition. This is more common with trade creditors than with HMRC, but we have seen HMRC agree to Time to Pay arrangements even after filing a petition, provided the proposal is realistic and the company can demonstrate ongoing viability.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Option 3: Dispute the debt.</strong> If the debt is genuinely disputed on substantial grounds, you can oppose the petition at the hearing. The court will not grant a winding-up order on a disputed debt. But the dispute must be genuine and substantiated. Saying "I disagree with the amount" without evidence will not succeed.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Option 4: Convert to a CVL.</strong> You can pass a winding-up resolution and initiate a voluntary liquidation before the court hearing. This converts the process from creditor-driven to director-initiated.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We advise this route when the company is genuinely insolvent and the petition debt cannot be paid, because a CVL produces a better conduct outcome than compulsory liquidation. Our voluntary liquidation guide explains the CVL process in full. The liquidator's investigation is less automatic, you demonstrate responsible action, and the process is generally faster and less expensive.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Option 5: Apply for a validation order.</strong> If you need the company's bank accounts to remain operational pending the hearing (for example, to pay employees or fulfil existing contracts), you can apply to the court for a validation order under section 127 of the Insolvency Act.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>This is not guaranteed, but courts will grant it where there is a reasonable prospect of the petition being dismissed or where the company's business would suffer disproportionate harm from the freeze.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The Bank Account Freeze: Before the Hearing, Not After</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We dedicate a section to this because it catches directors by surprise more than any other aspect of the process. Your bank will freeze the company's accounts when the petition is advertised in the London Gazette. This happens before the court hearing, not after the winding-up order.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Banks monitor the Gazette as part of their compliance obligations. When they see your company's name in a winding-up petition advertisement, they freeze the accounts automatically. We have seen this happen within hours of the Gazette publication.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>From that point, you cannot pay staff, cover rent, make supplier payments, or fund any trading activity from the company's accounts.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:group {"className":"cd-callout cd-callout\u002d\u002dtimeline-reality"} -->
<div class="wp-block-group cd-callout cd-callout--timeline-reality">
  <p class="cd-callout__label">Timeline Reality</p>
  <p class="cd-callout__title">Seven Business Days: The Window Between Petition Service and Gazette Advertisement</p>
  <!-- wp:paragraph -->
  <p>The petitioner cannot advertise the petition in the London Gazette until at least 7 business days after service on the company (Insolvency (England and Wales) Rules 2016). This creates a short but critical quiet window; the last period before banks, suppliers, and credit agencies see the entry.</p>
  <!-- /wp:paragraph -->
  <!-- wp:paragraph -->
  <p>Directors who receive a petition can use these 7 days to pay the debt, obtain a restraining order, or begin CVL proceedings.</p>
  <!-- /wp:paragraph -->
  <!-- wp:paragraph -->
  <p>After advertisement, the bank freeze is automatic and almost instantaneous. A director who engages a licensed insolvency practitioner on day 1 of service has a materially better range of options than one who waits until the freeze lands.</p>
  <!-- /wp:paragraph -->
</div>
<!-- /wp:group -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If you know a petition is coming, the time to plan for the freeze is before the Gazette advertisement, not after. Ensure essential payments (employee wages for work already done, critical utility bills) are made before the advertisement date.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We are not suggesting you empty the accounts. We are saying you should ensure your staff are paid and your essential obligations are covered before the freeze makes it impossible.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Winding-Up Petition or Compulsory Liquidation: What to Do Next</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If you have received a winding-up petition, you have a window. Use it. Do not wait for the hearing and hope it goes away. The options we have described above are real, but they all require action in the first few days after service.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If you have not yet received a petition but a creditor is threatening one, you have an even wider window. Our guide on whether to close or save your company can help you decide.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Acting before the petition is filed gives you the most control and the best options. A voluntary liquidation initiated before a petition is always a better outcome than a compulsory liquidation imposed after one. If you are still weighing the alternatives, our guide <a href="/liquidation/cva-vs-strike-off-vs-liquidation/">comparing CVA, strike off and liquidation</a> sets out how the main closure and rescue routes differ.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->

If you have received a petition, or if a creditor is threatening one, speak to a practitioner today. The difference between acting this week and acting next month can be the difference between a manageable process and one you cannot control.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">FAQs: Winding-Up Petition vs Compulsory Liquidation</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-block {"blockID":"79322-faq-block","theme":"#ffffff","collapsed":true,"collapsedOnMobile":true,"titleColor":"#000000","hasFAQSchema":true} -->
<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"theme":"#ffffff","collapsed":true,"collapsedOnMobile":true,"hasFAQSchema":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eIs a winding-up petition the same as compulsory liquidation?\u003c/strong\u003e"} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>No. A winding-up petition is the application to the court. Compulsory liquidation is the outcome if the court grants the order. Between the petition and the order, there is a 6 to 10 week window during which you can pay the debt, negotiate, dispute, or convert to voluntary liquidation. Once the order is granted, those options close.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- /wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"index":1,"theme":"#ffffff","collapsed":true,"collapsedOnMobile":true,"hasFAQSchema":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eCan I stop a winding-up petition before the court hearing?\u003c/strong\u003e"} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Yes. Pay the petition debt in full (including the creditor's costs) and the petition must be dismissed. You can also oppose the petition at the hearing if you have a genuine dispute about the debt, or negotiate a settlement with the petitioning creditor. Converting to a CVL before the hearing is also an option that avoids compulsory liquidation.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- /wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"index":2,"theme":"#ffffff","collapsed":true,"collapsedOnMobile":true,"hasFAQSchema":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eWhy does the bank freeze my accounts before the court hearing?\u003c/strong\u003e"} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Banks freeze accounts when the petition is advertised in the London Gazette, not when the court order is made. Under section 127 of the Insolvency Act, any disposition of company property after the commencement of winding up (which relates back to the date of the petition) is void unless the court approves it. Banks freeze accounts to avoid processing payments that might later be reversed.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- /wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"index":3,"theme":"#ffffff","collapsed":true,"collapsedOnMobile":true,"hasFAQSchema":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eIs a CVL better than compulsory liquidation for directors?\u003c/strong\u003e"} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Almost always. A CVL demonstrates responsible action, gives you preparation time, allows you to influence the liquidator appointment, and results in a less automatic investigation.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Compulsory liquidation involves a mandatory investigation by the Official Receiver, no director control over the process, and is perceived as a failure to act. Converting to a CVL before a winding-up hearing is one of the strongest steps a director can take.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- /wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"index":4,"theme":"#ffffff","collapsed":true,"collapsedOnMobile":true,"hasFAQSchema":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eHow long do I have between receiving a petition and the court hearing?\u003c/strong\u003e"} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Typically 6 to 10 weeks. The petition must be served at least 21 days before the hearing, and it must be advertised in the Gazette at least 7 business days after service and at least 7 business days before the hearing. The exact timeline depends on the court's schedule and the petitioner's compliance with procedural requirements.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
