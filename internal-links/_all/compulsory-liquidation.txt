<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What Compulsory Liquidation Means for Your Company</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Compulsory liquidation is the forced closure of your company by court order. It is not something you choose. It happens because a creditor, most commonly HMRC, has asked the court to wind your company up.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Once a winding-up order is granted, the Official Receiver (a government-appointed officer) takes control of your company. You lose the power to manage the business, access company bank accounts, or dispose of company assets. The company stops trading and its assets are collected, sold, and distributed to creditors.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The legal basis sits in the Insolvency Act 1986. The court can order compulsory liquidation if it is satisfied your company cannot pay its debts, under section 122(1)(f).</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The distinction that matters: in <a href="/liquidation/creditors-voluntary-liquidation/">a creditors' voluntary liquidation</a> (CVL), you initiate the process and choose the insolvency practitioner. In compulsory liquidation, the creditor forces it and the Official Receiver is appointed.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That difference affects cost, speed, the depth of director scrutiny, and whether your employees can claim statutory redundancy pay through the normal route. Our guide to <a href="/liquidation/voluntary-vs-compulsory-liquidation/">the difference between voluntary and compulsory liquidation</a> sets the two routes side by side.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>In our practice, the CVL route consistently produces better outcomes for directors than waiting for compulsory liquidation. Directors who act before the Gazette notice retain the ability to choose their insolvency practitioner, face less intensive Official Receiver scrutiny, and give employees a cleaner route to statutory redundancy pay.</p>

<p>Compulsory liquidation is not a worse version of the same outcome. It is a materially different one, with more personal exposure.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Why Compulsory Liquidation Matters Right Now</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Compulsory liquidation is not a background legal process. It triggers immediate, practical consequences that affect you personally and your company operationally.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Bank account freeze.</strong> Banks routinely freeze company accounts when they become aware of a winding-up petition, often after the Gazette notice is published. This can happen before the court hearing, halting your payroll, supplier payments, and all outgoing transactions overnight.</li>
<li><strong>Gazette publication.</strong> The petition is advertised in The London Gazette at least 7 business days before the hearing. This is a public record. Your customers, suppliers, landlords, and lenders can see it.</li>
<li><strong>Loss of director control.</strong> Once the winding-up order is made, you have no authority to act on behalf of the company. The Official Receiver manages everything from that point.</li>
<li><strong>Director investigation.</strong> The Official Receiver is required to investigate your conduct as a director. If wrongful or fraudulent trading is found, you can face personal liability, disqualification for up to 15 years, or both.</li>
<li><strong>Employee impact.</strong> Your employees are automatically dismissed when the winding-up order is made. Redundancy claims go through the Redundancy Payments Service rather than the employer.</li>
</ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The practical issue is timing. In our experience, once the petition is advertised, the damage to your company's reputation and banking relationships has already started, regardless of the court outcome.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What Happens If You Ignore a Compulsory Liquidation Petition</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>In our experience, doing nothing is the most damaging option. If a winding-up petition is served and you file no response, the court will almost certainly grant the winding-up order.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>No defence filed.</strong> The court treats the debt as undisputed. The order is made on paper or at a short hearing. There is no second chance.</li>
<li><strong>Bank account frozen before hearing.</strong> Most UK banks freeze accounts on sight of the Gazette notice. Directors often find this out when payroll fails or a supplier payment bounces.</li>
<li><strong>Official Receiver appointed.</strong> Your company is placed into compulsory liquidation immediately. You must cooperate with the OR's investigation, hand over all company records, and attend interviews if required.</li>
<li><strong>Director conduct scrutiny intensifies.</strong> Ignoring a petition does not look like an oversight. The OR and the Insolvency Service will look closely at whether you continued trading while knowing the company was insolvent.</li>
</ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The window to respond is short. Between service of the petition and the hearing, you have 8 to 10 weeks in most cases. But the real pressure point is the Gazette advertisement, which triggers the bank freeze. That happens much sooner.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Immediate Steps to Take on Compulsory Liquidation</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If you have received a winding-up petition or a statutory demand, these are the actions available to you. The right step depends on whether the debt is disputed, whether your company can pay, and how much time you have left.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list {"ordered":true} -->
<ol class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Check the deadline.</strong> A statutory demand gives you 21 days. A winding-up petition hearing is typically 8 to 10 weeks from issue. Both deadlines are hard.</li>
<li><strong>Decide whether the debt is genuinely disputed.</strong> If you have a legitimate reason to challenge the debt (the amount is wrong, the goods were defective, the invoice was already paid), you may be able to apply to have the petition dismissed or restrained. The court can do this if the debt is disputed on substantial grounds.</li>
<li><strong>Pay the debt in full.</strong> If your company can pay the petitioning creditor, paying the full amount owed (including legal costs) before the hearing will usually result in the petition being dismissed.</li>
<li><strong>Negotiate a settlement.</strong> Creditors sometimes agree to withdraw the petition if you offer a payment plan or partial settlement. This needs to happen quickly and in writing.</li>
<li><strong>Consider a voluntary route.</strong> If your company cannot pay its debts but you want to close on your own terms, a CVL may be available. This allows you to choose the insolvency practitioner and typically results in less scrutiny than compulsory liquidation.</li>
<li><strong>Get licensed advice immediately.</strong> A licensed insolvency practitioner can assess your position, identify which options are still open, and act on the same day if needed.</li>
</ol>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Delaying past the Gazette notice point makes every option harder in our caseload. The bank freeze alone can make it impossible to fund a settlement or pay for a voluntary process.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Legal Grounds for Compulsory Liquidation: Who Can Petition</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Compulsory liquidation is governed by the Insolvency Act 1986. The key sections are:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Section 122(1)(f).</strong> The court may order winding up if the company is unable to pay its debts.</li>
<li><strong>Section 123.</strong> Defines what "unable to pay its debts" means. The two main tests are the cash-flow test (the company cannot pay debts as they fall due) and the balance-sheet test (liabilities exceed assets).</li>
<li><strong>Section 124.</strong> Sets out who can present a winding-up petition. This includes creditors, the company itself, directors, shareholders, the Secretary of State, and certain regulatory bodies.</li>
</ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>In practice, HMRC is the petitioner in the majority of compulsory liquidation cases. HMRC petitions have risen sharply since the end of the COVID-era enforcement pause.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The minimum debt threshold for a winding-up petition is <strong>£750</strong>, set by section 123(1)(a) of the Insolvency Act 1986. In practice, most petitions involve debts of several thousand pounds or more, but the legal threshold is low.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A creditor does not need to prove the company is balance-sheet insolvent. Failure to pay a debt of £750 or more within 21 days of a statutory demand is enough to establish a presumption of inability to pay under the Act.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The Compulsory Liquidation Court Process</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The winding-up process follows a fixed legal sequence. Knowing the stages helps you identify where you still have options.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list {"ordered":true} -->
<ol class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Statutory demand served.</strong> The creditor sends a formal demand for payment. Your company has 21 days to pay. If the debt is not paid or disputed, the creditor can issue a petition.</li>
<li><strong>Winding-up petition filed.</strong> The petition is filed at court. The petitioner must serve it on your company at its registered office.</li>
<li><strong>Gazette advertisement.</strong> The petition must be advertised in The London Gazette at least 7 business days before the hearing. This is the trigger point for bank freezes and reputational damage.</li>
<li><strong>Court hearing.</strong> The court hears the petition, typically 8 to 10 weeks after issue. Possible outcomes are: a winding-up order made, the petition dismissed (debt paid or legitimately disputed), the petition adjourned (more time given, often for payment negotiations), or the petition stayed (if a voluntary arrangement is proposed).</li>
<li><strong>Official Receiver appointed.</strong> If the order is made, the Official Receiver becomes liquidator. A private insolvency practitioner may later be appointed if creditors request it.</li>
<li><strong>Investigation and asset realisation.</strong> The OR investigates director conduct, collects company assets, and distributes proceeds to creditors in the statutory order of priority.</li>
</ol>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The entire process from petition to final dissolution can take 12 months or longer. For you as director, the critical period is the weeks between the petition being filed and the hearing date. That is when we see the most options still available.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What Directors Should Avoid During Compulsory Liquidation</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Once your company is facing a winding-up petition, certain actions will make your situation significantly worse. Some carry personal liability. Others are criminal offences.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Do not pay one creditor ahead of others to reduce pressure.</strong> Paying a connected creditor (a director's loan account, a family member's invoice, a related company) when the company is insolvent is a preference under section 239 of the Insolvency Act 1986. The liquidator can reverse the payment and pursue you personally.</li>
<li><strong>Do not move or sell company assets below value.</strong> Transactions at an undervalue (section 238) can be reversed. Transferring stock, vehicles, equipment, or property to connected parties while the company is insolvent will be investigated.</li>
<li><strong>Do not continue trading if there is no reasonable prospect of paying creditors.</strong> Wrongful trading under section 214 can result in you being ordered to contribute personally to the company's debts. The test is whether a reasonably diligent person would have concluded there was no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvent liquidation.</li>
<li><strong>Do not ignore the Official Receiver.</strong> You are legally required to cooperate with the OR, provide company records, attend interviews, and complete a questionnaire. Non-cooperation can lead to arrest, contempt of court, and a longer disqualification period.</li>
<li><strong>Do not destroy company records.</strong> Destroying or concealing company books and records is a criminal offence under sections 209 and 210.</li>
</ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The theme is straightforward: actions that protect you personally at the expense of creditors will be scrutinised and, where found, penalised. We advise you to take no unilateral action with company assets once a petition is in play.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What Compulsory Liquidation Means for Directors, Employees, and Bank Accounts</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>For you as director.</strong> You are automatically removed from your management role when the winding-up order is made. You must cooperate with the Official Receiver's investigation into the company's affairs and your own conduct.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If the investigation reveals wrongful trading, fraudulent trading, or misfeasance, you may face personal liability for company debts (wrongful trading claims under section 214), disqualification from acting as a director for between 2 and 15 years, or criminal prosecution in cases of fraud or deliberate concealment.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Personal guarantees (commonly given for commercial leases, bank lending, or HMRC time-to-pay arrangements) survive liquidation. If you have personally guaranteed a debt, the creditor can pursue you directly after your company enters liquidation.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>For your employees.</strong> Employees are automatically dismissed on the date of the winding-up order. They become preferential creditors for certain claims (up to 4 months' unpaid wages, capped at £800 per employee, plus accrued holiday pay).</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Redundancy pay, notice pay, and unpaid wages beyond the preferential cap are claimed through the government's Redundancy Payments Service (part of the Insolvency Service), not from the company directly. This process typically takes 3 to 6 weeks after the claim is submitted.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>For your bank accounts.</strong> Company bank accounts are frozen either when the bank becomes aware of the petition (usually via the Gazette notice) or when the winding-up order is made. Any disposition of company property after the petition is presented is void unless the court orders otherwise (section 127).</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>This means payments made from the company account after the petition date, including wages, supplier payments, and rent, may need to be returned to the liquidator. You should seek a validation order from the court if you need to continue making essential payments before the hearing.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Alternatives to Compulsory Liquidation</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Even after a winding-up petition has been filed, some alternatives may remain open. Which ones depend on your company's financial position, how much time is left before the hearing, and whether the petitioning creditor will agree.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Pay the debt.</strong> If your company or a third party can pay the full petitioning debt plus the creditor's legal costs, the petition will usually be dismissed. This is the most direct route but requires available cash.</li>
<li><strong>Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation (CVL).</strong> If your company is insolvent and closure is inevitable, you can initiate a CVL before the winding-up order is made. A CVL allows you to choose the insolvency practitioner, typically involves less scrutiny than compulsory liquidation, and may preserve employee access to statutory redundancy pay through the normal route.</li>
<li><strong>Company Voluntary Arrangement (CVA).</strong> A CVA is a formal agreement to repay creditors over time, supervised by an insolvency practitioner. It requires 75% creditor approval by value. If accepted, the petition is usually stayed. This route suits companies that are viable but temporarily unable to pay.</li>
<li><strong>Administration.</strong> If your company has a viable business that could be rescued or sold as a going concern, administration may be appropriate. An administrator is appointed to manage the company and can block further action by creditors, including winding-up petitions.</li>
</ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The window for these alternatives narrows as the process advances. Before the Gazette notice, most options are still available. After the bank freeze, funding any of them becomes the primary obstacle in our experience.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Not every company in your position qualifies for every route. We help directors identify which alternatives are realistic based on the company's actual financial position, not which ones sound appealing in theory.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The fork is simple but the timing is not: pay the debt and end the petition, initiate a CVL before the order is made, or face compulsory liquidation on the creditor's terms. CVL is available only before the winding-up order. Once the court grants it, that door closes permanently.</p>

<!-- wp:paragraph {"placeholder":"Panel content"} -->
<p>The Official Receiver is a civil servant employed by the Insolvency Service. After a winding-up order, the OR takes control of the company, investigates director conduct, collects and realises company assets, adjudicates creditor claims, and distributes proceeds in priority order. The OR may also report on whether you should be disqualified. You must cooperate fully with the OR.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- /wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"index":7,"theme":"#f8f8f8","collapsed":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eHow much does compulsory liquidation cost?\u003c/strong\u003e","toggleIcon":"plus","showOnlyOne":true} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph {"placeholder":"Panel content"} -->
<p>The petitioning creditor pays a court fee plus a deposit to the Official Receiver. You do not pay these costs directly, but your company's assets are used to cover the OR's costs and expenses before any distribution to creditors. If the company has no assets, the OR's costs are met from the deposit.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- /wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"index":8,"theme":"#f8f8f8","collapsed":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eCan I convert a compulsory liquidation to a CVL?\u003c/strong\u003e","toggleIcon":"plus","showOnlyOne":true} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph {"placeholder":"Panel content"} -->
<p>Once a winding-up order has been made, you cannot convert to a CVL. The CVL route is only available before the order is granted. This is one of the main reasons we tell directors to act quickly after receiving a petition: if voluntary liquidation is a better outcome, it must be initiated before the court hearing, not after.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- /wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"index":9,"theme":"#f8f8f8","collapsed":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eWhat happens to company debts after compulsory liquidation?\u003c/strong\u003e","toggleIcon":"plus","showOnlyOne":true} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph {"placeholder":"Panel content"} -->
<p>Company debts are dealt with through the liquidation process. The liquidator distributes available assets to creditors in the statutory priority order: secured creditors, preferential creditors (employees for certain claims), unsecured creditors with a prescribed part allocation, then remaining unsecured creditors.</p>

<p>Any debts not covered by available assets are written off when the company is dissolved. Personal guarantees are not extinguished by company liquidation and remain enforceable against you personally.</p>
