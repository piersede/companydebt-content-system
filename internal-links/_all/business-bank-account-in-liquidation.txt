<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The moment a winding-up petition is advertised in the London Gazette, most banks freeze the company account within 24 hours. Every outflow after that is potentially void under Section 127 of the Insolvency Act 1986.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If you're reading this because your account has just frozen, you have days, not weeks, to act.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If your account has just frozen, the immediate questions are: when did the bank freeze it, why, and what payments can still go through. The decisions you make in the next 48 hours determine whether your unsecured creditors get a few pence in the pound or nothing.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We handle calls from directors in exactly this situation every week, so the examples here come from real patterns we see, not from a textbook.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>It doesn't cover personal accounts or sole trader bankruptcy, both of which work differently. For the broader picture, see our guide to <a href="/liquidation/">how company liquidation works</a>.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Quick answer if your account just froze</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Petition advertised in the Gazette, account frozen:</strong> you cannot legitimately make payments. Do not attempt workarounds. Call an IP today and ask about a validation order for essential payments.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Petition filed but not yet advertised:</strong> the voluntary window is still open. We've seen directors enter CVL at this stage and retain meaningful control over the process.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Company in voluntary liquidation (CVL):</strong> the liquidator opens an estate account and takes control of existing funds. You no longer operate the account.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item --></ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->



<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">When and Why Banks Freeze Company Accounts</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Banks don't wait to be told. Most UK clearing banks run automated monitoring against the London Gazette, so the moment a winding-up petition against your company is advertised, the compliance team sees it within hours.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>From the bank's point of view, continuing to process payments on the account after the petition is a legal risk.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Any payment made between the petition and the winding-up order can be declared void by the court under Section 127, and the bank wants no part of that. The fastest way to avoid liability is to freeze the account immediately.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>In our experience, directors are often surprised by two things.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The first is how quickly the freeze happens: often within 24 hours of the Gazette entry, sometimes the same day.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The second is how complete the freeze is: incoming payments, direct debits, card terminals, and even salary payments to the director all stop at the same time.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If you want to know how administration interacts with this process, see our <a href="/company-administration/">administration</a> guide. The only transactions that may still complete are ones the bank is already committed to process, or transactions that fall within an existing validation order.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:group {"className":"cd-callout cd-calloutu002du002dkey-takeaway"} -->
<div class="wp-block-group cd-callout cd-callout--key-takeaway">
<p class="cd-callout__label">Key Takeaway</p>
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The speed of the bank freeze catches most directors off guard, and the completeness of it is worse. It is not just outgoing payments that stop. Incoming transfers and card receipts freeze too, which means a trading business can have live revenue that it physically cannot access.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The only legitimate route through this is a validation order, which buys time but requires an insolvency practitioner to prepare. Every day spent waiting rather than calling an IP is a day of the voluntary window lost.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
</div>
<!-- /wp:group -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What to Ask the Bank When You Call the Compliance Team</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Directors who call the bank's compliance team often don't know what to ask, and the bank won't volunteer the information. These are the three questions worth asking:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list {"ordered":true} -->
<ol class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Has the account been frozen because of a Gazette entry?</strong> If yes, get the date of the entry. This tells you exactly when the petition window started.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Will any committed payments still process?</strong> Direct debits and standing orders set up before the freeze may continue. Find out which ones, because anything that goes out is potentially void under Section 127.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>What is the bank's position on a validation order?</strong> Some banks will release specific payments under a validation order without requiring the order to name them individually. Others insist on a court order naming each payment. Knowing this in advance saves a wasted application.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item --></ol>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The bank will not give you legal advice but they will answer factual questions about the freeze. Get the answers in writing where possible.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Section 127: Why Post-Petition Payments Can Be Void</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Section 127 of the Insolvency Act 1986 is the key provision to understand. It says that in a compulsory winding-up, any disposition of company property made after the petition is presented is void, unless the court orders otherwise.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>In plain language: if you pay a supplier, a family member, or yourself after a petition is filed, the liquidator can later demand that money back from whoever received it.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The court treats the company's money as already belonging to the creditors collectively from the date of the petition, not the date of the winding-up order.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We see this trip directors up all the time. They reason that since the company is still trading, they should pay urgent bills to keep things running.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>What they don't realise is that each of those payments is at risk of being clawed back, and the director who authorised them can be held personally liable to restore the money.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The legal relevance of Section 127 applies specifically to compulsory liquidation following a petition.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>In a Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation, the equivalent principle is that the liquidator has control of all company assets from the date the resolution is passed, and unauthorised disposals afterwards can be challenged.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:group {"className":"cd-callout cd-calloutu002du002dlegal-exposure"} -->
<div class="wp-block-group cd-callout cd-callout--legal-exposure">
<p class="cd-callout__label">Legal Exposure</p>
<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Section 127 makes every post-petition payment a potential personal liability for the director who authorised it. The safeguard is not paying nothing. It is getting a validation order first. Without one, even legitimate-looking payments to keep the business running can be reversed by the liquidator.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
</div>
<!-- /wp:group -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Validation Orders: How to Keep Essential Payments Moving</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If a petition has been filed but the winding-up order has not yet been made, the company can apply to the court for a validation order authorising specific payments. This is the formal route for keeping the business trading while the petition is dealt with.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A validation order typically covers things like:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>Payroll for employees</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>HMRC tax liabilities that are falling due</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>Essential supplier payments where stopping them would collapse the business before the petition is resolved</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>Payments necessary to realise a specific asset (for example, to release goods from a warehouse)</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item --></ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The application needs to demonstrate that the proposed payments are in the interests of creditors as a whole rather than the director or a favoured supplier. The court will usually want to see cash-flow evidence, a list of payments covered, and ideally support from the petitioner.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We have seen validation orders granted quickly when the application is properly prepared and the case is clear. We have also seen them refused when the court suspected the order was being sought to prefer one creditor over another. The difference is usually in the quality of the application.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>What a strong validation order application typically contains:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>A witness statement from the director explaining the company's position and why the payments are necessary</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>A 13-week cash-flow forecast demonstrating that the proposed payments preserve value for creditors as a whole</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>Supporting evidence: payroll register, HMRC liability schedule, supplier list with amounts and due dates</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>A letter of consent from the petitioning creditor where possible (this materially improves the chance of approval)</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item --></ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Courts usually list these for hearing within 5 to 10 business days of filing. The application should be prepared by an insolvency practitioner or insolvency solicitor, because the court will scrutinise it closely.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:group {"className":"cd-process-steps"} -->
<div class="wp-block-group cd-process-steps">
<!-- wp:list {"ordered":true} -->
<ol><li>Call a licensed insolvency practitioner the same day, because the voluntary window is time-limited once the petition is advertised.</li><li>Identify which payments are essential: payroll, HMRC liabilities, and any supplier whose failure to pay would collapse the business immediately.</li><li>Prepare a witness statement and 13-week cash-flow forecast showing payments are in creditors' collective interest.</li><li>Obtain consent from the petitioning creditor where possible, because this materially improves approval prospects.</li><li>File the application; courts typically list a hearing within 5–10 business days.</li></ol>
<!-- /wp:list -->
</div>
<!-- /wp:group -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Bank Set-Off: What If We Owe the Bank Money?</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If your company owes money to the same bank that holds its accounts, the bank has a right of set-off.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>In practice, this means that the moment the company enters liquidation, the bank can combine the balances: your credit balance is offset against your loan, overdraft, or facility.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The effect is that the cash in the current account may disappear into the overdraft or loan balance the moment liquidation starts, leaving nothing for the liquidator to distribute. This is legal and the bank is exercising a contractual and statutory right under the Insolvency Rules.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The practical consequence is that we advise you to keep an eye on which bank your operating accounts are with, relative to your borrowing facilities.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A company whose main operating cash sits with the same bank as its overdraft is more exposed on day one of liquidation than one whose cash is separated. Where set-off leaves the estate empty, our guide to <a href="/liquidation/liquidating-a-company-with-no-assets-or-bank-account-uk/">liquidating a company with no assets</a> explains how the process still works.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What Happens to Secured, Trust, and Client Funds</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Not all money in a company's bank accounts is the company's money. If the company holds funds on trust or for clients, those funds should not form part of the estate available to creditors. The liquidator has a duty to identify and ring-fence them.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Examples of funds that are usually not part of the liquidation estate:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>Money held on trust in a client account (typically regulated professions like solicitors, estate agents, and travel agents)</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>Deposits taken for future goods or services that are clearly ring-fenced</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>PAYE and National Insurance deducted from employee wages but not yet paid to HMRC (though this is contested and HMRC may claim it as a preferential debt)</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item --></ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Your duty as director here is to flag these funds to the liquidator at the earliest opportunity, with clear paperwork showing the trust arrangement. If you mix client money with trading funds, or if the trust claim is weak, the liquidator may treat the entire balance as a company asset.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Where the Cash Goes: the Statutory Distribution Waterfall</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Once the liquidator has realised all available assets and transferred them to a liquidation estate account, the money is distributed according to a strict statutory order set out in the Insolvency Act 1986 and the Insolvency Rules 2016. Where the company owns premises, our guide to <a href="/liquidation/company-property-and-real-estate-in-liquidation/">what happens to company property</a> explains how those assets are dealt with.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The order of priority is:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list {"ordered":true} -->
<ol class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Secured creditors with fixed charges</strong> (typically mortgages over specific assets), paid from the sale of the secured asset</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Costs and expenses of the liquidation</strong> (the liquidator's fees and related costs)</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Preferential creditors</strong> (employees for wages up to statutory limits, HMRC for certain taxes from 1 December 2020 under the Finance Act 2020)</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Prescribed part</strong> set aside for unsecured creditors out of floating charge realisations</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Secured creditors with floating charges</strong></li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Unsecured creditors</strong> (most trade creditors, suppliers, unsecured loans)</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Shareholders</strong> (only if there's anything left, which in an insolvent liquidation almost never happens)</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item --></ol>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>This waterfall is the reason we have to correct a common misconception. Directors often assume that if they pay off a specific creditor before entering liquidation, that creditor "at least got paid."</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>In reality, a payment made in the months before liquidation that disrupts this statutory order can be reversed as a preference under Section 239 of the Insolvency Act 1986. The liquidator can claw it back and redistribute it through the proper waterfall.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Costly Mistakes Directors Make With Frozen Accounts</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We see the same four mistakes repeatedly when directors respond to a frozen account without advice. If your account has just frozen, check this list before you do anything.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Moving money to a personal account "to keep it safe."</strong> This is the classic preference trap. The liquidator will find it, clawback will follow, and the director will face personal liability for the same amount plus potentially wrongful trading and misfeasance claims under Section 212 of the Insolvency Act 1986.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Opening a new account at a different bank.</strong> Any funds moved across are still company funds and still subject to Section 127. The new account will typically be frozen within days as well, once the bank notices the related company name on the Gazette.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Paying favoured creditors.</strong> Directors sometimes try to "settle" with key suppliers or family members before the process completes. Under Sections 239 and 423, these payments are reversible and the recipient may be required to repay.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Ignoring the petition in the hope it goes away.</strong> It does not. Once a petition is advertised, the bank freeze happens automatically.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Waiting for the court hearing to "see what happens" means losing every day of the voluntary window, which is the only period where the director still has control over which route the company takes.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Scotland and Northern Ireland: Procedural Differences</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The substance of what happens to a business bank account in liquidation is similar across the UK, but procedural differences exist:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>In <strong>Scotland</strong>, the equivalent process is administered under the Bankruptcy (Scotland) Act 2016 for personal insolvency and the Insolvency Act 1986 (as applied to Scotland) for corporate insolvency. The terminology differs: winding-up petitions become petitions to the Court of Session or Sheriff Court, and the liquidator role is often held by an interim liquidator first.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>In <strong>Northern Ireland</strong>, corporate insolvency is governed by the Insolvency (Northern Ireland) Order 1989, which is materially similar to the Insolvency Act 1986 but with separate court procedures.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item --></ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>In both jurisdictions, the bank's response to a petition or winding-up order is the same in practice: freeze your account fast, then wait for the office-holder to take control.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If your company is registered in Scotland or Northern Ireland, you should still call us, because the substance of the advice is the same.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->



<h2 class="wp-block-heading">FAQs on Business Bank Accounts in Liquidation</h2>

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-block {"blockID":"79379-bank-account-faqs","theme":"#f1f1f1","collapsed":true,"titleColor":"#000000"} -->
<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"theme":"#f1f1f1","collapsed":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eHow quickly does a bank freeze a company account after a petition?\u003c/strong\u003e"} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph {"placeholder":"Panel content"} -->
<p>Usually within 24 hours of the petition being advertised in the London Gazette, and often the same day. UK clearing banks run automated monitoring against the Gazette so their compliance teams see the entry within hours. The bank's motivation is to avoid being caught by Section 127 of the Insolvency Act 1986, which makes post-petition disposals potentially void.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- /wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"index":1,"theme":"#f1f1f1","collapsed":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eCan I move the company's money before the account is frozen?\u003c/strong\u003e"} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph {"placeholder":"Panel content"} -->
<p>No, and attempting to do so is one of the fastest routes to personal liability as a director. Any disposal of company funds after a winding-up petition has been filed is potentially void under Section 127.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A disposal made in the months before liquidation to "keep money safe" is likely to be reversed as a preference or transaction at undervalue. The liquidator will investigate every unusual transfer.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- /wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"index":2,"theme":"#f1f1f1","collapsed":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eWhat is a validation order and how do I get one?\u003c/strong\u003e"} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph {"placeholder":"Panel content"} -->
<p>A validation order is a court order authorising specific payments after a petition has been filed but before a winding-up order is made. It covers things like payroll, HMRC liabilities, and essential supplier payments.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The application needs to show that the proposed payments are in the interests of creditors as a whole rather than the director alone. A licensed insolvency practitioner or insolvency solicitor should prepare the application because the court scrutinises these closely.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- /wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"index":3,"theme":"#f1f1f1","collapsed":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eWhat happens to employees' wages if the account freezes?\u003c/strong\u003e"} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph {"placeholder":"Panel content"} -->
<p>Unpaid wages immediately before liquidation become preferential claims for employees up to a statutory cap.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If the company cannot pay them, employees can claim unpaid wages, holiday pay, and statutory notice pay from the Redundancy Payments Service, which covers these from the National Insurance Fund. The director should not attempt to pay wages out of a frozen account without a validation order.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- /wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"index":4,"theme":"#f1f1f1","collapsed":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eDoes bank set-off apply if I borrow from a different bank?\u003c/strong\u003e"} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph {"placeholder":"Panel content"} -->
<p>No. Set-off only applies to debts between the same parties: the bank that holds your loan or overdraft against the balance you hold with that same bank. If your operating account is with Bank A and your borrowing is with Bank B, set-off doesn't apply between them, and the cash in Bank A forms part of the liquidation estate.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- /wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"index":5,"theme":"#f1f1f1","collapsed":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eWhat happens to the account once a CVL starts?\u003c/strong\u003e"} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph {"placeholder":"Panel content"} -->
<p>The liquidator takes control of all company bank accounts. They will typically open a new estate account in their own name as liquidator, transfer the existing balances, and close or freeze the trading accounts. From that point the director has no operational control over the company's finances. Any attempted payment by a director after the CVL resolution is passed is unauthorised.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- /wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"index":6,"theme":"#f1f1f1","collapsed":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eHow long does it take to get the money back once liquidation completes?\u003c/strong\u003e"} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph {"placeholder":"Panel content"} -->
<p>For creditors, distributions typically happen 6 to 18 months after the liquidation starts, depending on how complex the asset realisation is. The statutory priority waterfall determines who gets paid and in what order. Unsecured creditors usually receive very little in an insolvent liquidation, often pence in the pound, and in some cases nothing at all.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- /wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"index":7,"theme":"#f1f1f1","collapsed":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eCan the liquidator investigate transactions before the petition?\u003c/strong\u003e"} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph {"placeholder":"Panel content"} -->
<p>Yes. The liquidator can look back at company transactions in the months (and sometimes years) before liquidation to identify preferences, transactions at undervalue, and transactions defrauding creditors under Sections 238, 239, and 423 of the Insolvency Act 1986.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The look-back period is longer for transactions with connected parties (for example, family members or related companies) than for arm's-length transactions. Any suspicious transfer is likely to be unwound.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
