
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Carillion’s collapse through an application for compulsory liquidation has sent shock waves through the UK economy. Carillion is much more than just one of the UK’s largest construction companies. As a provider of many other services that help the country function, the loss of this giant will be felt well beyond its construction roots.</span></p>



<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The decision to effectively declare itself bust leaves 43,000 jobs around the world hanging in the balance. For now, the government has committed to funding public service contracts, and the Insolvency Service has urged all 19,500 UK staff to turn up for work as normal. But there are no guarantees how long that will last. </span></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The Ripple Effect</h2>



<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">One of the biggest causes of insolvencies in the UK is the closure of other firms in the supply chain. When a major customer or contractor goes bust, it’s inevitable that those who rely on those businesses will feel the effect. With the collapse of a sprawling business like Carillion, this news will make waves rather than ripples in the wider supply chain and leave potentially thousands of creditors and subcontractors out of pocket. Many could be forced out of business. &nbsp;</span></p>



<p>For now, the government has requested supply chain businesses and contractors to continue working on the premise that they will be paid for their work during the liquidations.As to money that was owed by Carillion prior to the insolvency, creditors will be paid in order of priority once the insolvency practitioners realise Carillion&#8217;s assets.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What Went Wrong?</h2>



<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Carillion ran into financial difficulties last year after issuing three profit warnings in five months. It also wrote down more than £1bn on the dwindling value of contracts in the UK, Canada and the Middle East.</span></p>



<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Last-ditch talks with the firm’s leaders collapsed last week as the government refused to hand over the £20 million required to encourage further backing from the banks. With Downing Street not willing to take part in the bailout, the banks, headed by HSBC, Santander and Barclays, refused a restructuring plan, and the liquidation process was subsequently announced.</span></p>



<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Unlike an administration, where a firm continues to trade while attempts are made to find a buyer, it has been suggested that there is very little value within the business. This led to the decision to shut it down. Now, an employee of the government’s Insolvency Service, advised by six ‘special managers’ from PwC, has assumed control of the company to sell its assets, deal with creditors’ claims and investigate the cause of the failure.</span></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What’s the Likely Impact on Carillion&#8217;s Subcontractors <b>&nbsp;</b></h2>



<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Subcontractors caught up in the collapse of Carillion will now have to deal with the fallout. When a contractor becomes insolvent mid-project, the impact is far-reaching. Sub-contractors face non-payment and uncertainty as to their continued involvement in the project. Thousands of subcontractors have already been left in limbo over retention payments owed by Carillion, with many small firms awaiting payments from the collapsed contractor totalling hundreds of thousands of pounds. &nbsp;</span></p>



<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Estimates of the amount of money held by Carillion in retentions could be as high as £1bn, and many electrical and other specialist contractors risk losing millions. Those subcontractors will now have to join the long list of unsecured creditors. Even if money does become available, they are only likely to receive a small proportion of the money they are owed. This presents a clear danger to many small businesses and means it’s not a question of ‘if’ subcontractors will be forced into insolvency, but ‘how many’. </span></p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Is Your Business Insolvent?</h2>



<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">As soon as a business becomes insolvent, the directors&#8217; obligations change. The business must then be run in the best interests of its creditors, and that brings certain responsibilities. Failure to meet these obligations could lead to accusations of misconduct and the various penalties that apply.</span></p>



<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">For that reason, it’s essential you recognise the signs that your business is insolvent. The two tests for insolvency are:</span></p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><span style="font-weight: 400;">The business cannot pay its debts when they become due for payment;</span></li>



<li><span style="font-weight: 400;">The value of its liabilities exceeds the value of its assets.</span></li>
</ul>



<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If either or both of these tests are true of your business then it is an indication of insolvency and you should contact a turnaround professional to discuss your options immediately. For a quick check, our </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">Insolvency Test</span><span style="font-weight: 400;"> can help you identify your company’s situation.</span></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What are the Options for Insolvent Subcontractors?</h2>



<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">When cash flow has been disrupted in the short term, but the underlying business is sound, trading out could be an option for you.</span> Trading out is a common way to address the problem of negative cash flow<span style="font-weight: 400;">. There are two approaches you can take:</span></p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><span style="font-weight: 400;">Informal negotiations with creditors which allow you to reduce the repayments over the short-term or extend repayment terms to reduce the pressure;</span></li>



<li><span style="font-weight: 400;">Proposing a </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">Company Voluntary Arrangement (CVA)</span><span style="font-weight: 400;"> is a more formal approach to creditor negotiations. A specialist turnaround practitioner will help you draw up a proposal to repay your creditors all or part of the money they are owed over a period of up to five years. </span></li>
</ul>



<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Trading out can give you the breathing space you need to get back on your feet. The guidance of a turnaround professional will make a big difference in the likely success of this approach, instilling greater confidence in your creditors and helping you create proposals that creditors like HMRC will consider. &nbsp;</span></p>
