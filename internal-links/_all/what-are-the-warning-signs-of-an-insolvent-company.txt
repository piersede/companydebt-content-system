<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>You are sitting with three quarters of management accounts open on the laptop and the pattern is hard to miss. Cash out has exceeded cash in for nine consecutive months.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>PAYE is a fortnight late, your factor has quietly trimmed the advance rate from 85% to 70%, and two of your regular suppliers have moved you to pro-forma. The question you are really asking is not "is this bad?" but "is this legally insolvent, and what does that mean for me personally?"</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Insolvency is not a feeling, it is a statutory test. Section 123 of the Insolvency Act 1986 defines it in two ways.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The company cannot pay its debts as they fall due (the cash-flow test), or its liabilities exceed its assets on a "point of no return" analysis (the balance-sheet test, per <em>BNY Corporate Trustee Services v Eurosail</em> [2013] UKSC 28). You cross that line well before a winding-up petition lands.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The warning signs below are the ones we see first when a director rings us, in the order they usually appear in the case files we take on. The sooner you recognise where you are on that curve, the more options you still have, and the lower the risk of personal liability under section 214 wrongful trading.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The First Warning Signs of an Insolvent Company: Cash Flow</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The earliest indicator is the Friday BACS run you start to dread. You have the list of suppliers open in one tab and the bank balance in another, and you are sequencing payments by who will shout loudest rather than who is contractually due. That is not a liquidity hiccup, it is a structural mismatch between working capital and obligations.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Concrete patterns to watch for:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>The overdraft is at its ceiling every month-end rather than only in the dip before a big receipt.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>You are paying rolling PAYE from current-month sales rather than from funds set aside the month before.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>You have started asking suppliers for 60-day terms on invoices you used to clear in 14.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>Directors' loans are going in, not coming out, and the board has stopped discussing when they will be repaid.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item --></ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>None of those is fatal on its own. Three of them together, running for two quarters, means you are in cash-flow insolvency under section 123(1)(e) whether you have filed for anything or not.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>See our guide to <a href="https://companydebt.com/company-cash-flow-problems/">company cash flow problems</a> for the triage sequence we run with directors on a first call. In the cases we take on at that stage, we usually find the board has not modelled a 13-week cash-flow in three months, and the director is treating the overdraft ceiling as working capital rather than as a debt facility.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Warning Signs of Insolvent Trading: The Creditor Letters Change Tone</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Creditor pressure has a recognisable arc. First come reminder statements that look like the old ones. Then comes a "final demand" printed in red, usually with a seven-day deadline that is actually fourteen.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Then comes the statutory demand: a specific legal instrument under section 123(1)(a) of the Insolvency Act 1986, claiming a debt over £750, with 21 days for the company to pay, secure, or compromise before the creditor can petition to wind up.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A statutory demand is not a bluff. Ignore it and on day 22 your creditor can apply to the court for a winding-up petition on the ground that the company is deemed unable to pay its debts. Our note on <a href="https://companydebt.com/company-cash-flow-problems/what-is-a-statutory-demand-against-a-company/">statutory demands against a company</a> sets out the defences and the tight deadline for challenging one.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The other tonal shift happens in the phone calls. Once a creditor's in-house credit team hands your file to external solicitors or a commercial debt collector, the correspondence becomes standardised.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Bland letterhead, formal language, threats quoted verbatim from pre-action protocols. The danger sign there is not the letter itself. It is that the human relationship you had with the accounts manager is gone, and we rarely see that relationship come back once the file has been outsourced.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">HMRC Arrears: The Most Lethal Warning Sign of Insolvency</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>HMRC is the creditor most companies treat casually and it is the one most likely to push a viable business into compulsory liquidation. Three HMRC-specific indicators every director should audit monthly:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>PAYE or VAT slipping by more than one filing period.</strong> One missed quarter is a cash-flow wobble. Two missed quarters is a pattern, and HMRC's late-payment interest now runs at the Bank of England base rate plus 4 percentage points.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>A Time to Pay arrangement that has been refused or defaulted.</strong> HMRC will entertain a TTP once, sometimes twice. A third approach after a broken previous arrangement rarely lands.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>A Field Force visit or a "seven-day letter" from Debt Management.</strong> These are the pre-petition steps. By the time you see one, the file has been escalated internally.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item --></ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Since the Finance Act 2020 reinstated Crown preference on 1 December 2020, HMRC ranks as a secondary preferential creditor for VAT, PAYE, employee NIC, and CIS. That changes the economics of any rescue.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A floating-charge lender now sits behind HMRC for those taxes, so the usual "bank will lend against the book" escape route is narrower than it was. Our note on <a href="https://companydebt.com/hmrc/hmrc-threatening-letters/">HMRC threatening letters</a> unpacks the typical escalation path.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Warning Signs of Insolvency in the Ledger and Books</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Two diagnostic lines on the trial balance tell an IP more than a page of commentary. First, the aged debtors report: if debtors over 90 days have grown as a percentage of total debtors for three months running, you have a collections problem that is being masked by new billing.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Second, the aged creditors. If the list is growing and the oldest entries are HMRC and the pension scheme, you are at the stage where every new sale makes the hole deeper, not shallower.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The quieter signal is the one directors miss. The bookkeeper pushing management accounts back a month, then two, then producing figures that always look "provisional". Late management accounts are rarely incompetence; in our casework they are almost always a coping mechanism for a finance team that knows what the numbers will show and does not want to be the messenger.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If you have not seen a full management pack (P&amp;L, balance sheet, cash-flow forecast) in the last six weeks, that is itself a warning sign. A director who signs a set of accounts in those conditions, and who ought to have known the position was worse, is the director a liquidator writes to under section 214.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">When Suppliers and Insurers Move First: Warning Signs from the Outside</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The earliest external signal is trade credit drying up. Your wholesaler quietly halves your credit limit. The trade insurer who used to cover your largest customer rings to say they are reviewing the cover.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Your invoice discounter moves from 85% advance to 70%, then to a daily notification regime where every invoice is pre-approved. These are not administrative changes. They are credit decisions taken by people who have seen your Experian file and your filed accounts.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>You will often get the news from a sales rep, not from a formal letter. The rep says "head office has pulled credit" and moves on. It is the director's job to treat that call as a data point on par with a Companies House filing, not a personality problem to negotiate around.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Directors tend to focus on the noisy creditor: the HMRC envelope, the supplier threatening court. The dangerous creditor is usually the quiet one. The landlord who stops responding to emails because they have instructed surveyors to value the unit for forfeiture, or the bank relationship manager who has been replaced by "the recoveries team" without announcement.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The Legal Warning Signs of an Insolvent Company: CCJs and Petitions</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A County Court Judgment (CCJ) against the company is a public event. It appears on the Registry Trust record within days, credit agencies pick it up within a week, and your suppliers' credit-risk teams will see it before you have finished deciding whether to appeal. Two or more unsatisfied CCJs is, in practice, how most trade creditors' systems reclassify you as high-risk.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The next step up is the winding-up petition. Once presented, two things happen almost immediately. The petition is advertised in <em>The Gazette</em>, which banks monitor electronically.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Within hours of the advert, your bank will freeze the company account because of section 127 of the Insolvency Act 1986: every disposition of company property after presentation of the petition is void unless validated by the court.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>In the cases we take on after Gazette advertisement, the director is usually ringing from a landline because the Barclays app has stopped working. Our guide to <a href="https://companydebt.com/county-court-judgements/">CCJs against a limited company</a> walks through the five working days between petition and Gazette in detail.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Bailiffs at the door are the late-stage symptom rather than the early warning. By the time a High Court Enforcement Officer is attending premises, a judgment has been obtained and a writ of control issued.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If that is where you are, the window for a controlled insolvency process has narrowed sharply. A conversation with a licensed IP within 24 hours is not a precaution, it is a necessity.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Warning Signs of Insolvency and the Duty Shift Under Sequana</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Directors running a solvent company owe their duties to the company's members, as codified in section 172 of the Companies Act 2006. Once the company is on the brink of insolvency, the Supreme Court in <em>BTI 2014 LLC v Sequana SA</em> [2022] UKSC 25 confirmed that directors must instead have regard to the interests of creditors as a whole.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The duty shifts before formal insolvency, and the threshold is "insolvency is imminent or probable". A liquidator does not read board minutes to find what was decided, they read them to find what was not.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The practical consequence is this. The moment you recognise two or three of the warning signs above, you need to minute that recognition, record the board's assessment, and start taking decisions with creditor interests weighted alongside shareholder ones. A liquidator reading board minutes eighteen months later is looking for exactly that shift in framing, not for evidence of heroism.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Section 214 of the Insolvency Act 1986 provides the bite. If at some point before winding-up you knew, or ought reasonably to have concluded, that there was no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvent liquidation, and you did not take every step to minimise the loss to creditors, you can be ordered to contribute personally to the estate.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The clock on "ought to have concluded" starts running the day the warning signs became unmistakable, not the day you rang an IP. Our page on the <a href="https://companydebt.com/insolvency/insolvency-test/">statutory insolvency test</a> explains how IPs reconstruct that timeline.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Your Next Step on the Warning Signs of an Insolvent Company</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The honest verdict is this. If you have recognised one warning sign you have a cash-flow problem. If you have recognised three or more and they have persisted for two quarters, you have a directors' duties problem. The first is commercial and you can probably trade through it. The second is legal and it does not resolve by itself.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Directors split into two camps on that call. If your company has a genuine prospect of recovery, a live order book, a lender willing to re-facilitate, a one-off loss that is behind you, your next step is a documented board review with advice on file. That paper trail is the section 214 defence.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If you cannot honestly tell yourself there is a reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvent liquidation, your next step is a formal conversation with a licensed IP this week, not next month.</p>

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">FAQs on Warning Signs of an Insolvent Company</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-block {"blockID":"68123-warning-signs-faqs","theme":"#f1f1f1","collapsed":true,"titleColor":"#000000"} -->
<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"theme":"#f1f1f1","collapsed":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eWhat is the legal definition of an insolvent company in the UK?\u003c/strong\u003e"} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph {"placeholder":"Panel content"} -->
<p>Section 123 of the Insolvency Act 1986 sets the tests. A company is "unable to pay its debts" if it has failed to satisfy a statutory demand over £750 for 21 days, if an execution against it is unsatisfied, if it cannot pay its debts as they fall due (cash-flow test), or if liabilities exceed assets on a "point of no return" basis (balance-sheet test per <em>Eurosail</em>). Any one limb triggers the Act.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- /wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"index":1,"theme":"#f1f1f1","collapsed":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eWhat are the earliest warning signs of insolvency?\u003c/strong\u003e"} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph {"placeholder":"Panel content"} -->
<p>Persistent overdraft use at the ceiling, PAYE or VAT slipping by a filing period, aged debtors over 90 days growing for three months running, and suppliers withdrawing credit. Any two of those persisting across two quarters is a directors' duties issue, not a cash-flow wobble.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- /wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"index":2,"theme":"#f1f1f1","collapsed":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eWhat happens if a director ignores the warning signs of insolvency?\u003c/strong\u003e"} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph {"placeholder":"Panel content"} -->
<p>Section 214 of the Insolvency Act 1986 allows a liquidator to pursue the director personally for the increase in net deficiency caused by continued trading after the point at which a reasonably diligent director ought to have concluded there was no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvent liquidation. Disqualification under the CDDA 1986 is a separate parallel risk.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- /wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"index":3,"theme":"#f1f1f1","collapsed":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eDoes a statutory demand mean the company is insolvent?\u003c/strong\u003e"} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph {"placeholder":"Panel content"} -->
<p>Not automatically, but an unpaid statutory demand over £750 that has been outstanding for 21 days is deemed evidence of inability to pay debts under section 123(1)(a). The creditor can then present a winding-up petition. The time to challenge the demand is within 18 days of service, not after.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- /wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"index":4,"theme":"#f1f1f1","collapsed":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eWhen does the Sequana duty shift kick in?\u003c/strong\u003e"} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph {"placeholder":"Panel content"} -->
<p>The Supreme Court in <em>BTI v Sequana</em> [2022] held that directors must have regard to creditors' interests when insolvency is imminent or probable, or when an insolvent liquidation or administration is probable. It is not tied to the formal section 123 threshold; in practice it bites as soon as the warning signs are clustering.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- /wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block -->

<!-- wp:ub/content-toggle-panel-block {"index":5,"theme":"#f1f1f1","collapsed":true,"titleColor":"#000000","panelTitle":"\u003cstrong\u003eCan a company trade through insolvency?\u003c/strong\u003e"} -->
<!-- wp:paragraph {"placeholder":"Panel content"} -->
<p>Sometimes, but only with a documented board decision that trading on will minimise creditor loss and with professional advice on file. Without both, continued trading after the warning signs are clear becomes the factual basis for a section 214 wrongful trading claim. A CVA or a moratorium is often the formal way to trade through.</p>
