<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The moment you should stop trading is not when the bailiffs arrive or when the bank freezes your account. It is the moment you know, or should know, that your company cannot avoid insolvent liquidation. Every day you trade beyond that point increases your personal liability.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We speak to directors every week who kept trading for months past that point. They always have a reason: a big invoice was about to land, a new contract was close, they thought one more month would turn it around.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Sometimes it does. More often, the extra months add £20,000 or £50,000 to the company's deficiency, and the liquidator calculates that increase as the starting point for a wrongful trading claim against you personally.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We have written this page to help you identify the point at which continued trading becomes dangerous, explain the legal framework that creates your personal exposure, and set out what you should do when you reach that point.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Quick Answer: When a Director Must Stop Trading</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>You should stop trading when there is no reasonable prospect of the company avoiding insolvent liquidation. That is the statutory test under section 214 of the Insolvency Act 1986.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The test is not whether you personally believed the company could survive. It is whether a reasonably diligent person in your position, with the general knowledge, skill, and experience that you have (or should have), would have concluded that insolvent liquidation was unavoidable.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We tell every director who asks: if you are choosing which creditors to pay each week, if you are relying on next month's revenue to cover this month's obligations, or if you are borrowing to pay tax debts, you have almost certainly passed that point. The honest question is not "can we survive?" but "when did survival stop being realistic?"</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The Warning Signs You Should Not Ignore</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We find that the tipping point is rarely a single dramatic event. It is usually a pattern that builds over weeks or months. These are the signals we see most often in companies that continued trading too long:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>You are choosing which creditors to pay each month.</strong> If you cannot pay everyone, you are insolvent. Prioritising some creditors over others is not cash management; it is creditor triage, and it is evidence that the company cannot meet its obligations as they fall due.</li>
<li><strong>HMRC debts are accumulating.</strong> Falling behind on PAYE, VAT, or Corporation Tax is one of the clearest indicators. These debts compound quickly, and HMRC escalates faster than most trade creditors. We see directors treat HMRC as the creditor of last resort. In reality, HMRC is the creditor most likely to force compulsory liquidation.</li>
<li><strong>You are funding the business from personal savings.</strong> Putting personal money into the company to cover shortfalls is a sign that the business cannot sustain itself. It also increases your personal exposure, because the money you inject may not be recoverable.</li>
<li><strong>Creditors are issuing statutory demands or county court claims.</strong> If creditors are escalating from letters to legal action, the informal phase is over. A statutory demand is 21 days from a winding-up petition. A county court judgement gives the creditor enforcement powers immediately.</li>
<li><strong>Your accountant or bookkeeper has raised concerns.</strong> If the person who sees your numbers is telling you the position is unsustainable, listen. We see directors dismiss their accountant's warnings because they believe they understand the business better. The liquidator will ask your accountant what they told you, and when.</li>
<li><strong>You are delaying payroll or paying staff late.</strong> If you cannot pay your employees on time, the company's cash position has deteriorated to a critical level. Your staff will leave, and the operational disruption will accelerate the decline.</li>
</ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The Legal Framework: Section 214 Wrongful Trading</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:group {"className":"cd-callout cd-callout\u002d\u002dlegal-exposure"} -->
<div class="wp-block-group cd-callout cd-callout--legal-exposure">
  <p class="cd-callout__label">Legal Exposure</p>
  <p class="cd-callout__title">Personal Contribution Liability Under s.214 IA 1986</p>
  <!-- wp:paragraph -->
  <p>Section 214 of the Insolvency Act 1986 imposes personal liability on any director who continued to trade when they knew, or ought to have concluded, that there was no reasonable prospect of the company avoiding insolvent liquidation.</p>
  <!-- /wp:paragraph -->
  <!-- wp:paragraph -->
  <p>If the liquidator establishes that threshold was crossed, the court can order the director to contribute to the company's assets by an amount equal to the increase in net deficiency between the date they should have stopped and the date of liquidation. There is no cap on contribution orders; we have seen six-figure personal liability arise from a few months of continued trading.</p>
  <!-- /wp:paragraph -->
</div>
<!-- /wp:group -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Section 214 of the Insolvency Act 1986 creates personal liability for directors who continued to trade when they knew, or should have known, that there was no reasonable prospect of the company avoiding insolvent liquidation.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The test has two limbs. The first is subjective: what did you actually know? The second is objective: what would a reasonably diligent person with your general knowledge, skill, and experience have concluded? The objective limb is the one that catches directors, because "I did not realise" is not a defence if a competent person in your position would have realised.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If the liquidator establishes that you crossed the threshold, the court can order you to contribute personally to the company's assets. The contribution is calculated based on the increase in the company's net deficiency between the date you should have stopped trading and the date the company actually entered liquidation.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We have seen contribution orders ranging from a few thousand pounds to six-figure sums, depending on how long the director continued trading and how much additional debt was incurred.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The Supreme Court's decision in <em>BTI 2014 LLC v Sequana SA</em> [2022] also confirmed that directors must consider creditor interests as the company nears insolvency, sharpening the duty under section 172 Companies Act 2006.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>There is one defence: section 214(3) provides that you are not liable if you took every step a reasonably diligent person would take to minimise the potential loss to creditors.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>In practice, this means seeking professional advice, acting on that advice, and stopping trading if advised to do so. We cannot overstate how important that first conversation with an insolvency practitioner is for your personal defence. Our guide on <a href="/liquidation/seek-insolvency-advice-before-missing-payments/">getting insolvency advice early</a> explains why timing that call before a missed payment matters so much.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What "Stop Trading" Actually Means</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:group {"className":"cd-callout cd-callout\u002d\u002ddirectors-miss"} -->
<div class="wp-block-group cd-callout cd-callout--directors-miss">
  <p class="cd-callout__label">What Most Directors Miss</p>
  <p class="cd-callout__title">The Section 214(3) Defence Requires You to Have Acted, Not Just Hoped</p>
  <!-- wp:paragraph -->
  <p>Many directors believe that seeking one piece of professional advice, or having a conversation with their accountant, satisfies the s.214(3) defence. It does not. The defence requires you to have taken every step a reasonably diligent person would take to minimise creditor losses; and to have acted on that advice.</p>
  <!-- /wp:paragraph -->
  <!-- wp:paragraph -->
  <p>A director who called an insolvency practitioner, received a recommendation to enter <a href="/liquidation/creditors-voluntary-liquidation/">CVL</a>, and then continued trading for another three months cannot rely on the initial call as a shield. The defence is measured by what you did, not what you were told.</p>
  <!-- /wp:paragraph -->
</div>
<!-- /wp:group -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Stopping trading does not mean locking the door and walking away. It means taking formal steps to close the company in an orderly way through a proper insolvency process.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>In practice, "stop trading" means:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list {"ordered":true} -->
<ol class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Seek professional advice immediately.</strong> Contact a licensed insolvency practitioner and explain the company's position. They will assess whether the company is viable or whether formal insolvency is the right step.</li>
<li><strong>Stop incurring new debts.</strong> Do not place new orders, take on new credit, or make commitments the company cannot honour. Every new debt you incur after the tipping point is a debt the liquidator will examine.</li>
<li><strong>Stop making selective payments.</strong> Pay normal operating expenses in the ordinary course (rent, utilities, wages for work done) but do not settle favoured creditors, repay your own loan account, or make payments to connected parties. Preferences under s.239 IA 1986 can be unwound by the liquidator.</li>
<li><strong>Preserve the company's assets.</strong> Do not sell assets below market value, transfer assets to family members, or remove property from company premises.</li>
<li><strong>Initiate a CVL if advised to do so.</strong> A voluntary liquidation demonstrates that you recognised the problem and acted responsibly. This is the strongest evidence you can present in your defence against a wrongful trading claim.</li>
</ol>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The Cost of Continuing Too Long</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We want to be concrete about what continued trading costs you personally, because the numbers are often larger than directors expect.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If your company's net deficiency is £100,000 when you should have stopped trading and £150,000 when the company actually enters liquidation, the liquidator can seek a personal contribution order of £50,000 against you. That is £50,000 from your personal assets, not from the company. If the increase is £200,000, the order could be £200,000.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>On top of the contribution order, you face potential director disqualification under the Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986. Wrongful trading is one of the grounds the Insolvency Service cites most frequently in disqualification proceedings.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A disqualification order of 5 to 10 years prevents you from acting as a director of any company, which for many owner-directors is effectively a ban from their profession. Our guide to <a href="/liquidation/what-happens-to-directors-in-liquidation/">where directors stand in liquidation</a> sets out the full range of consequences a director faces once the company closes.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We see directors weigh the cost of stopping now (loss of the business, employee redundancies, personal embarrassment) against the cost of continuing (additional debt, wrongful trading exposure, potential disqualification). The arithmetic always favours stopping earlier. The cost of continuing is not just the money. It is the years.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Stop Trading Now: Your Immediate Next Steps</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If you recognise any of the warning signs described on this page, we advise one immediate step: call a licensed insolvency practitioner today. Not tomorrow. Not next week. Today.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The date you first sought professional advice is one of the key data points in the liquidator's conduct report. A director who called an IP on Monday and stopped trading on Friday is in a fundamentally different position from a director who waited three more months.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>We have seen that distinction make the difference between a clean conduct report and a disqualification proceeding.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Wrongful trading (section 214) is a civil claim, not a criminal offence. However, fraudulent trading (section 213), where the business is carried on with intent to defraud creditors, is both a civil claim and a criminal offence carrying up to 10 years' imprisonment.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The distinction is intent: wrongful trading is about negligence, fraudulent trading is about deliberate dishonesty. Most cases involve wrongful trading, not fraud.</p>

Speak to a licensed insolvency practitioner immediately, stop incurring new debts, and begin preparing for a formal insolvency process. The conduct report will note when you finally acted, and acting now limits your exposure.</p>
